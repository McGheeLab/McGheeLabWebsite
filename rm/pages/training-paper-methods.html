1. What the Methods does

The Methods section has exactly one job: let a stranger reproduce your experiment. Not "give context." Not "motivate the design." Not "justify the philosophical choice." Reproducibility.

The right standard is: a postdoc at a different lab, with access to the same equipment + reagents, should be able to run your experiment from your Methods section alone. If they would need to email you for clarification, your Methods is incomplete.

Methods writes itself if you kept good notes. If you can't remember what your camera exposure was or which passage your cells were at, you didn't capture enough in the lab notebook. Methods is the section where good notebook habits get cashed in.

2. The anatomy

The lab's standard Methods section has 7 elements. Cover all of them or have a defensible reason for skipping one.

  • Cells / model organisms / samples. Source, identifier, passage, mycoplasma status. Catalog or ATCC number.
  • Reagents. Antibodies (catalog + clone + lot if it matters), drugs (vendor + cat number), media + supplements.
  • Equipment. Make + model + objective + camera. For our scopes: Nikon AX-R confocal with 25×/1.1 water objective; Ti2-U epi-fluorescence with X-Cite XLED.
  • Acquisition parameters. Exposure, frame rate, pinhole, Z step, scan mode, averaging.
  • Analysis pipeline. Software + version (StarDist v0.8.5; Cellpose 2.x; Fiji 1.54f). Every named software gets a citation.
  • Statistics. Test used, n (with units — "n=12 spheroids" not just "n=12"), what error bars mean.
  • Data + code availability. Required by most journals now. Repository, accession number, GitHub link.

3. Catalog numbers + named versions

Every commercial reagent gets a catalog number. Every named software tool gets a version number and citation. If you used StarDist, cite Schmidt 2018. If you used Cellpose, cite Stringer 2021. If you used Fiji, cite Schindelin 2012.

Why this matters: people you've never met will try to reproduce your work years from now, with their own fluorophores and antibodies. Catalog numbers + versions are how you make that possible.

The Lot trap. Some reagents — particularly antibodies — behave differently across lots. If lot variability matters for your experiment, cite the lot. If not, cite catalog + clone alone.

4. Voice — Technician throughout

Methods is written in Technician voice (V3.182 § 4): terse, factual, recipe-like, no interpretation. Sentences are short. Verbs are concrete. No "interestingly," no "we chose this approach because," no "this method has the advantage of."

The acceptable verbs: were grown, were mounted, were imaged, was measured, was computed, were segmented, were tracked. Avoid: we believe, we feel, we found that, importantly.

5. When passive voice is right

The lab uses active voice as the default in every section except Methods, where passive is sometimes legitimately better. The criterion: is the agent (the "we") relevant?

  • "RNA was extracted using TRIzol." — Passive is fine. We extracted it; nobody else could have; the agent doesn't carry information.
  • "We developed a custom segmentation pipeline." — Active. The fact that this lab built it matters.
  • "Cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS." — Passive is fine.
  • "We selected the 25×/1.1 water objective because oil-into-aqueous mismatch would have produced spherical aberration deep into 200-μm spheroids." — Active, and we keep the rationale because it's a defensible Methods choice the reader needs to follow.

6. The "but everyone knows..." trap

The most common student mistake: skipping details that feel obvious. "We did Western blots" is not a Methods section — it's a sentence in someone's notebook.

What "everyone knows" but you still write:

  • How long you blocked, in what buffer.
  • What primary antibody dilution.
  • What secondary, what HRP/fluor, what detection.
  • How long you exposed, on what imager.
  • Whether you stripped + re-probed.

When in doubt, include it. A short Methods section will hurt you in review; an overly thorough one almost never does.

7. Methods vs Introduction — same content, wrong voice

The same factual content can be written in Methods (Technician) voice or Introduction (Storyteller) voice. Toggle to see how the same fact lands two ways.

Cells were imaged on a Nikon AX-R confocal microscope (25× / 1.1 NA water-immersion objective) with 488 nm excitation, 1 AU pinhole, 200 nm Z step, every 30 min for 24 h. Per-cell intensities were measured by StarDist segmentation (v0.8.5, default 2D_versatile_fluo model) with downstream tracking via the Spatial Fingerprint backend of Cell Tracker.

Technician voice. Recipe. Reproducible by a stranger.

To investigate the role of edge density in invasion, we chose to use the Nikon AX-R confocal because of its excellent optical sectioning capabilities, which are critical for resolving fine details of cell behavior deep within thick samples like spheroids. The 25×/1.1 water-immersion objective was particularly well-suited to this study because of its unique combination of high NA and long working distance.

Storyteller voice in the wrong place. "We chose to use," "particularly well-suited" — this is Introduction reasoning, not Methods. Move it.

8. Methods vs Supplementary Methods

When the Methods section gets long, the temptation is to push detail to Supplementary. Resist when possible. Reviewers and most readers will not click through to Supplementary; if a detail is required for reproducibility, it belongs in the main Methods.

What legitimately belongs in Supplementary Methods:

  • Long protocols you derived from a published one — main Methods cites the paper; Supplementary has your tweaks.
  • Detailed parameter sweeps + their rationale.
  • Algorithmic detail for custom code (with code in a public repo).
  • Reagent tables (when there are many — single antibody table, e.g.).

What does NOT belong in Supplementary:

  • Acquisition parameters for your headline experiment.
  • What cell line you used.
  • Statistical method choice.
  • Software versions for your primary analysis tool.
