Paper writing — the Results

Figure-anchored prose in Reporter voice. Report what you found; save the interpretation for the Discussion. One subsection per figure, one paragraph per panel.

1. What the Results does

Results reports what you found. Not what it means — that's Discussion. Not how you did it — that's Methods. Just: here are the data, anchored to the figures, with the numbers and statistics needed to evaluate them.

Done well, the Results section is the easiest one to write: you already have the figures, you already know what each one shows, so the prose is largely a narration of the figures. The discipline is in keeping yourself from editorializing.

Write Results second. After Methods, before Discussion or Introduction. By then your figures exist, the measurements are concrete, and you can walk through them one by one. Drafting Results before figures are final is how you produce paragraphs that get deleted.

2. Section structure

The standard shape:

  • One subsection per figure. If you have 5 main figures, you have 5 Results subsections.
  • Each subsection has a claim-shaped heading. Not "Edge density experiments" — that's a topic. "Edge density inverts the effect of stiffness on invasion" — that's a claim.
  • One paragraph per panel (roughly). A 4-panel figure typically warrants 3-4 paragraphs in its Results subsection.
  • Each paragraph references its panel. "(Fig. 2a)" appears somewhere in nearly every paragraph; if it doesn't, the paragraph might not belong.

3. The Results paragraph skeleton

The Results variant of the 3-line skeleton (V3.182 § 2):

observation — what you measured, in plain terms
quantification — the number + statistical test
conclusion-from-this-figure — what this panel alone supports

Note the third line: the conclusion is bounded to this figure alone. Broader conclusions that tie multiple figures together belong in the Discussion. If your "Results conclusion" sentence requires citing two other figures, you've slipped into Discussion-mode.

4. Voice — Reporter throughout

Results is written entirely in Reporter voice (V3.182 § 4): neutral, anchored to a figure panel, statistics inline, no editorializing.

Words that do NOT belong in Results:

  • Interestingly — every result is interesting; saying so adds nothing.
  • Importantly — let the reader judge importance.
  • Remarkably — same.
  • Strikingly — same.
  • Surprisingly — same; also, surprises belong in Discussion.
  • Suggests / argues for — these are Advocate verbs. Save them for Discussion.

5. Statistics inline

Every quantitative claim in Results carries its statistics inline. Reviewers will look for them; if they're not there, the reviewer assumes you didn't run them.

The minimum that goes after every number:

  • n with units — "n=12 spheroids," not just "n=12." The units matter: 12 spheroids ≠ 12 cells ≠ 12 fields of view.
  • Test name — "two-tailed t-test," "Mann-Whitney U," "one-way ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc."
  • p value — exact (p=0.024) is preferred to threshold (p<0.05) when possible.
  • Multiple-comparison correction — when there are multiple tests, say what you did (Bonferroni, FDR).

Error bars in figures need a sentence in the legend explaining what they are (SD, SEM, 95% CI) — but figure-legend text isn't your Results section's job.

6. Interpretation creep — the most common failure mode

The single most-flagged issue in first-draft Results sections: students mix Reporter and Advocate voice. The symptom is sentences that look like reporting but actually contain interpretation.

Hidden interpretation, by example:

  • "This important finding shows..." — "important" is interpretation.
  • "As expected, invasion increased..." — "as expected" foreshadows; foreshadowing is interpretation.
  • "Invasion increased, likely because..." — the "likely because" is a Discussion sentence.
  • "The mechanism appears to be..." — "appears to be" is an Advocate verb.
  • "To our surprise, the effect reversed..." — surprise belongs in Discussion.

The Reporter version of each: state the observation + quantification, period.

7. Same observation in two voices

Toggle below to see Reporter vs Advocate phrasings of the same finding. The Advocate version is well-written prose — it just belongs in the Discussion, not the Results.

Spheroid invasion increased 2.4-fold under stiff (4.5 kPa) vs soft (1.5 kPa) matrix conditions at 24 h in BT-549 spheroids (Fig. 2a; n=12 spheroids per condition; two-tailed t-test, p<0.001). At MDA-MB-231 spheroids, the effect was attenuated (1.4-fold increase, p=0.018) and at MCF-10A spheroids no significant difference was detected (p=0.41, Fig. 2b).

Neutral. Anchored to panels. Numbers + tests + n inline. No editorializing.

We found a striking 2.4-fold increase in invasion under stiff matrix conditions, which strongly supports the widely-held view that stiffer matrices promote invasion. Importantly, this effect was attenuated in MDA-MB-231 cells, likely because of their already-high baseline invasiveness. Remarkably, no effect was seen in MCF-10A cells — possibly reflecting their non- tumorigenic nature.

All five Advocate verbs in one paragraph. Every "likely," "possibly," and "importantly" should move to Discussion. The numbers, panels, and n are missing.

8. Negative results — write them without apologizing

A null result is still a result. Report it the same way as a positive one: observation + quantification + conclusion-from-this-figure. Do not apologize. Do not speculate why ("possibly because...") — speculation is Discussion.

Negative results that read well: "No significant difference in invasion was detected between stiff and soft conditions in MCF-10A spheroids (Fig. 2c; n=12, p=0.41, two-tailed t-test)." Done. Move on. The Discussion can take this further.
