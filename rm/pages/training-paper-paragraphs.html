1. Why most first drafts fail at the paragraph level

When students hand Alex a first draft and he sends it back covered in red, the problem is rarely the science. The science is usually fine. The problem is that the paragraphs aren't paragraphs — they're stacks of true sentences that don't add up to a unit of argument.

Good paragraphs have a shape. Each one makes a single move, supports it, and hands the reader off to the next paragraph. Once you can see the shape, you can write paragraphs that don't need to be rewritten — even on a first draft.

The fix is mechanical. It's not about talent or style. It's about writing a 3-line skeleton before you write the actual sentences. Sections 2 and 3 cover the skeleton; the rest of this training covers the voice and scope choices that come next.

2. The paragraph skeleton

Every scientific paragraph has 3 jobs to do. Write them out as a 3-line skeleton before you write the paragraph itself. You can always fold them into 2 or expand to 5 sentences in the final prose — but the skeleton stays 3 lines.

The default skeleton

claim — what this paragraph asserts
evidence — what supports the claim
link — how this connects forward

Section-specific variants

  • Results paragraph: observation → quantification → conclusion-from-this-figure
  • Introduction paragraph: context → gap → what's-next
  • Discussion paragraph: interpretation → evidence + literature → caveat / implication
  • Methods paragraph: what → how → (occasionally: why this and not the alternative)

The variants aren't competing formats — they're the same 3-line shape with section-appropriate verb choices. Once you've seen four of them, you can write the fifth.

4. The four voices of scientific writing

Every section of a scientific paper is written in a specific voice. Picking the right one is the single biggest move you can make at the paragraph level. The four voices, with the same factual content rendered four different ways:

The Reporter
Used in: Results
Invasion increased 2.4-fold under stiff (4.5 kPa) versus soft (1.5 kPa) matrix conditions at 24 h (Fig. 2a; n=12 spheroids per condition; p<0.001, two-tailed t-test).
Neutral. Anchored to a figure. Reports observations + statistics. Never says "interestingly," "surprisingly," "remarkably." No interpretation.
The Storyteller
Used in: Introduction, Abstract, Cover letter
For two decades, the role of substrate stiffness in tumor invasion has been contested — some reports show stiffer matrices promote invasion, others show the opposite. We reasoned that the discrepancy might stem from an unappreciated dependence on the density of cells at the spheroid edge.
Narrative. Sets stakes. Identifies a gap or contradiction. Connects to a broader audience. Earns the right to take the reader deep.
The Advocate
Used in: Discussion
Our results suggest that edge density is the missing variable in stiffness-driven invasion. While we cannot rule out contributions from intracellular tension changes, the dose-dependent inversion at the density threshold argues for a primarily mechanical mechanism.
Claim-making, with humility. "Suggests," "argues for," "consistent with," "we cannot rule out." Bold but bounded.
The Technician
Used in: Methods
Patient-derived breast tumor spheroids (BT-549, passage 8–12) were embedded in collagen-I matrices (1.5 or 4.5 kPa, calibrated by rheometry) and imaged on a Nikon AX-R confocal at 25×/1.1 NA, 488 nm excitation, 1 AU pinhole, 200 nm Z step, every 30 min for 24 h.
Recipe. Precise. Reproducible. No interpretation, no stakes-setting. Cites the equipment + reagents + parameters.

5. Which voice for which section

The default voice per section, and what happens when you mix them up.

  • Abstract — Storyteller blended with Reporter. Storyteller for the opening sentence and the closing impact; Reporter for the result.
  • Introduction — Storyteller throughout. Earns reader investment.
  • Methods — Technician. Don't tell stories here. Don't justify philosophical choices here.
  • Results — Reporter. Don't interpret. Don't editorialize. Save it.
  • Discussion — Advocate. Now you interpret. Now you make claims. Now you situate against the literature.
  • Cover letter — Storyteller, with extra urgency.
Failure mode: voice creep. Storyteller voice in a Results section reads as overreach ("Strikingly, we found..."). Reporter voice in a Discussion reads as cowardice ("The data show X. The data also show Y."). Technician voice in an Introduction reads as a methods section in the wrong place. Reviewers spot all three immediately.

6. Active vs passive, "we" vs "I" vs "the data"

Active where possible

"We measured X" beats "X was measured." Active voice is shorter, easier to read, and assigns agency. The lab uses active voice as the default in every section except Methods, where passive is sometimes legitimately better.

"We" vs "I"

In a paper, always "we" — even on a single-author paper, "we" is the convention. "I" reads as informal and confuses readers used to "we." Save "I" for theses, personal statements, and acknowledgments.

"The data show" vs "the data argue"

"The data show X" is fine. "The data demonstrate X" is fine. "The data argue X" is anthropomorphic and weak — data don't argue, people do. "The data are consistent with X" is the Advocate-voice phrasing for Discussion sections where you want to make a claim without overclaiming.

When passive is right

Methods: "RNA was extracted using TRIzol" is fine — the agent (us, presumably) doesn't matter. "We extracted RNA using TRIzol" adds nothing. Use passive when the agent is irrelevant and the construction is more concise.

7. Narrative scope — broad enough to care, narrow enough to focus

The same project can be described at ten different scopes, from "cancer kills people" to "edge density above 0.12 cells/μm² inverts the effect of 1.5 vs 4.5 kPa stiffness on a 24-h invasion assay in BT-549 spheroids." Each scope is factually true. The skill is choosing the right scope for the audience, the section, and the sentence.

The shape of scope across a paper

  • Title: level 4–6. Specific enough to be discoverable, broad enough to feel novel.
  • Abstract opening: level 2–3. Hook the broadest audience.
  • Abstract closing: level 6–8. Make your specific result land.
  • Introduction: opens at level 2–3, funnels down to 7–8 by the third paragraph.
  • Methods + Results: stays at level 7–9. This is where you've earned the right to be specific.
  • Discussion opening: level 7–8 — re-state your result.
  • Discussion closing: back up to level 4–5 to project the impact.

8. Zooming out without losing focus

The hardest move in scientific writing: broaden the scope (so a general audience cares) without letting go of your specific claim (so you don't drift into a review article). Three reliable techniques:

The callback

Re-mention something from earlier in the paper — usually a motivating fact from the Introduction. The reader has been reading detailed Results for pages; reminding them of why the project mattered re-engages without leaving your claim. "Recall the 20-year contradiction we opened with."

The implication ladder

Climb from your specific finding outward, one rung at a time. Each rung is a slightly broader claim that follows from the rung below. The reader feels the broadening happen; you don't actually leave your data.

edge density inverts stiffness's effect on invasion (your finding)
↑ tumor mechanics include an unappreciated density variable
↑ matrix experiments need to control for cell-edge density
↑ field-wide reproducibility issues may have a common mechanical cause

The comparison anchor

Compare your result to a well-known finding the reader already cares about. "Just as VEGF blockade was first dismissed before being recognized as central, edge density may belong in standard mechanobiology toolkits." The comparison broadens scope; the specificity of your claim never moves.

9. Common voice mistakes

  • Storyteller in Results. "Strikingly, we found..." Save the editorializing for Discussion. In Results, just report.
  • Reporter in Discussion. Pure observation in the Discussion reads as cowardice. The Discussion is where you stake claims; if you can't say what your results mean, you don't have a Discussion.
  • Technician in Introduction. Long lists of methods + parameters in the Introduction belong in the Methods. Yes, even if you think it's "background." If it's a procedure, it's Methods.
  • Voice swap mid-paragraph. Don't start in active voice and finish in passive. Pick a voice per paragraph and stay there.
  • Anthropomorphism. "The cells decided to invade." Cells don't decide. They invade or don't. Same with data, genes, signals, pathways.
  • Hedging too much. "These results may possibly suggest a potential mechanism for..." Pick a verb and commit.
  • Hedging too little. "This proves that..." No paper proves anything. "Demonstrates," "shows," "is consistent with" are your verbs.
