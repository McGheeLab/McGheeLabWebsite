Scientific Communication

Telling a story in scientific framing — posters and presentations. The core idea: complexity and scope are inversely correlated with audience attention. Good talks oscillate; bad talks drown.

1. Why storytelling

You can be the most rigorous person in the room and still lose the room. Science doesn't propagate by being correct — it propagates by being heard, understood, and remembered. That is communication. And human brains, including the ones in your audience, have a stubborn architecture: they listen to stories and tune out from information dumps.

This isn't a flaw to apologize for. It's the medium you're working in. Every good scientific talk and every good poster uses the audience's attention as a constrained resource — spending it deliberately, replenishing it on purpose, and never assuming you have more of it than you do.

One principle for this whole training. Your audience will pay attention to whatever feels broad, human, and surprising — and check out when it feels narrow, technical, and grinding. Your job is to design a journey that uses both states on purpose.

2. The attention arc

The central concept of this module. Imagine your talk's timeline on the x-axis and complexity on the y-axis. Complexity is how narrow, technical, and jargon-dense your content is at any given moment. As complexity rises, audience attention falls. As complexity falls — when you pull back broad, show a striking image, ask a question, reconnect to the stakes — attention recovers.

The lesson

  • Monotonic ramps kill talks. If complexity only goes up, attention only goes down. By beat 4 you're talking to yourself.
  • Permanent shallowness is also a failure. If you never go deep, the audience never gets the actual science — they remember "that was nice" and nothing more.
  • Oscillate deliberately. Two to three deep dives in a 10-min talk; each one preceded by a broadening move and followed by a recovery. Plot your beats before you write a slide.
  • Long talks need more cycles, not deeper ones. A 60-min seminar isn't a 10-min talk with longer technical sections — it's 5–7 mini-talks, each with its own arc.

3. The opening hook — the first 60 seconds

The opening is when you negotiate with the audience whether they're going to invest. Get this wrong and the rest of the talk is pulling rope. Get it right and you've earned the right to take them deep.

What the opening must accomplish:

  1. Connect — make this feel like a human problem, not a niche technical one.
  2. Motivate — answer "why should I care?" before the audience asks it.
  3. Spark curiosity — leave a clear question hanging that the rest of the talk will answer.

Tactics that work

  • Surprising statistic. "One in eight women will develop breast cancer. Two-thirds of those who recur die within five years. We don't know why some recur and some don't." Three sentences. The audience is in.
  • Patient or clinical story. A specific person's situation. Always more powerful than aggregate numbers, used sparingly.
  • Striking image. A confocal slice that's beautiful before it's informative. A scanning electron micrograph. A drawn diagram with one piece deliberately missing.
  • Bold question. "Why do tumors that look the same under the microscope behave so differently in the clinic?" If the audience would also like to know, you've already won.
  • Counter-intuitive claim. "Stiffer matrices should promote invasion — every textbook says so. We don't think they always do." Now the audience has to listen to find out why.
  • Personal motivation. Sparingly. "I started working on this because my grandmother..." can land. It can also flop. Read the room.
What to never open with. "Today I'd like to talk about..." / "First a bit of background on my project..." / a slide of seven bullet points labelled "Outline." None of these connect. None of them motivate. None of them spark curiosity. The audience uses them as license to check email.

4. The funnel into complexity

You've hooked them broad. Now you need to take them somewhere specific enough that the science actually lands. This is the funnel. Done well, the audience comes with you. Done badly, they're left on the surface while you talk to yourself in the deep end.

Bring them with you

  • Build one concept at a time. A single new idea per slide is the cap. Two and you've already lost the back of the room.
  • Define every jargon term the first time you use it. Including ones you assume everyone knows. They don't.
  • Use analogies — carefully. "Like a thermostat" / "like a key in a lock." Good for first orientation. Wrong if pushed too hard. Drop the analogy before it starts misleading.
  • Show, don't define. A short animation, a clean schematic, or a single illustrative dataset beats two paragraphs of explanation.
  • Pause + check in. Especially in seminars. "Does this part make sense before I go further?" Cheap; high payoff. The room either nods or stares — both are signal.
The audience-arrogance trap. "But everyone in this field knows what NF-κB does." Maybe. But your audience isn't your field — even at a conference. Define it. The specialists will tolerate ten seconds of recap; the non-specialists will be lost without it.

5. Pulling back broad

The recovery move. Most students never learn it; it's the single biggest gap between a competent talk and a great one. You've been deep. You feel the room slipping. You need to climb back out — broaden, reconnect, refresh stakes — before you take them deep again on the next point.

How to pull back

  • "OK let me zoom out for a second." Spoken transition. Cheap. The audience instantly knows you're moving from detail back to context. Buys you 10 seconds to recover.
  • A striking visual. A photograph, a histology image, an animation. Something the audience can rest on while you reframe.
  • Ask a question. "So what does this actually mean for a patient?" — answers itself, broadens scope, re-engages.
  • Tell a quick anecdote. Twenty seconds. About the lab. About a moment in the project. About how an experiment failed and what you tried next.
  • Connect back to the opening hook. Best move when available. "Remember those statistics about recurrence? Here's where what we just measured starts to matter."
  • Refresh the stakes. Why this matters beyond the technical detail you just delivered.
The rhythm to feel. Broad → narrow → broad → narrow → broad. Two to four cycles in a 10-minute talk. Each broad moment lasts 20–40 seconds; each narrow descent 60–120. The exact numbers vary; the rhythm doesn't.

Every story has characters. Your science has characters too, even if you've never named them. Picking your protagonist deliberately is one of the highest-leverage moves in a scientific talk — same data, different protagonist, totally different talk.

7. Classic story arcs

Audiences are pattern-matchers. Your story will land harder if it fits a shape the audience already knows. Pick the arc that matches the actual structure of your work — don't force it, but recognize when one fits.

Hero's journey
status quo → call → trials → breakthrough → return
"For years we assumed X. Then we noticed Y. We tried A, B, C — all failed. Eventually we tried D, and it worked. Now we know..."
Mystery
unknown → clues → suspect → resolution
"We had a clinical observation no one could explain. Here are the clues we collected, and here's the mechanism we eventually uncovered."
Tragedy + redemption
attempt → failure → reframe → success
"Our first model was wrong. The data forced us to abandon it. The reframing gave us the right model — and a better one than we'd hoped for."
Discovery
expectation → anomaly → investigation → new fact
"We expected the spheroids to grow uniformly. They didn't. Following the anomaly led us to a mechanism we hadn't predicted."
Quest
goal → obstacles → prize
"We needed to image actin dynamics inside a 200-μm spheroid. Three years of obstacles. Here's how we finally got there — and here's what we saw."
Comparison / conflict
two hypotheses → decisive data → winner
"Two competing models. The decisive experiment had to discriminate them. Here's what it showed, and what that means for the field."
The grit arc. The tragedy-and-redemption / quest arcs both pay off the lab's "grit pillar" from the PhD triangle. When the failures are real and the eventual success was earned, say so. Audiences trust scientists who admit how hard the work actually was.

8. Other hooks — keeping attention beyond the opening

The opening hook gets them in the room. Mid-talk hooks keep them there. Every "pull back broad" move is a chance to deploy one of these.

  • Surprise. A counter-intuitive result. The data not matching the prediction. Lead with what didn't fit the textbook.
  • Humor. Sparingly. Self-aware, not at the audience's expense, never about other scientists. One well-timed line is worth ten attempted ones.
  • Stakes. Real-world consequences. What does this actually mean for a patient, a clinical decision, the next paper, the next experiment?
  • Beauty. A stunning image. A clean dataset. Elegant math. Audiences will pay attention to beauty even when they wouldn't pay attention to the science underneath it.
  • Controversy. "Everyone thinks X. We don't." Stake out a position and defend it with the data.
  • Personal connection. A moment from the project. Why you specifically work on this. Earns trust if it's brief and sincere.
  • The unsolved. Point at what's still unknown. The audience leaves the room wanting more, instead of feeling the talk closed the topic.

9. Adapting to the format

The same story has to be re-cut for the channel it's running on.

60-second elevator pitch

One hook + one claim. No cycles. The hook IS the talk. Memorize the exact words; you'll deliver this dozens of times.

10-minute conference talk

Two or three cycles. Tight. No introductory throat-clearing. Open with the hook, deliver each cycle with a clear figure, close with a memorable takeaway and one slide of acknowledgments. Practice it standing up at least three times.

45-minute seminar

Five to seven cycles — basically a series of mini-talks, each with its own arc. The transitions between cycles are where you "pull back broad" — by far the highest-risk parts of the talk. Use them to refresh stakes and re-engage.

Poster

Different beast. The audience moves at their own pace and reads in non-linear order. Visual hierarchy and eye-flow matter more than narration order. Each subsection of the poster is its own mini-arc: hook-in-the-title, point-in-the-figure, claim-in-the-conclusion. The whole poster's job is to attract → reward 30-second visitors → reward 5-minute visitors. Three audiences, one design.

Posters are not papers printed on cardboard. The most common poster mistake is reproducing the manuscript's dense paragraphs at A0 size. Cut text by ~70%. Lead with figures. Title is a claim, not a topic. Use 24 point as your minimum.
