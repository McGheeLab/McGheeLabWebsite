1. Why peer review exists

Peer review is the social contract that gives a paper its scientific weight. Other people in your field — usually two or three of them, working anonymously and unpaid — go through your manuscript and decide whether the claims are supported by the data, whether the methods are sound, whether the literature is engaged with honestly, and whether the result is worth a journal's pages.

Two reframings worth internalizing before your first submission:

  • Peer review is not adversarial. Even when comments sting, the reviewer is donating their time to make the paper better. That's worth gratitude, not resentment.
  • Peer review makes papers better. Almost every paper that's been improved by review reads more clearly afterward — and almost every paper rejected at one journal lands stronger at the next. The friction is the point.
One sentence to keep on a sticky note. The reviewer is a colleague trying to help. The reviewer is not your enemy. Even when they're wrong, treat them as a colleague.

2. The reviewer's six jobs

A reviewer is asked to evaluate six dimensions of a manuscript. Knowing what they're evaluating helps you anticipate what they'll comment on.

  1. Rigor. Do the methods actually support the claims? Are the controls right? Are the statistics correct? Did the design discriminate between hypotheses?
  2. Novelty. Is this new? Or has it been shown before — in this field or in an adjacent one? Reviewers are gatekeepers against duplication.
  3. Completeness. Are there obvious experiments missing? Is one critical control absent? Would another lab member do an additional check before submitting?
  4. Writing quality. Is the paper readable? Are the figures clear? Does the structure work? Reviewers often flag writing issues even when they shouldn't strictly affect acceptance.
  5. Unstated limitations. What's wrong with this paper that the authors haven't acknowledged? This is the reviewer's most valuable contribution — they see what you can't.
  6. Suggested additions. What experiments or analyses would strengthen the claims? Sometimes these are scope-creep ("you should also test Y"); sometimes they're critical ("without Y you cannot support claim X").

The reviewer's final recommendation — accept / minor revisions / major revisions / reject — synthesizes all six. The editor uses the recommendation but isn't bound by it.

3. What reviewers actually look at when they sit down to read

A reviewer typically gives a manuscript 2–4 hours of attention spread over a few sessions. That's not very much time, and they're prioritizing.

The order they read in (most reviewers):

  1. Title + Abstract. Decides whether this is something they should read carefully or skim.
  2. Figures + captions. Many reviewers read figures before any prose. If the figures don't tell the story, the reviewer is already skeptical when they get to the text.
  3. Introduction last paragraph. Where's the claim?
  4. Results section. Skimming for the headline numbers, the n, the statistics.
  5. Discussion. Checking whether the authors over-claim or admit the right limitations.
  6. Methods. Cross-referenced when something in Results looks off.
  7. Introduction body. Read last, often skimmed.

Implications for how you write: your figures and your last Intro paragraph carry disproportionate weight. If those are weak, even a methodologically strong paper will get a tough review.

4. The six reviewer-comment types

Every reviewer comment falls into one of roughly six categories. Knowing the category helps you pick the right response shape — agree / partial / disagree — and the right kind of evidence to bring.

  • Methodological. "Did you control for X?" / "Why didn't you use technique Y?" — concerns about how the experiment was designed.
  • Statistical. "How was n chosen?" / "Why a t-test rather than ANOVA?" — concerns about analysis choices.
  • Literature. "You did not cite Smith 2019, which reported the same effect" — concerns about how you've engaged the prior work.
  • Interpretation. "Your data don't support the claim that..." / "The mechanism could equally be Z" — concerns about the Discussion's interpretive claims.
  • Stylistic. "Figure 3 is unclear" / "This paragraph is confusing" / "The Introduction is too long" — concerns about presentation.
  • Scope-creep. "You should also test in vivo" / "What about a different cell line?" — requests for additional experiments that may or may not be in-scope.

5. The author's job — what you owe the reviewer

When the editor returns reviews, you owe the reviewers four things in your response:

  1. A response to every comment. Not 14 of 15. Every one. Even the typos. Missing a comment reads as ignoring the reviewer.
  2. A clear indication of what changed. With manuscript page + line references (or section names if line numbers shift). "We have added Figure 4 (p. 8, lines 23-34)."
  3. A respectful tone, especially when disagreeing. The editor reads the response letter as carefully as the reviewers do; your tone shapes whether the editor sides with you on contested points.
  4. Engagement with substance, not surface compliance. "We have addressed this concern" without saying how is worse than not responding at all. Show your work.
The format that universally works: a single document, point-by-point. For each reviewer in turn, list every comment they made (quoted in italics or in a callout), then your response below it. No exceptions.

6. The three response shapes

Every response to a reviewer comment is one of three shapes: agree, partial, or disagree. Each has a template.

Agreement

When the reviewer's comment is correct and you've fixed the manuscript to address it.

Template: "We thank the reviewer for this (suggestion / observation / correction). We have now (specific change), which appears in the revised manuscript at (p. X, lines Y–Z). The (figure / table / paragraph) now reads: (short quoted excerpt of the new text)."

Partial agreement

When the reviewer has a valid point but the full suggestion is out of scope.

Template: "We agree that (narrower restatement of the underlying concern) is important to address. We have added (what you did do). We respectfully note that (what you did NOT do) is beyond the scope of this work because (specific reason); we acknowledge this limitation explicitly in the revised Discussion (p. X, lines Y)."

Disagreement

When you genuinely think the reviewer is wrong, and you have data or literature to defend the position.

Template: "We respectfully disagree with this interpretation. Our data show (specific evidence, with figure reference), which is consistent with (citation if applicable). We have clarified our reasoning in the revised text (p. X, lines Y–Z), which now reads: (quoted text)."

7. How to disagree with a reviewer

The highest-stakes skill in the response letter. Sometimes the reviewer is wrong — they misread the figure, they missed your control experiment, or they're pushing a methodological preference that isn't standard. You should disagree. But how you disagree is the difference between the editor siding with you and the editor sending the paper for another round.

The five rules of disagreement

  1. State the disagreement specifically. Don't hedge ("we feel that perhaps..."). Be direct: "We respectfully disagree with the reviewer's interpretation of Figure 3."
  2. Give a reason backed by data or literature. "Figure 3c shows X" or "Smith 2019 reports similar findings under the same conditions." Don't disagree on principle alone.
  3. Acknowledge the reviewer's underlying concern. Even if you reject their suggestion, name the worry they're voicing. "We understand the reviewer's concern about Y, but believe our control experiment in Fig. 3c addresses it because..."
  4. Never make it personal. Never write "the reviewer has misunderstood." Write "we may have presented this poorly; we have clarified the text." Save face for the reviewer; they're more likely to back down if they don't have to admit being wrong.
  5. Make changes where possible. Even when you disagree, change something — clarify the wording, add a sentence to the Methods, expand a figure legend. A disagreement paired with zero changes reads as defiance.
The "we may have presented this poorly" move. When a reviewer misreads your figure or methods, the correct response is to assume the misread is your fault for not being clear, not theirs for misreading. Then you clarify the text. Even when you both know the reviewer made a mistake, this framing keeps the relationship functional.

8. The editor as your ally

The editor is the third party in every response-letter transaction. They synthesize the reviewer comments, make the final decision, and have authority to override reviewer requests they consider unreasonable.

What the editor can do for you

  • Override scope-creep. If a reviewer demands an experiment that would take 6 months and isn't required for the claim, the editor can side with you against it.
  • Mediate disagreements. When you and a reviewer fundamentally disagree, a calmly-written response letter helps the editor decide in your favor.
  • Recognize unreasonable reviews. Editors have seen hundreds of reviews; they spot the bitter / hostile / off-topic ones and weigh them accordingly.

How to invite the editor's help

  • Include a brief cover letter. Half a page max, addressed to the editor by name. Summarize what you've changed and flag any reviewer requests you've pushed back on.
  • Be specific about scope. "Reviewer 2 requests an in vivo validation, which we agree would strengthen the paper but is a multi-year project beyond the scope of this manuscript. We have added X as a partial response and discussed the limitation in the Discussion."
  • Acknowledge legitimate criticism even when defending against it. Editors trust authors who admit what's hard.

What never works

  • Asking the editor to overrule a reviewer because the reviewer is "wrong." Editors hate this. Make the technical case and let them weigh it.
  • Personal attacks on reviewers, no matter how badly the review reads. The editor is reading.
  • Ignoring a reviewer's concern and hoping the editor doesn't notice. They notice.

9. Common author failure modes

  • Ignoring comments. Skipping a comment because you didn't know how to respond. Always respond — even with "we acknowledge this limitation but believe it is beyond scope," at minimum.
  • Defensiveness. "The reviewer is mistaken; we clearly stated..." reads as combative. Reframe: "We may have been unclear; we have revised."
  • Capitulating to bad suggestions. If a reviewer demands an experiment that would weaken the paper or is genuinely off-scope, push back. Surrendering to all requests is not a virtue.
  • Scope creep. Running every suggested experiment out of fear. A revised paper with 14 new experiments often reads worse than the original.
  • Missing the deadline. Most revision deadlines are 60–90 days. Plan from day 1; don't start at day 50.
  • Hand-waving "addressed." "We have addressed this concern" with no specifics is worse than not responding. Always cite manuscript pages.
  • Anger leaking through. Frustration in a response letter survives every revision pass. If you're angry, write the draft, then sit on it 24 hours, then re-write before sending.
  • Treating each reviewer in isolation. If both reviewers raised the same concern, address it once and reference the second response. Don't duplicate effort.
