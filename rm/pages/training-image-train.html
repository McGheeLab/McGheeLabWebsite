Image Train & Objectives

The optical components from sample to camera, the markings on an objective, when aberrations bite, and how to pick the right lens for a job.

1. The journey of a photon

Follow a photon emitted by a GFP molecule inside a tumor spheroid on its way to a camera pixel. Every component between the sample and the sensor either bends, filters, focuses, or absorbs that photon — and each is a potential source of aberration if it's the wrong piece of glass.

The components in order

  1. Sample + mounting medium — water, glycerol, or fluoromount; their refractive index is the first thing the photon experiences.
  2. Coverslip — #1.5 thickness 170 μm is the worldwide standard. Objectives are designed for this.
  3. Immersion medium — air, water, glycerol, or oil between coverslip and objective front lens.
  4. Objective — does the heavy lifting (NA, magnification, aberration correction).
  5. Tube lens — forms the intermediate image (200 mm focal length on Nikon).
  6. Intermediate optics — filter cubes, beam splitters, scan optics (confocal only).
  7. Port — camera or eyepiece. You can't have both at once.
The first failure mode every student hits. Using a #1 coverslip (~150 μm) under an oil 60× / 1.4 objective designed for #1.5 (170 μm) introduces severe spherical aberration — features blur axially, the PSF skews to one side. Fix: use the right coverslip. Correction collars (Tab 3) help only marginally.

2. Reading the markings on an objective

The text engraved on the front of an objective contains essentially the whole specification. Once you can decode it, you can predict what it'll do without consulting the data sheet.

The canonical example

Plan Apo λ 60× / 1.40 Oil ∞ / 0.17 WD 0.13

What each token means

  • Plan — field flatness; the image is in focus from center to edge. (Not Plan = curved field, edges blur first.)
  • Apo / Fluor / Achromat — chromatic correction tier. Achromat ≈ 2 colors; Fluor ≈ 3 colors; Apo ≈ 4+ colors. For multicolor fluorescence you want Apo.
  • λ — extended UV / IR transmission. Necessary for DAPI (UV excitation) and far-red dyes.
  • 60× — nominal magnification at the manufacturer's reference tube-lens focal length.
  • 1.40 — numerical aperture (NA).
  • Oil — immersion medium.
  • ∞ — infinity-corrected. Needs a tube lens to form the image (modern standard).
  • 0.17 — designed coverslip thickness in mm (= #1.5).
  • WD 0.13 — working distance in mm (= 130 μm).

Try this

  1. Paste any objective marking (or use one of the preset buttons) into the field below.
  2. The decoder splits the string into tokens and color-codes each.
  3. The stats panel reports each spec value with a one-line explanation.
  4. Try a #1 coverslip objective ("0.13") vs a #1.5 ("0.17") — same lens otherwise. The decoder flags the mismatch on a #1.5-only lens.

3. Immersion, coverslips & correction collars

Light bends every time it crosses a refractive-index boundary. Manufacturers pre-compute the aberration for a specific design set (typically 170 μm of #1.5 coverslip + a specified immersion medium) and grind the lens elements to cancel it. The moment you stray from those conditions — different coverslip, mismatched immersion, deep inside an aqueous sample with an oil lens — that cancellation breaks and the image gets spherically aberrated.

The classic mismatches

  • #1 coverslip with a #1.5 oil objective — under-correction; axial blur skewed deeper-than-expected.
  • Oil objective into an aqueous sample > 20 μm deep — mismatch grows linearly with depth; deep into a spheroid is unusable.
  • Water objective with too much glycerol mounting medium — water lens designed for n=1.33; glycerol is n=1.47.

Correction collars

High-NA water and air objectives often have a rotating ring on the barrel — a correction collar — that adjusts internal spacing to compensate for coverslip-thickness deviation. To use it: focus on the deepest plane of your sample, rotate the collar slightly, refocus, look at the PSF. Iterate until the PSF is symmetric in Z.

The correction-collar trap. Students will spend twenty minutes twiddling a collar on an objective that doesn't have one. The 25×/1.1 water on the AX-R does. The 60×/1.4 oil does not (oil-into-coverslip-into-aqueous-sample mismatch can\'t be corrected by a collar — only by switching to a water lens).

  1. The simulation below shows the PSF (point-spread function) of a single bead, cross-section in XZ.

4. Field of view & pixel sampling

Magnification at the camera is set by M = M_obj × (f_tube / f_design) — a 60× objective designed for a 200 mm tube lens (Nikon) becomes 60× on a Nikon scope but ~54× on an Olympus (180 mm) and ~49× on a Zeiss (165 mm). Pixel size at the sample then determines whether you meet Nyquist.

Pixel size at the sample

px_sample = px_sensor / M_system

For Nyquist sampling of a fluorescent point object you want px_sample ≤ λ_em / (4·NA). If you're scoping for deconvolution or strict quantitative work, tighten to λ_em / (4.6·NA).

FOV at the camera

FOV at the sample is sensor_diagonal / M_system. Bigger sensors give bigger FOVs but only up to the objective's field number — beyond that, the corners go dark or aberrated.

  3. Pick an emission wavelength. The Nyquist target updates.

5. Capstone — pick an objective

Scenario: A new project needs to image actin filaments (Phalloidin-AF488, λ_em ≈ 510 nm) inside fixed tumor spheroids. Targets are ~100 nm wide. You need to reach about 50 μm into a 100 μm spheroid mounted in aqueous medium. State your priorities below and the page will narrow the lab's candidate catalog to a short list, scored against your needs.

Build the spec

  1. Pick your minimum required NA — sets resolution.
  2. Pick your minimum required working distance — sets reachable depth.
  3. Pick the immersion medium you're willing to use (oil is highest NA but creates aberration deep into aqueous samples).
  4. Pick budget tier — Apo costs ~3× Fluor.
