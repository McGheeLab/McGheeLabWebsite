1. Why we write papers

A scientific paper is not a souvenir of work you've already done. It's the unit of currency by which scientific claims travel from the people who established them to the people who will build on them. If your work doesn't get into a paper, in practical terms it didn't happen — no one outside the lab can use it, cite it, extend it, or even know it existed.

Two things follow from that. First, writing should start early — long before the work is "done." Drafting reveals which questions your data actually answers and which it doesn't. Second, every choice in the paper (structure, citation, figure design, journal) is a choice about how the claim will be received. Your job as a writer is to make that reception easy.

One-sentence test. If you can't say what your paper claims in a single sentence — "we show that X happens when Y, contrary to the previous belief that Z" — then you don't yet have a paper, you have notes. Write the sentence first; everything else hangs off it.

2. Figure-first drafting

Before you write a paragraph, draft your figures. Most papers tell their story across 4–6 figures, each making one discrete point. If you can sketch those 4–6 figures from your existing data — even badly, on paper — you have a paper. If you can't, you don't have one yet, and writing prose is premature.

The pencil-sketch test

  • Number each figure 1–6.
  • Write a one-line caption for each, in the form of a claim ("Spheroid invasion is reduced 40% in TGF-β-treated samples").
  • Decide what panels A, B, C of that figure show, and which dataset each comes from.
  • Walk a labmate through the sketches verbally. If they stop you with questions, the story has gaps.

Only after the sketch survives a peer reads cleanly should you open the paper builder and start prose.

The cost of skipping this. Drafting prose before you've sorted out the figures means you'll write paragraphs you later delete because the figure you needed doesn't exist. We've all done it. Don't do it on this paper.

3. IMRaD anatomy

IMRaD — Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion — is the backbone almost every empirical biology paper hangs on. Each section has a specific job. Mixing them up is the most common structural failure mode in first drafts.

Introduction — the funnel

Opens wide, narrows to your claim. Three paragraphs is the canonical length. (1) The broad biological context — why this question matters. (2) The specific gap — what's known, what's not, what conflict exists in the literature. (3) Your claim and your approach — in one sentence, what this paper shows.

(Last paragraph of an Introduction.) The role of substrate stiffness in tumor invasion remains contested: some reports show stiffer matrices promote invasion, others show the opposite. We hypothesized that the discrepancy stems from an unappreciated dependence on cell-density at the spheroid edge. Here we show that, in patient-derived breast tumor spheroids, edge cell density inverts the effect of stiffness on invasion — at low edge density, stiffer matrices suppress invasion; at high edge density, they promote it.

Why it works: names the contested question, identifies the missing variable, states the claim in one sentence, hints at the experimental system. Reader knows the entire paper in 70 words.

(Last paragraph of an Introduction.) In this work, we used patient-derived breast tumor spheroids and varied matrix stiffness to study invasion. We performed live-cell imaging on a confocal microscope and analyzed the resulting data using a variety of computational methods to quantify invasion as a function of multiple parameters. Our results show interesting effects that may have implications for our understanding of tumor biology.

Why it doesn't: describes what you did, not what you found. "Interesting effects" tells the reader nothing. The reader has to read the rest of the paper to find out what the paper claims — and many won't.

Methods — the recipe

Methods is for reproducibility, not for storytelling. Someone should be able to redo the experiment from this section. Reagents (catalog numbers), equipment (make + model), parameters (exposure, frame rate, NA), and analysis pipelines (named software versions) all go here. Don't put novel biological context here — that's Introduction or Discussion.

  • Sentence patterns: "Cells were maintained in..." / "Images were acquired on a Nikon AX-R confocal..." / "Segmentation was performed using StarDist (v0.8.5)..."
  • Catalog numbers for every commercial reagent.
  • Cite tool papers — Fiji, Cellpose, StarDist all have papers that should be cited every time they're used.

Results — figure-anchored prose

Each Results subsection is anchored to one figure. The text walks the reader through what's in the figure and what conclusion it supports. Don't interpret yet — that's Discussion. Don't describe the method beyond what's needed to orient the reader — that's Methods.

Each paragraph in Results follows the same rough shape: (setup what was measured) → (what the data show) → (the conclusion drawn from this figure alone). Then the next paragraph picks up the next figure.

Discussion — interpret, situate, limit, project

Four jobs for the Discussion: (1) interpret what your data mean biologically, (2) situate the result against the existing literature, (3) honestly note the limitations, (4) project to what the next experiment should be. Don't re-summarize Results — the reader just read them.

Reviewers reliably attack the Discussion. They want to see you grapple with conflicting prior work and name your own limitations before they do. A Discussion that doesn't do either reads as a sales pitch and reviewers know it.

4. Citation discipline

Citations are how scientific claims are anchored. Under-cite and you sound either ignorant or arrogant; over-cite and you dilute the few citations that actually matter. The rule of thumb is to cite every non-obvious claim, and cite the source closest to where the claim is actually established (not a review that re-cites it).

When to cite

  • A factual claim that isn't textbook common knowledge.
  • A method, reagent, or tool you used — credit the originator.
  • A prior result you're building on, agreeing with, or contradicting.
  • The first introduction of a non-obvious concept or term.

When not to cite

  • Common knowledge ("DNA encodes proteins").
  • Methods you described in earlier sentences with the same citation.
  • Where citing a review would be lazy — find the original paper instead.

Try the quick exercise below to calibrate.

"DNA is composed of four nucleotide bases (A, C, G, T)."
Textbook common knowledge — no citation needed.
"TGF-β signaling has been shown to drive epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in mammary tumors."
Specific mechanistic claim with a literature trail — cite the primary papers establishing it.
"We segmented nuclei using StarDist."
Always cite the tool paper for software you used (Schmidt et al., 2018). Other people deserve credit; readers need a pointer.
"Substrate stiffness modulates invasion in 3D matrices, though the direction of the effect remains contested."
"Contested" is a strong claim — cite both sides. At minimum 2–3 papers showing each direction.
"We previously developed this hydrogel system (see Methods)."
If you cite your own prior paper at first mention, you don't have to re-cite it every sentence. "See Methods" + one Methods-section citation is enough.

In paper-builder, use the Library to drop citations. Don't paste raw citation text in the body — the citation manager handles formatting + the bibliography auto-rebuilds.

5. Authorship & contributions

The McGhee Lab uses ICMJE-adjacent authorship criteria. To be an author, a person should meet all four:

  1. Substantial contribution to conception, design, acquisition, or analysis.
  2. Drafted or critically revised the manuscript for important intellectual content.
  3. Final approval of the version to be submitted.
  4. Accountable for the integrity of the work.

"Lent us a reagent" — not authorship; acknowledgments. "Ran one batch of cells once" — usually not authorship; acknowledgments. "Designed the experiment, analyzed half the data, and wrote two figures" — authorship.

Order of authors

  • First author — did the bulk of the work. Usually the student or postdoc whose project this is.
  • Last author — PI (Alex). The senior accountable position.
  • Co-first / co-corresponding — when contributions are genuinely equal. Decided up front, not retrofitted.
  • Middle authors — by descending contribution.

Contribution statement

Every paper includes a contribution statement using the CRediT taxonomy. Fill it out honestly — co-authors will see it and future grant reviewers may read it. Use the verbs from the CRediT spec: conceptualization, methodology, software, validation, formal analysis, investigation, resources, data curation, writing — original draft, writing — review & editing, visualization, supervision, project administration, funding acquisition.

The hardest part of authorship. Conversations about whether someone has earned authorship should happen before the writing starts. Not after submission, not after revisions. Bring authorship questions to your next PMR.

6. The lab's draft workflow

The lab's drafts live in the paper builder — not in Word, not in Overleaf, not in personal Google Docs. Why: the paper builder syncs to the Library for citations, tracks revisions, and lets Alex read sections live as you write rather than waiting for "the next draft."

The cadence

  1. Outline + figure list in the paper builder. Section headings + 1-sentence purpose per section + figure captions.
  2. Methods + Results first. They're the easiest to write because they describe what you already did. Doing them first builds momentum.
  3. Discussion next. The intellectual heavy lifting. Often goes through 3–4 rounds of revision before Introduction is touched.
  4. Introduction last. Counter-intuitively. You only know what to set up once you know where you're going.
  5. Polish + Abstract. The Abstract is the last thing to write because it summarizes everything that's stable.

Don't batch reviews

Drop a section into the paper builder as soon as you have a messy first version. Alex (and other co-authors) can comment inline. Don't wait until the paper is "done" — by then it's too late to restructure, and you'll spend a month deleting prose that won't survive review.

Track your tasks in PMR

The PMR paper-writing block surfaces all drafts where you're an author. Use it to track status (drafting / review / submitted / in revision / published) and to make sure papers don't quietly stall.

7. The revision letter

When a journal returns reviews, you respond with a point-by-point revision letter alongside the revised manuscript. The letter is a separate document of its own scientific writing — reviewers and the editor read it carefully, and your tone in it shapes whether the next round goes well.

Structure

  • Open with a short, sincere thank-you to the editor and reviewers.
  • For each reviewer, list every comment in order, with your response below.
  • Quote the reviewer's comment verbatim (in italics or a quote block).
  • Your response: what you did, where it appears in the revised manuscript, and any quoted text from the revision.

The tone

Stay technical, not defensive. Disagree where you must — but with a reason and a citation, not with frustration. Even when a review is wrong, your job is to explain why in a way that lets the reviewer save face. The editor is reading this letter as much as the reviewers are.

The three response shapes

  • Agreement. "We thank the reviewer for this excellent suggestion. We have now [...] (revised manuscript p. 8, lines 23–34)."
  • Partial agreement. "We agree this is important to address. We have added [...]. However, we respectfully note that [...] is beyond the scope of this work, because [...]."
  • Disagreement. "We respectfully disagree. Our data show [...] (Figure 3D), consistent with [refs]. We have clarified this in the revised text (p. 5, lines 12–18)."
Never ignore a comment. Even if it's "this is a typo, fix it" — list it, respond, fix it. A revision letter that addresses 14 of 15 comments looks like a revision letter that addresses none of them.

8. Journal selection

The right journal matches the paper's scope, audience, prestige tier, open-access policy, and the lab's budget for APCs (article processing charges). Picking a journal is a conversation with Alex, not a unilateral decision — bring it to your PMR.

What to weigh

  • Scope. Does the paper fit the journal's stated mission? Check the most recent year of articles in your area.
  • Audience. Engineers, biologists, clinicians? The right audience cares about your result; the wrong one finds it niche.
  • Prestige. "Aim high but be realistic." Reach journals are worth one try; rejection costs you time.
  • OA policy. Some funders require open access. Check before submission.
  • APC. Open-access fees can be $3–11k. Check whether the lab has budget; some institutional deals waive the fee.
  • Turnaround time. Some journals decide in 6 weeks; some in 6 months. Important when a thesis deadline is involved.

The "ladder" approach

Pick a target journal and 1–2 fallbacks. If the target rejects, the fallback is ready. Don't reformat for each — pre-formatting for one specific journal before the first submission is a common time-sink that doesn't pay off until you've been accepted.

9. Common failure modes

The first-draft failure modes Alex (and any reviewer) sees over and over.

Buried lede

The actual claim of the paper appears in the third paragraph of the Discussion instead of the last paragraph of the Introduction. Fix: hoist it. If the reader has to read 12 pages to find out what you're claiming, they've already checked out.

"Methods" as storytelling

Three paragraphs of biological context in the middle of the Methods section. Methods should be a recipe, not a narrative. Move the biology to Introduction or Discussion.

Discussion that re-summarizes Results

First two paragraphs of Discussion repeating what Results just said. Reviewers spot this immediately. Replace with interpretation: what does this mean, beyond the data?

Over-claiming generalization

"These results suggest a universal mechanism for tumor invasion." From one cell line, one matrix, one stiffness range? No, they don't. State the scope honestly. Reviewers respect — and grant agencies fund — work that knows its limits.

Citation laziness

Citing a review for a specific mechanistic claim, or citing only the lab's own prior papers when others have published on the same topic. Both signal under-reading and invite rejection.
