Epi-Fluorescence — Ti-e & Ti2-U

The light path of an epi-fluorescence microscope, filter cubes, sources, and the differences between the lab's two workhorses (Ti-e and Ti2-U).

1. The epi-fluorescence light path

"Epi" means the excitation light comes through the objective to the sample, then the same objective collects the emission. One optical column does double duty — that's the whole architecture in a sentence.

The components in order

  1. Source — LED engine or arc lamp.
  2. Excitation filter — narrow bandpass that selects the wavelength your fluorophore is excited by.
  3. Dichroic mirror — reflects the short excitation wavelengths down to the sample, passes the longer emission back up to the camera.
  4. Objective — focuses excitation onto the sample, then collects emission back.
  5. Sample — fluorophore absorbs and re-emits at a longer wavelength (Stokes shift).
  6. Emission filter — narrow bandpass that picks the emission band you want, blocks the rest.
  7. Tube lens — forms the image on the camera.
  8. Camera — the detector at the end of the path.

Stokes shift

A fluorophore re-emits at a longer wavelength than it absorbs. GFP absorbs ~488 nm and emits ~510 nm — about 22 nm of shift. That gap is what makes the dichroic mirror work. No Stokes shift, no fluorescence microscopy.

The "dirty image" failure mode. A bright high-NA sample plus a poorly-matched emission filter lets excitation light bleed onto the camera. Tell: image is bright but contrast is dead and the background is uniformly elevated. Fix: check the cube is seated correctly and that excitation is matched to the cube's spec.

2. Filter cubes

A filter cube packages three optics — the excitation bandpass, the dichroic mirror, and the emission bandpass — into a single slot in the microscope turret. Every fluorophore needs a cube whose three components are matched to its excitation and emission spectra.

What "matched" means

  • Excitation band overlaps the fluorophore's excitation peak (its absorption spectrum).
  • Dichroic edge sits between excitation and emission wavelengths.
  • Emission band overlaps the fluorophore's emission peak, but does not overlap the excitation band.

What happens when it's mismatched

Three failure modes, in order of severity:

  1. Dim signal — excitation doesn't overlap the absorption peak. You're hitting the fluorophore with off-spec light.
  2. No signal — emission band misses the fluorophore peak entirely. The fluorophore is emitting; the cube just isn't letting that light through.
  3. Crosstalk into your channel — emission band is too wide and catches a different fluorophore's tail. Common with DAPI bleeding into GFP.

Try this

  1. Pick a fluorophore from the dropdown. Watch its excitation/emission spectra appear.
  2. Match it with the correct cube. The transmission curves should overlap the spectra.
  3. Now mismatch it: pick a fluorophore but force the GFP cube. Watch the verdict shift to "no signal" or "dim signal."

3. Light sources & power

The lab's two epi-fluorescence frames have different sources. The newer Ti2-U uses a stable LED engine (Lumencor SOLA / X-Cite XLED). The older Ti-e uses a metal-halide arc lamp (X-Cite 120). Both work, but they behave very differently — especially over time.

LED (Ti2-U)

  • Stable output — output power day-to-day variability is < 1%.
  • Instant on / off. No warmup. Safe to switch between samples.
  • Lifetime ~50,000 h. Practically infinite.
  • Discrete wavelengths — typically 4–7 channels with dedicated LEDs.
  • The lab's default for new experiments.

Arc lamp (Ti-e)

  • Bright but unstable — output drifts ±5–20% over a session, more across days.
  • Warmup ~10 min minimum. Bulb lifetime ~2,000 h.
  • Continuous spectrum from UV to red — every cube wavelength is covered.
  • Quantitative live-cell intensity measurements are hard because of drift — use only when the LED engine can't reach a wavelength you need.

The power dial

Both scopes expose a 0-100% power setting. The relationship between dial setting and photon dose is approximately linear for LEDs; for arc lamps it is more variable and depends on bulb age. The right move on either source: find the lowest power that gives usable SNR and stay there.

Try this

  1. Pick a source. Drag the power slider and watch the signal-vs-time curve over 100 frames.
  2. Find the "knee" of the bleach curve — the laser power above which the curve plummets.
  3. Switch to the arc lamp source. Notice the lamp drift — even at low power, the signal isn't flat.
  4. Decide: for a 4-hour live-cell time-lapse where you want intensity to be quantitative, which source would you pick?
