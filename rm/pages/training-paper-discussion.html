Paper writing — the Discussion

Four jobs: interpret, situate, limit, project. Advocate voice with humility. The section reviewers attack hardest — and the section where your scientific contribution actually lands.

1. The four jobs of the Discussion

The Discussion has four distinct jobs. Each one needs a paragraph (or more); missing any of them is a reviewer's entry point to ask for revisions.

  1. Interpret — what your results mean biologically, mechanically, clinically. The interpretive leap from "we observed X" (Results) to "X implies Y" (Discussion).
  2. Situate — how your finding agrees with, contradicts, or extends the existing literature. Specific citations. Real engagement with conflicting work.
  3. Limit — honest acknowledgment of what your data does not show. The specific scope of your claim.
  4. Project — what the next experiment should be, what direction your finding opens up for the field.

The order isn't fixed — many Discussions interleave interpretation with situating, and put limitations near the end with projection. But all four jobs need to appear.

2. The opening paragraph

The Discussion's first paragraph restates your central finding in one sentence and previews how the rest of the section will unpack it. Do not re-summarize Results. The reader just read them.

What the opening paragraph contains:

  • Your central claim, restated in Advocate voice (one sentence).
  • The most important interpretation that follows from it (one to two sentences).
  • A foreshadowing of the rest of the Discussion (one sentence).
The canonical opening: "Our results demonstrate that (your finding). This implies (your strongest interpretation), with consequences for (the broader question). In what follows we place this finding in the context of (prior literature) and consider its limitations." Five-sentence template; works for most papers.

3. Situating against the literature

The Discussion is where you engage prior work, including work that disagrees with you. Reviewers want to see this; its absence reads as either ignorance or cowardice.

  • Agreement. "Consistent with X et al., we find Y..." — extending an established line of work.
  • Disagreement. "In contrast to the report of X et al., we find Y. We attribute this divergence to Z." — name the disagreement, then explain it. Never ignore it.
  • Extension. "X et al. established Y in 2-D culture. Here we extend the finding to 3-D spheroid systems and find that..." — credit the prior work, then mark your contribution.
  • Reconciliation. "Reports have differed on whether stiffness promotes or suppresses invasion. Our results suggest that both findings can be correct, contingent on edge density." — the most powerful Discussion move.

4. Limitations — honest, specific, not boilerplate

Every paper has limitations. State them. Reviewers will find them anyway; better that you name them first and frame them on your terms.

What good limitations look like:

  • Sample scope. "We tested 3 patient-derived spheroid lines; broader cell-type generalization will require additional models."
  • Mechanistic ambiguity. "We cannot fully distinguish a primarily mechanical effect from one mediated by intercellular signaling, though the threshold behavior at 0.12 cells/μm² argues for the former."
  • Method-specific. "Our 24-h assay does not address the longer-timescale invasion phenotypes relevant to clinical metastasis."

What boilerplate limitations look like (avoid):

  • "Further studies are needed." — universally hated.
  • "While our study has some limitations..." — empty hedge.
  • "In vitro models do not fully recapitulate in vivo biology." — true; says nothing.

The honesty meter in § 9 helps you find the middle path between over-claim and over-hedge.

5. The implication ladder

The hardest move in Discussion writing: climb from your specific finding to a broader claim about the field, one rung at a time, without ever leaving your data. This is the "zoom out without losing focus" move from V3.182 § 8.

↑ broadest: field-wide reproducibility issues may have a common mechanical cause
↑ matrix experiments need to control for cell-edge density
↑ tumor mechanics include an unappreciated density variable
your finding: edge density inverts stiffness's effect on invasion

Each rung is a slightly broader claim that follows from the rung below. You earn each rung by making the connection explicit. Skipping rungs ("our finding will change cancer treatment") = over-claim, and reviewers will call it out.

6. Future work — project forward without overpromising

The closing of the Discussion projects forward. Two moves work; one doesn't.

  • Works: Pointing at the specific next experiment your finding makes possible. "Determining whether edge density emerges from cell-density or from boundary mechanics will require a controlled-density assay we describe in Supplementary Note 2."
  • Works: Pointing at the broader open question. "Whether edge density modulates response to mechanotherapeutics remains an open question."
  • Doesn't: "These results may eventually lead to new cancer treatments." Vague hedge, no specific projection.

7. Voice — Advocate throughout

Discussion is written entirely in Advocate voice (V3.182 § 4): claim-making, with humility. The defining verbs:

  • "suggest" — light claim. "Our results suggest that edge density..."
  • "argue for" — stronger claim. "The threshold effect argues for a primarily mechanical mechanism."
  • "are consistent with" — agreement framing.
  • "contrast with" — disagreement framing.
  • "we cannot rule out" — limitation framing.
  • "demonstrate" — strong claim, used sparingly, reserved for the central finding.

What you don't say in Discussion:

  • "prove" — papers don't prove things.
  • "definitively show" — same.
  • "universally" — outside your scope.

8. Common Discussion traps

  • Re-summarizing Results. The first three paragraphs of the Discussion repeat what Results just said. Replace with interpretation.
  • Over-claim generalization. "These results have universal implications for cancer biology." Stick to the scope you actually tested.
  • Ignoring contradictory literature. If a paper disagrees with yours, name it and explain the disagreement. Don't pretend it doesn't exist.
  • Boilerplate limitations. "Further studies are needed" — every reviewer flags this.
  • The future-work avalanche. Listing 10 follow-up experiments dilutes the strongest one. Pick 1–2.
  • No interpretive leap. A Discussion that never moves beyond "we found X" reads as a Results section repeated. Make the interpretive claim explicit.
  • Mismatched scope. Discussion at scope level 2 (cancer kills people) when your data is at level 8. Climb the ladder gradually.
