The Three Pillars of a Good PhD

What we expect of you, what we'll help you develop, and how to know you're growing.

What "a good PhD" actually means here

A doctorate isn't a credential you earn by accumulating credits. It's a transformation — by the end of it you should be able to identify a real scientific problem, design experiments that genuinely discriminate hypotheses, do the work even when the work fights back, and explain what you found to other humans well enough that they will trust and build on it.

We've found that the people who do this well tend to be strong on three pillars — and the strongest students are not the ones who arrive with all three; they're the ones who recognize which of their pillars is underdeveloped and put in the work to grow it.

The honest part: almost no one comes in with all three fully formed. That is fine. Your job over the next few years is to figure out which one is yours to grow.

✍️ Scientific Communication

Writing papers and grants, presenting at lab meeting and at conferences, teaching, and defending what you've done. Communication is the channel through which your work exists outside your own head — without it, brilliant results stay private and never compound.

What good looks like: You can outline a paper from your figures before you write a sentence. You can walk someone outside the lab through your project in five minutes without losing them. You handle reviewer pushback as a technical problem, not a personal one.

How to grow it here: draft early and often in the paper builder (don't wait until the work is "done"); present at journal club; volunteer to teach a class section; ask Alex for feedback on early prose, not just polished prose.

Failure mode: clear thinking that never reaches another human — work no one finds because it was never written up.

💡 Creativity & Synthesis

Generating questions that haven't been asked, connecting ideas across fields, and designing experiments that genuinely discriminate between hypotheses (not just confirm the one you already believe). Synthesis is what turns a stack of papers into a research program.

What good looks like: You can read three papers from adjacent fields and propose an experiment none of them did. You can predict what the result would mean before you run it. You routinely suggest the experiment that would break your own model.

How to grow it here: use the Library aggressively — annotate, don't just read; bring suggestions to PMR brainstorming blocks; rotate through other lab members' projects in group meetings; design at least one experiment per quarter that you wouldn't have designed a year ago.

Failure mode: technically competent but derivative — only asks questions other people have already framed.

🪨 Grit

The capacity to keep working through failed experiments, lost samples, rejected papers, scooping, funding turn-downs, and the years-long arc of doctoral work. Grit is not stubbornness — it's the ability to take a real blow, reassess, and keep moving on the parts that are still worth moving on.

What good looks like: when an experiment fails twice you treat it as data about your protocol, not evidence of personal inadequacy. You can articulate what changed between attempt 3 and attempt 4. You ask for help before you've spent two weeks stuck.

How to grow it here: use the PMR barriers block the moment something blocks you, not three weeks later; bring near-failures to lab meeting, not just successes; build the habit of weekly written reflection on what worked and what didn't.

Failure mode: bright but fragile — the first hard "no" ends the project, or stubborn for-its-own-sake persistence on a doomed experiment.

How the lab will help you grow each pillar

You won't develop these in isolation. Every surface in RM was built to feed one or more pillars — use them.

✍️ Communication

  • Paper builder — start drafting early
  • Library — annotate as you read
  • Lab meeting + journal club rotations
  • Conference travel — talk to Alex about budgets

💡 Creativity

  • PMR brainstorming sections
  • Protocols — see what already works
  • Cross-project group meetings
  • Sit in on other labs' seminars

🪨 Grit

  • PMR barriers block — name the obstacle
  • Weekly 1:1s with Alex
  • Peer support inside the lab
  • Weekly reflection on what worked / didn't

📚 Knowledge

  • Lab Notebook — record everything
  • Library annotations + discussions
  • Recommended reading lists per project
  • This training library — keep coming back

One thing to take away

You will not finish a PhD by maxing out the pillar you were strongest on at the start. You finish it by recognizing the one you've been quietly avoiding — and growing it.
