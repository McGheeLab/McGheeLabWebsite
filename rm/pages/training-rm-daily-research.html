Daily Research Workflow in RM

2. Activity tracker

The tracker is the single ledger of your research time. Most entries land in it without you typing — every meaningful action on every RM page fires a pending tracker entry via the V3.160 ActivitySignals helper. Your job is to confirm hours, not to dictate the whole entry.

The two lanes that matter

  • Pending banner — at the top of the daily view. Auto-created entries waiting on hours. One tap on the hours field + ✓ commits the entry as confirmed. ✗ dismisses it.
  • Coming-up lane — pending entries scheduled for today: a subtask whose due_date is today, a calendar event on today's date, an action item with a deadline of today. Each row has a "Log it" button that moves it into the pending banner once the work actually happened.

The 30-second habit. End of day, open the tracker, walk the pending banner. Three taps per entry on an average day. Anyone who tells you they "don't have time" is paying the cost in an empty PMR Activity block and a 1:1 where Alex doesn't know what they did this week.

3. Lab notebook

The notebook is where you write up an experiment — title, date, body, attachments (images / PDFs), references to protocols and inventory items, and observations once a run is underway. Each saved entry auto-creates a pending tracker entry (you'll confirm hours on it in the tracker).

What to include in an entry

  • Date. Defaults to today. Set it to the day the work actually happened, not the day you wrote it up.
  • Project. Picks from your assigned projects. Drives downstream filtering + the PMR project-overview rollup.
  • Phase. plan / running / complete. Switch to running when you walk to the bench; complete when the run is over. Running mode shows the observations log + summary fields.
  • Attachments. Drop in microscopy images, gel scans, vendor datasheets. Each gets a thumbnail in the attachments strip.
  • References. Reagents from inventory, protocols you followed. These chips light up the lab-wide "Materials Used" panel and the protocol's "followed by" panel.

The PMR badge — flag images for discussion

Every image attachment has a in its top-left corner. Clicking it flags the image for review in your next PMR period. The image then appears as a card in the PMR's Figure Reviews block, with an editable caption, a fullscreen lightbox, a deep-link back to this notebook entry, and a comment thread the admin can use during the 1:1.

4. Library

The library is the lab's collective paper reading surface. It ingests PDFs (Chrome extension grab, manual upload, or the auto-download pipeline), runs metadata extraction, and opens each paper in the reader with highlight + comment tooling.

The four jobs the library does

  • Ingest. Paste a DOI, drop a PDF, or let the Chrome extension grab one from a journal tab. Metadata extracts automatically (title, authors, year, journal, abstract, references).
  • Read + annotate. Open a paper, drag to highlight, double-click a highlight to leave a comment. Citations on the page are listed in the left-rail context menu; hover one to see it highlighted inline.
  • Discuss. Comments thread per paper and per highlight. Other lab members see them on the same paper.
  • Surface citations for paper writing. Library entries are the canonical source for citations dropped into the paper builder via Cmd+K.

Per-review-task overlays

You can scope highlights + comments to a specific review task. Reading the same paper for two different reviews (a GELS lit review and an MEBP comparison, say) → two overlays, toggleable. Highlights stay separate even though the paper is shared.

Comments are durable. They survive re-imports and overlay toggling. Use them for the non-obvious "this paragraph is actually the load-bearing one for our argument" annotations that you'd otherwise forget by next week.

5. Protocols

Protocols are the lab's reusable step-by-step procedures — hydrogel formulations, staining workflows, cell-culture checklists. Each protocol is a Y.Doc collaborative document with versioning, materials lists, figure attachments, and (the killer feature) formula blocks that turn the protocol into a live calculator.

Anatomy of a protocol

  • Sections + steps. Numbered steps, with notes and safety callouts. Markdown-style rich text.
  • Materials block. Reagents + equipment. Each row can resolve to a real inventory item (chip with current stock + hazard pictograms).
  • Formula block. Named variables and an expression. Inline [!var:name] tokens inside step text render as live computed chips. When you run the protocol, you enter input values once; every step's calculations update.
  • Version history. Every protocol auto- saves snapshots. Roll back to any prior version; diff them to see what changed.

"Run" mode pulls protocols into the notebook

When you click calc in the notebook's protocol picker, the protocol's formula block opens as a modal — enter inputs, choose render format (Steps / Snippet / Table), and the rendered block lands in your notebook entry with all variable chips computed. Re-click to re-open with the same inputs preserved.

Build the formula block early. Even if a protocol is mostly text steps, naming the 2-3 quantities that matter as variables turns the rest of the protocol into a live worksheet. You'll never re-do the maths by hand again.

6. How these four pages talk to each other

The connections aren't decorative — they're how the system avoids making you re-type things. Memorize this short list:

  • Notebook → Tracker. Save a notebook entry → pending tracker entry on the entry's date, category research › experiments. Confirm hours later.
  • Protocol → Tracker. Save a protocol (create or substantial edit) → pending tracker entry on today, category research › writing › protocols.
  • Notebook → Protocol. Notebook's protocol-followed panel back-links to every notebook entry that referenced this protocol. Useful when debugging "why did my hydrogel set differently last week?" — open the protocol, see which run it was, jump to that entry.
  • Library → Paper builder. Library entries are the citation source for Cmd+K in the paper builder. Annotations on a library paper surface in the paper's "evidence" panel when you cite it.
  • Notebook image → PMR. The flag (Section 3).
  • Protocol materials → Inventory. Each materials row that resolves to an inventory item appears in that item's "used in protocols" panel — so you know what to keep stocked.
The pattern: do the work once on the page where it's most natural. The cross-links fire automatically. Never duplicate.

7. How they feed PMR

At the end of the week, you open PMR and almost everything is already there:

  • Activity block — your confirmed tracker entries dated within the period, grouped by week and category. Pending entries appear with an amber badge — Alex will ask about those during the 1:1.
  • Stats block — hours-by-top-level- category for the period + a daily sparkline. Sourced from the same tracker entries.
  • Figure reviews block — every notebook image you flagged with in this period. Editable caption, comment thread, deep-link back.
  • Open tasks block — every subtask where you're the assignee, across all of your task builders. Subtasks created from PMR (via "+ Assign task") cross-link back.
  • Paper writing block — auto-listed drafts where you're in authorUids.

All you actually write in PMR is:

  • A short paragraph in the current weekly entry — what got done, what's blocked, what's coming next.
  • Barriers cards when something's blocking your progress (and you don't want to wait for the 1:1 to raise it).
  • Optional comments on any block where you want to flag something for the 1:1.
An empty PMR Activity block on Friday means something went wrong during the week. Almost always: pending tracker entries weren't confirmed. Backfill them; the block fills in.

8. Capstone — a real day

Work through this scenario start-to-finish. Each step is the smallest possible action that leaves the right trace in RM. Total time: ~10 minutes of work + 8 minutes of RM capture spread across the day.

  1. Morning. Open the home page → Lab Research workflow card. Strip appears with six chips. Glance at the tracker's Coming-up lane — anything due today? Note the deadline.
  2. At the bench. Open Lab Notebook → "+ new entry". Title: "Hydrogel batch 12 — day 7 imaging". Date: today. Project: GELS. Phase: running. Hit save.
  3. Scope time. Acquire 5 confocal images of your hydrogel. Drag-and-drop one of them into the notebook entry's body. Hit the PMR badge on the image — it's the one Alex needs to see at next 1:1. Type 2 sentences in the body explaining what you saw.
  4. Reagent reference. Click Inventory in the notebook's toolbar. Pick "PBS, 1×". A chip lands in the body. Same for "Hoechst 33342". Both show hazard pictograms.
  5. Followed a protocol. Click Protocol in the toolbar. Pick "GELS staining v3". Press Alt+ Enter on the row → calc opens. Enter sample volume (2 mL), pick Steps format, insert. The protocol lands in your entry as live-computed steps.
  6. Phase complete. Click End run. Type a one-line summary in the Summary field that just appeared. Save.
  7. Library hop. Switch chip on the strip to Library. Open the Maeda 2024 paper you've been reading. Highlight the methods paragraph that contradicts your batch's behaviour; double-click to comment "ours gels in 45 min not 30 — investigate temperature".
  8. End of day. Switch to tracker. Pending banner shows two entries: the notebook entry (research › experiments) and the protocol-save (research › writing › protocols). Type real hours (1.5h, 0.5h), hit ✓ on both.
  9. Friday. Open PMR. Activity block shows your week's entries grouped by day. Stats block shows ~10h research-experiments, ~3h research-writing-papers, etc. Figure reviews block has the confocal image you flagged earlier in the week. File the weekly entry paragraph. You're done.
What didn't you do? You didn't type a tracker entry by hand. You didn't paste an image into PMR. You didn't write "I worked on protocol X today" in the weekly entry — the Stats block shows it. You did the work; the system recorded the work.

9. Quiz

Five questions on the daily flow. Pick an answer; wrong picks explain the right one.

You spent 90 minutes acquiring confocal images and writing them up in a lab notebook entry. How does that time enter the tracker?
Fully automatic. Saving a lab notebook entry logs an activity event on its own (V3.320) — it shows up on your activity heatmap with zero clicks. There are no hours and nothing to confirm.
A confocal image you acquired is the single most important thing to discuss at next PMR. What do you do?
flag. One click creates a figure-review card in your next PMR period with editable caption + comment thread + deep-link back to the originating notebook entry. The other options all lose the notebook context.
A subtask has a due date of today. Where do you see it on the tracker?
Coming-up lane. Future-dated entries (subtask due_date, calendar events, action item deadlines) surface in the Coming-up lane on their due date. Click "Log it" once the work actually happened and it moves into the pending banner for hours-confirmation.
You're running a protocol and need 2.5 mL working solution. The protocol has a formula block. Power-user move?
calc. The notebook's protocol picker has a calc sub-action that opens the protocol's formula block as a modal, prefilled. Outputs render as live chips in your notebook entry. Re-clicking re-opens with the same inputs preserved.
Friday afternoon. Your PMR Activity block is empty. What went wrong?
Backfill pending. The PMR Activity block reads confirmed tracker entries. Pending entries don't count. Empty block almost always means tracker confirmations were skipped — walk the pending banner across the week's dates and confirm.
Pick an answer on each item to see how fluent you are in the daily flow.
