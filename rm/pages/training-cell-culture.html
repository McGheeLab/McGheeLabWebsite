Cell Culture & Sterile Technique

The cabinet, the incubator, your hands, and the bottle everyone shares — the four things that actually kill cultures, and what is physically happening in each. Tab 5 is a diagnostic gallery; Tab 6 is your bench sign-off sheet. The full derivations live in the four-lesson Cell Culture Foundations track.

1. The cabinet is a shape made of moving air

A biosafety cabinet is not a box that keeps things out. It is two streams of air doing two different jobs, and the metal is what holds their shape. Turn the blower off and it is a shelf.

  • Downflow (≈ 0.25–0.36 m/s, from the HEPA filter in the ceiling) protects your culture — a slow rain of sterile air onto the work surface.
  • Inflow (≥ 0.51 m/s, room air pulled in at the front grille) protects you — it is captured at the grille and never reaches the work surface.

The two meet a few centimetres inside the opening and turn down together. That meeting line is the air curtain. Everything that goes wrong at a cabinet is something you did to it.

Why the sash height is a safety setting

The blower moves a roughly fixed volume per second, and volume flow is velocity times area. The area is the work opening — width × sash height — so:

vin = Q / (w · hsash)

Inflow velocity is inversely proportional to sash height. Going from the certified 8 inches to 16 halves the velocity holding the room out — and nothing sounds any different.

Overcrowding, which is the question people actually get wrong. Blocking the front grille makes the remaining air go faster, not slower. It is still worse, for two reasons: across the covered span the inward velocity is zero, so there is no curtain there at all; and the rest becomes a jet that rolls up into vortices and mixes room air into the work zone. About a third of the grille is enough to do this.

Try this

  1. Start with the sash at 8 in and nothing blocked. Note the inflow and the "reaching the work surface" figure.
  2. Raise the sash to 16 in without touching anything else. Watch the inflow readout and the verdict.
  3. Put the sash back and block 40% of the grille instead. Different cause, same result.
  4. Set hand speed above 50 cm/s. Your hands can be faster than the protection.

The curtain moves at about 0.5 m/s. Move your hands slower than that, and parallel to the sash rather than in and out through it. That one sentence is most of what "good technique in the hood" means, and it is the only part you can practise with no cells at all.

2. The incubator: three knobs and a puddle

37 °C, 5% CO2, about 95% relative humidity. The first is obvious, the second is not a magic number, and the third has an open tray of warm water attached to it that is a documented contamination source.

Why 5%

The pH of your medium is a ratio — the bicarbonate the manufacturer put in the powder over the CO2 the box is holding:

pH = 6.1 + log10( [HCO3−] / (0.0307 · pCO2) )

5% is what balances about 24 mM bicarbonate at pH 7.4 — which is RPMI. DMEM has 3.7 g/L ≈ 44 mM and therefore runs alkaline at 5%; the high-bicarbonate formulations specify 10%. The percentage on the incubator belongs to the medium, not to the cells.

Cells left too long. The gas has not changed. What changed is the top of the ratio: every millimole of lactic acid the cells make consumes a millimole of bicarbonate, and buffer capacity is proportional to the bicarbonate that is left. So the pH holds almost flat until about three quarters of the buffer is gone, then falls through two-tenths of a unit in a few hours. That is why it "looked fine yesterday" — it was fine yesterday. The colour change is the end of the buffer, not the beginning of the problem. And the medium goes acid while there is still plenty of glucose: the cells poison themselves before they starve.

The water pan

It is there to stop the medium concentrating, not to stop it drying out. A dish that has lost a fifth of its volume looks completely normal — liquid present, cells covered, colour fine — and is at 400 mOsm/kg against 320 fresh. It costs you a 37 °C open reservoir under a circulating fan: sterile distilled water only, cleaned monthly, spills wiped immediately, never allowed to run dry.

Try this

  1. Start on RPMI at 5%. That is the reference colour.
  2. Drag CO2 to 0.04% — room air. Where does the colour go?
  3. Switch to DMEM without touching the gas. Why did the colour change when the gas did not?
  4. Add lactate. Find the concentration at which the medium turns visibly orange.

3. The hands: accuracy, and then sterility

A pipettor does not touch your liquid. There is a column of air between the piston and the sample, and everything that goes wrong is something that happens to that air.

The two stops

The first stop is calibrated: it displaces the volume on the dial. The second stop pushes further and exists to clear the tip on dispense. Aspirating from the second stop pulls up more than you set and is the classic systematic error — the dial still says the right number. The one deliberate exception is reverse pipetting for viscous or foaming liquids: aspirate from the second stop, dispense only to the first, and discard the remainder with the tip.

The six habits

Every term in the error budget is a decision, not a hardware limit:

  1. Right pipettor for the volume. 10 µL from a 0.5–10 µL device, not from a 10–100 µL one. Usually the biggest single term.
  2. Pre-wet the tip above 10 µL — aspirate and dispense back into the source once.
  3. Hold it vertical while aspirating.
  4. Immerse just below the surface; never touch the bottom.
  5. Wait one second before withdrawing the tip.
  6. Touch off against the vessel wall to clear the tip.

Two more that are free: leave the pipettor on its stand between cycles (a warm hand expands the air cushion), and change the tip between liquids — reusing one costs about 4%.

And now the half that loses the experiment

None of the above keeps anything sterile. Three physical facts drive everything that does:

1 · Blow-out makes two populations. Big droplets are ballistic and land a hand's width away — you can wipe those up. Droplets around 5 µm stop essentially where they left the tip and then take minutes to fall 30 cm. That invisible fraction is why you touch off against the wall rather than blowing out into air, and why filter tips protect the pipettor shaft as much as the sample.
2 · A wet thread is a road. Capillary rise goes as 2γcosθ/(ρgr) — inverse in the gap — so a 0.1 mm cap thread lifts medium about 8 cm, many times the height of any bottle neck. A drop left on the threads after pouring becomes a continuous film from the medium to the outside. Do not pour; use a 50 or 100 mL pipette, and if you must pour, wipe the neck.
3 · A reach-over is 10–100× baseline. Not infinite — your hand is a local source at 10 cm instead of a room-averaged one at a metre. Nothing visible happens the first time, which is exactly why it has to be a habit rather than a judgement call. You do it two hundred times a month.

Opening things — the part nobody writes down

One rule, and everything below is a consequence: a sterile surface stays sterile only while nothing unsterile has passed above it or touched it.

  • Tip box — open it inside the cabinet; lift the lid on its hinge and leave it attached as a shield; take the tip straight down and straight up, never sweeping the pipettor sideways across an open rack; close it between uses.
  • Serological pipette — peel the wrapper from the plunger end, seat the controller while the pipette is still mostly wrapped, then slide the wrapper off and straight into the waste. Never tear open the pointed end.
  • Caps — pre-loosen before you start so one hand can open a tube while the other holds the controller. The cap stays in your hand, or goes threads-up on a clean surface. Never threads-down.
  • Anything from a water bath — wipe the outside before it comes in. A wet bottle drips into the work zone.
  • One pipette per liquid. Never re-enter a stock bottle with a pipette that has been in a culture.
McGhee Lab practice. This section is house procedure, not a vendor document — no published guide specifies how to open a tip box. The reasoning is physics and transfers anywhere; the exact hand order depends on your cabinet and your handedness. Walk through it once with Alex before you work alone.

4. Aliquoting: the one thing that works without changing your technique

Every entry into a vessel carries some small probability p of letting something in. Entries are independent, so over N of them:

P(contaminated) = 1 − (1 − p)N

A 500 mL bottle of medium opened 40 times, with technique most people would call good (p = 0.5%), has an 18% chance of being contaminated before it is used up. That is not a statement about you. It is a statement about doing anything forty times.

What that costs

Expected loss is probability times blast radius — how much that vessel was holding. Splitting the stock k ways shrinks both at once: each aliquot is entered N/k times and holds V/k. Twenty aliquots of a 500 mL bottle cuts the expected loss by roughly an order of magnitude, and your p does not move at all.

Where it stops improving, and why that matters. Making the aliquots is itself one entry into the master plus k fills, and neither of those falls with k. So the curve flattens onto a floor set entirely by the transfer that made them — and a failure there is copied into every tube identically. Aliquoting does not remove risk so much as concentrate it into one moment you can prepare for: a clean cabinet, nothing else in it, full attention, not at the end of a long day, and a sterility check afterwards.

The rest of stewardship

  • Size for a single session. A tube opened once and finished has no freeze–thaw term at all — not small, zero.
  • Five fields on the label: what it is (full name + concentration), lot / source, date split, volume, initials.
  • Sterility check the batch: 10 mL of outdated antibiotic-free medium with 3–5% serum in a screw-cap tube, incubated at 30 °C and 37 °C for at least two weeks. Clean is evidence, not a guarantee — mycoplasma passes it silently.
  • Small precious stocks have a floor. The few microlitres of unrecoverable film per tube is nothing out of 25 mL and a sixth of a 20 µL aliquot. Split a bottle of medium aggressively; find the bottom for an antibody.

Try this

  1. Start at 1 aliquot — that is one bottle. Read the expected loss.
  2. Drag to 20. How much better, as a ratio?
  3. Keep going to 100. Find the number the curve flattens onto.
  4. Set the master-transfer risk to zero. Where does the floor go?

5. Case files — ten cultures that look wrong

Diagnosis is the skill this training is actually for. Below are ten cultures, described the way you would actually meet them: what you see in the flask, what you see down the scope, and what the medium looks like. Pick the cause before you read the answer.

Four of these are not contamination. That is the point. The most expensive mistake in this list is not missing an infection — it is autoclaving a culture that had an incubator problem, or spending a fortnight on a coating protocol when something was vibrating.
0 / 10
Case 1. Yesterday the flask was clear and red. This morning the medium is cloudy, slightly yellow, and there is a faint film. Down the scope: shimmering motile dots between the cells.
Bacteria. Fast (overnight), visibly turbid, and the pH drops because they make acid too. Motile dots at high magnification confirm it. Autoclave it. Rescue attempts almost never work and the flask is a reservoir while you try. Then ask which rule broke: an unsterile entry, a reach-over, or a re-entered bottle — and check whether anything shares that bottle.
Case 2. The medium is clear and the right colour. Nothing visible down the scope. But growth has been slower for a month, and an assay that used to work does not replicate.
Mycoplasma. 0.3–0.8 µm, no cell wall, reaching 107–109/mL with no turbidity, no pH shift and no cytopathic effect. It changes metabolism and gene expression, so the symptom is irreproducibility, not sickness. You cannot see it — you test for it (Hoechst DNA stain, PCR, or culture), and the strongest predictor is a cell line brought in from another lab without quarantine.
Case 3. A fuzzy white sphere floating on the meniscus, filamentous strands radiating from it. Faintly musty smell when you open the flask. The medium is otherwise clear.
Fungal. Slower than bacteria, visible as mycelia, and often smellable. Ask where the spores came from: the incubator water tray and shelves soiled with spilled medium are the classic reservoirs, and the circulating fan distributes them. Cardboard and plants in the culture room are the other two. Autoclave the flask and clean the incubator.
Case 4. Monday morning. The medium is bright yellow. The cells are still attached but rounded. You seeded on Friday afternoon and the flask is about 70% confluent.
Not contamination. The medium is clear, just yellow. Lactate ate the bicarbonate over 64 hours; the pH held flat and then crashed on Sunday while there was still plenty of glucose left. Change the medium now — attached cells usually recover — and note it in the log, because they have an acid-stress history. The fix next Friday is more medium, not fewer cells: growth is limited by surface area, acid is diluted by volume, and those are independent levers.
Case 5. The medium in every flask on every shelf is pink-purple. Cells are rounded and poorly attached. Nothing is cloudy.
Not contamination — and the simultaneity is the diagnosis. Alkaline means the bottom of the pH ratio has collapsed: not enough dissolved CO₂. One pink flask is a flask problem; a pink incubator is the tank, the regulator, the sensor or a door left ajar. Check the cylinder pressure before you touch anything else.
Case 6. The line looks subtly wrong — morphology has shifted, and it now doubles noticeably faster than it used to. Everything is clean and healthy. Two lines were being fed in the cabinet at the same time last month.
Cross-contamination. Faster growth is the tell — the overtaking line is usually the more vigorous one, which is how HeLa displaced so many lines historically. It is invisible to every routine check and only found by authentication (STR profiling). Prevention is procedural and absolute: one cell line in the cabinet at a time, separate bottles of medium per line, disinfect between lines, quarantine anything that arrives from another lab.
Case 7. A 35 mm dish, fixed and stained. The cells have settled into clean concentric rings around the centre. Attachment is fine everywhere; only the density varies.
Vibration — not biology, not chemistry. Cells landing in a liquid driven at a standing-wave frequency collect at the nodes, and the spacing is set by the frequency. Look for a loose fan motor in the incubator, a centrifuge or fridge compressor on the same bench, or foot traffic on a suspended floor. This one costs people weeks on coating protocols.
Case 8. A 96-well plate after three days. The interior wells look perfect. The entire outer ring is sparse, the cells look shrunken, and those wells are visibly a little low.
The edge effect. An outer well has a shallow, exposed vapour path and only ~200 µL to lose it from, so it concentrates several times faster than a middle well. Osmolality goes as V₀/V, so it accelerates — a well that has lost a fifth of its volume is already past 380 mOsm. Fixes: keep the incubator water tray full, and fill the outer ring with sterile PBS and do not use it.
Case 9. Patchy, blotchy attachment with clear round "holes" in the monolayer, plus odd spots on the vessel. It happens to whoever seeded the plate, not to a particular incubator or medium lot.
Handling. Uneven mixing or dispensing straight onto the monolayer makes bubbles and foam, which block attachment and leave spots — dispense down the wall. Static electricity does something similar in low-humidity rooms: try not to rub vessels against the packaging when opening them. The tell is that it follows the person, not the equipment or the lot.
Case 10. Cells die by passage three, but only in one incubator. Sterility checks are clean, mycoplasma is negative, pH and humidity read normal, and the same medium works fine next door.
Chemical. Nothing is growing — something is poisoning. The usual suspects, in order: disinfectant residue on shelves or in the tray after a clean, impurities in the CO₂ supply, plasticiser or endotoxin leaching from a storage vessel, and photoactivation of HEPES/riboflavin/tryptophan by fluorescent light producing hydrogen peroxide and free radicals. Chemical contaminants are additive, which is why the same medium can be fine in one box and lethal in another.
The habit worth taking away. Before reaching for the autoclave, ask three questions in order: is it in every vessel or just this one? (a whole-box symptom is equipment), is anything actually growing? (clear medium with sick cells is chemistry or physics), and does it follow the person, the lot, or the box? That triage separates eight of these ten cases in under a minute.

This is the competency check. Configure a real media change on six T-25 flasks of one cell line, then answer six things that will actually happen during it. The sheet at the bottom only shows a pass when every critical item is right; print it and bring it to your first supervised session.

Six things that will happen during the run

Pick what you would actually do.

0 / 6
You drop the cap of the medium bottle on the work surface, threads down.
Fresh cap. Alcohol on the threads does not sterilise a contact-contaminated surface reliably, and it introduces liquid exactly where a capillary bridge forms. Wiping is what you do to a neck that got wet; a cap that touched the surface is a different problem.
While reaching for tips, the bottom of the medium bottle brushes the front intake grille.
Wipe it. The outside of the bottle is not sterile — but the grille is the dirtiest surface in the cabinet, because everything that gets captured goes through it. The risk is not the bottle's contents; it is that you now carry grille-surface material over your open flasks. Discarding is over-reaction; ignoring is how a run goes wrong.
Flask three's medium is distinctly orange rather than red. The other five look normal.
Set it aside and look. Orange in one flask is either over-confluence (fine, feed it and log it) or early contamination (not fine, and you must not put a fresh pipette into your medium bottle afterwards). You cannot tell without looking, and the cost of getting it wrong is asymmetric. Finish the healthy flasks first so a possible contaminant is last, not first.
Halfway through, someone opens the culture-room door to ask you a question.
Stop and cap. A door sweeps a large volume of air and sets up a cross-draft that peels the air curtain off sideways — the same failure as a fast hand, but across the whole width. Talking also generates aerosols directly over your work, and mycoplasma has been recovered from exactly that. Cap, answer, let it settle, carry on.
The tip of your serological pipette brushes the inside edge of the sash on the way in.
Discard it. The sash is at the boundary — it is the surface room air flows past on its way to the grille, and it is not wiped between runs the way the work surface is. And never flame anything in a cabinet: it damages the filter and, per the CDC, "creates turbulence that disrupts the pattern of HEPA-filtered air being supplied to the work surface".
You realise you already put the used pipette back into the medium bottle two flasks ago.
Stop and tell people. This is the one on the list that reaches beyond your own bench: everything fed from that bottle is suspect, and anyone else who uses it needs to know today rather than in two weeks. The published human-factors point is exact — most major contamination events trace to someone who knew they had done something and said nothing. Communication is the control.
