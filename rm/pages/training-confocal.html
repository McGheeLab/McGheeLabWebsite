1. Why confocal: the pinhole

A widefield fluorescence image of a 200 μm spheroid is dominated by out-of-focus blur — every plane above and below your focal plane contributes signal. The confocal pinhole rejects that out-of-plane light, leaving you with a clean optical section. That's it. That's the whole reason this scope exists.

The Airy Unit (AU)

Pinhole diameter is measured in Airy Units. 1 AU is the diameter of the Airy disk projected to the pinhole plane — it's the natural scale for "how much in-focus light to keep."

section thickness ≈ √[ (0.88 · λ_em / (n − √(n² − NA²)))² + (√2 · n · pinhole_AU / NA)² ]

Don't memorize this — just hold the intuition: smaller pinhole → thinner optical section and less light. The choice is a real trade-off, not a single right answer.

Practical rule of thumb on the AX-R

  • 1 AU — the canonical "best of both worlds" setting. Use this unless you have a reason not to.
  • < 1 AU — thinner section, but you start losing real signal — usable only on bright samples.
  • > 1 AU — better SNR at the cost of axial resolution. Useful for dim live samples where you must collect more photons.
  • Wide open — you've turned a confocal into a widefield. Sometimes intentional (rapid live screening); often a mistake.
Worked example. Imaging GFP (λ_em ≈ 510 nm) with the 25×/1.1 water objective at 1 AU yields an optical section of ~0.6 μm — about 10× thinner than the equivalent widefield image of the same spheroid plane.

2. Scan modes — resonant vs. galvo

The AX-R scans the laser beam across the sample with two mirrors. "Galvo" mode drives them at slow, controlled angles for the highest fidelity per pixel; "resonant" mode oscillates one mirror at its mechanical resonance (~30 fps) for fast live imaging. They are good at different problems.

The trade-off

  • Galvo — long pixel dwell time → more photons per pixel → high SNR; slow (typically < 1 fps for a 1024×1024 frame).
  • Resonant — short pixel dwell → fewer photons → noisier individual frames; but you can run at 30+ fps and average many frames in software to recover SNR.

Frame averaging

Averaging N frames divides noise by √N. Resonant + N=16 averaging is the lab's default for "looks like galvo but you can still see live dynamics on the screen while you're acquiring."

Decision rule. Fixed-cell archival 3-D Z-stack → galvo. Live-cell dynamics where motion is faster than ~1 second per frame → resonant. Anything in between → start resonant with N=8 averaging and back off if the cells are dying.

Try this

  1. Toggle between Galvo and Resonant — note how noisy the single-frame resonant view is.
  2. Increase the averaging slider in Resonant mode and watch noise drop toward the Galvo image quality.
  3. Note the per-frame time: even with N=16 averaging, resonant gets you ~0.5 s per averaged frame; galvo would be ~8 s for the same image.
  4. Pick the mode you'd use for a 30-minute live-cell time-lapse with cells migrating ~1 μm/min. Then pick the mode for a 4-color fixed-spheroid Z-stack.

3. Laser lines & the spectral detector

The AX-R has a 4-line laser turret (405 / 488 / 561 / 640 nm) and a tunable spectral detector. Each fluorophore in your sample needs a laser to excite it and a detector window to collect its emission. Overlapping emission bands cause crosstalk — a second fluorophore's signal bleeding into your channel of interest. Sequential acquisition (not all-at-once) is the standard cure.

What couples with what

  • 405 nm → DAPI, Hoechst (nuclei)
  • 488 nm → GFP, Alexa-488, Phalloidin-488
  • 561 nm → mCherry, RFP, Alexa-568, mScarlet
  • 640 nm → Alexa-647, Cy5, mIFP

The crosstalk failure modes

  • DAPI (Em max ~460 nm) is broad — its tail leaks into the 488 detection window.
  • GFP (Em max ~510 nm) has a long red tail that bleeds into the 561 channel if you collect too wide a band.
  • mCherry (Em max ~610 nm) tails into far-red — usually less severe but watch it on bright samples.
Decision rule. Always acquire DAPI sequentially — its blue emission is too broad to share a frame with 488. GFP + mCherry can usually be acquired together if you keep the 488 collection window tight (500–540 nm). 488 + 640 are spectrally separated enough to be safe simultaneous.

Try this

  1. Toggle each fluorophore in the panel below. Note where each fluorophore's emission peak falls on the spectrum.
  2. Enable DAPI + GFP simultaneously and look at the crosstalk warning.
  3. Enable GFP + mCherry — the warning should fire if the collection bands overlap. Try narrowing the 488 collection window to fix it.
  4. The standard lab 4-color spheroid (DAPI + Phalloidin-488 + Anti-Target-561 + Anti-Target2-640) is safe with sequential acquisition. Verify the warning panel agrees.

4. Z-stacks & Nyquist in Z

Acquiring a Z-stack is the confocal's superpower — you build the 3-D volume one optical section at a time. The question is always: how thick should each step be? Too thick and you alias; too thin and you waste photons and time.

The Nyquist rule

Δz_Nyquist ≈ λ_em / (2 · NA² · n)

For our 25×/1.1 water objective imaging GFP at 510 nm: Δz ≈ 510 / (2 · 1.21 · 1.33) ≈ 159 nm. A 20 μm spheroid needs ~125 slices at Nyquist.

When to deviate

  • Survey scan / live cell — 2× under-sample is fine; you halve the photon dose and the time.
  • Deconvolution / quantitative — stay at Nyquist or slightly tighter; deconvolution can't recover what wasn't sampled.
  • Going thinner than Nyquist — almost never useful; bigger file, longer scan, no new information.

Try this

  1. Pick an objective (the AX-R workhorses are 25×/1.1 and 40×/1.3).
  2. Pick a wavelength — GFP at 510 nm is the canonical reference.
  3. Set a total stack depth (try 20 μm for a spheroid slab).
  4. Toggle through Nyquist, 2× under, and 2× over sampling. Note the time / file size / slice-count consequences.
  5. For the 200 μm spheroid Z-stack you defined in the V3.164 capstone, compute the slice count at Nyquist.

5. Photobleaching & phototoxicity

Every photon you collect from a fluorophore costs you photons of excitation, and many of those excitations destroy the dye (bleaching) or damage the cell (phototoxicity). Phototoxicity ≠ bleaching, but tracks it — if you're bleaching aggressively, you're also irradiating the cells.

The dose model

dose ∝ laser_power × dwell_per_pixel × N_passes

Bleaching falls off roughly exponentially with cumulative dose. What matters is not "is this exposure dangerous" but "what is the total dose by the time I'm done."

Live-cell tells

  • Membrane blebbing, retraction, rounding-up over time.
  • Stalled migration that resumes when you stop imaging.
  • Mitotic arrest — cells in mitosis at exposure are exquisitely sensitive.
  • The "looks fine now" trap — bright cells now ≠ alive 2 hours from now.
Practical workflow. Scan a sacrificial corner of the sample first — burn through it at your planned dose to confirm bleaching is acceptable. Favor lower laser + higher detector gain (HV). Never assume the first frame's brightness is sustainable.

Try this

  1. Start at the default dose (laser 10%, dwell 2.0 μs, 80 frames).
  2. Watch the bleach curve. The viability badge tells you whether the cells would survive this dose.
  3. Crank laser power to 50%. Watch viability fall off a cliff.
  4. Drop laser to 5% and double the dwell time. Same total dose? Look at the curve.
  5. For your real experiment, the question is always: what's the minimum dose that gives you usable SNR?
