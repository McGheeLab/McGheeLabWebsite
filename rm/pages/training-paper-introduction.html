1. What the Introduction does

The Introduction has 60 seconds to convert a casual reader into someone who will read the rest of the paper. It has to answer three questions, in this order: why care? (broad context), what's missing? (the gap), and what did we do about it? (your claim and approach).

Reviewers read the Introduction first and decide whether the paper is worth taking seriously. A weak Introduction poisons the reading of everything that follows — the reviewer is now skeptical, looking for problems. A strong Introduction earns the right to take the reader deep.

Write the Introduction last. You only know how to set up the paper once the paper exists. Drafting Introduction prose against half-formed Results is the fastest way to produce three rounds of revisions that you'll throw away.

2. The 3-paragraph funnel

The standard Introduction is three paragraphs. They form a funnel: each one narrower than the last, ending in your specific claim.

¶ 1 — broad biological context (scope level 2–3)
¶ 2 — specific gap / contested literature (scope 4–6)
¶ 3 — your claim + approach (scope 6–8)

Scope levels reference the dial from V3.182 § 7. The funnel is the visible shape of "broad enough to care, narrow enough to focus."

Longer Introductions (4 paragraphs) usually result from splitting the gap into two: one for the broad gap, one for the specific gap the paper addresses. Shorter Introductions (2 paragraphs) work only for short-form papers (Letters, Communications).

3. The opening sentence

The opening sentence is the only sentence in your paper you can be sure every reader will see. Make it count.

What works:

  • A surprising statistic. "One in eight women will develop breast cancer in their lifetime."
  • A specific contradiction. "Substrate stiffness has been reported both to promote and to suppress tumor invasion."
  • A bold claim about the field. "The mechanical landscape of solid tumors remains one of the least-quantified contributors to invasion."

What never works:

  • "Cancer is a major public health concern." — true; everyone has read it 10,000 times.
  • "In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to..." — the dead-on-arrival phrase.
  • "It is well known that..." — if it's well known, your audience already knows it.
  • A definition: "Tumor invasion is the process by which..." — definitions belong inside a paragraph, never opening one.

4. Paragraph 1 — broad context

Establish why the reader should care. Calibrate to your audience — too broad ("cancer kills people") wastes everyone's time; too narrow ("BT-549 spheroid invasion at 4.5 kPa") loses the non-specialists.

The skeleton for ¶1 is Context → Gap → What's-next (V3.182 § 2 skeleton variants), but in this paragraph the "gap" is broad and the "what's-next" foreshadows where the Introduction is heading.

Solid tumors are mechanical objects as much as biological ones — the stiffness, geometry, and composition of the surrounding extracellular matrix shape how cancer cells move through it. Two decades of work have built a picture of mechanotransduction connecting matrix mechanics to gene expression, migration, and invasion. Yet the field cannot agree on a fundamental question: does a stiffer matrix make tumors more or less invasive?

Why it works: opens with a perspective claim, references the field's progress without listing it, ends by previewing the gap. Reader knows what kind of paper this is going to be.

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide. In the United States alone, over 600,000 people die from cancer each year. It is well known that cancer can metastasize from a primary tumor to distant organs. This process involves multiple steps and is influenced by many factors. In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the role of mechanical properties of the tumor microenvironment.

Why it doesn't: opens with a statistic the reader has read 10,000 times, lists known facts at textbook level, never gets specific, ends without foreshadowing anything. Six sentences of throat-clearing.

5. Paragraph 2 — the contested gap

This is the most important paragraph of the Introduction. It does three jobs:

  1. Names the gap — what's contested, missing, or unresolved in the literature.
  2. Cites the contradicting work fairly — both sides if there are sides.
  3. Sets up why your contribution is the right one to resolve it.

If you can't write a strong ¶2, you don't yet have a paper — you have a project. The paper exists when the gap is sharp enough to defend in 4–6 sentences.

Reports disagree on the direction of the effect. In some systems — including 2-D culture and select 3-D collagen assays — stiffer matrices promote invasion (Paszek 2005, Levental 2009). In others — including patient-derived spheroid models and softer hydrogels — stiffer matrices suppress invasion (Han 2016, McGhee 2022). No framework reconciles the two sets of observations. The most common assumption — that the disagreement reflects different cell types or matrix chemistries — has not been tested against an obvious alternative: that an unaccounted variable in the experimental design itself determines the sign of the effect.

Why it works: names both sides, cites real papers, identifies the dominant assumption explicitly, hints at the alternative without revealing the answer. Reader is now leaning forward.

Some studies have suggested that stiffness affects invasion. Other studies have shown different results. There is some controversy in the field about this. More work is needed to understand the role of mechanical properties.

Why it doesn't: no citations, no specific findings, no naming of the conflict. "More work is needed" is the universally hated final phrase. The reader has no reason to care which side is right.

6. Paragraph 3 — your claim and approach

The last paragraph of the Introduction is the paper's elevator pitch. Most readers stop here and skip to the figures; many will never read past it. So write it like it has to stand alone.

It contains three sentences worth of content:

  1. The hypothesis or framing — what you reasoned, why you suspected the answer.
  2. The approach — what you actually did, in one sentence.
  3. The headline finding — what you show, also in one sentence.

The capstone widget in § 9 builds this paragraph for you.

"Here we show..." is the canonical phrase that introduces sentence 3 of ¶3. Use it. Reviewers and readers both look for it.

7. Voice + scope

The Introduction is written entirely in Storyteller voice (V3.182 § 4). Even ¶3, even the "Here we show" sentence — these are not Reporter moves. Reporter voice belongs in Results.

Scope funnels from level 2–3 in ¶1 down to level 7–8 in ¶3's headline finding. That funnel is the shape of the Introduction. If your scope chart looks like a flat line instead of a funnel, your Introduction is broken — either the opening is too narrow or the closing is too broad.

8. Common Introduction failure modes

  • The Methods invasion. Three paragraphs of how cells were cultured and imaged. Move it all to Methods. The Introduction is for context, not reagent lists.
  • The literature review. Paragraph after paragraph summarizing 50 prior papers without a thesis. The Introduction is not a Discussion section, and it is not a review article.
  • The buried claim. "Here we show..." appears nowhere; the reader has to infer the paper's contribution. Make ¶3 explicit.
  • The bait-and-switch. ¶1 promises a paper about cancer; the paper is about a software tool. Mismatched scope between Intro and Results.
  • The "more work is needed" exit. Discussion-section phrasing in the Introduction. Replace with a sharp framing of what your paper does.
  • Defining what the reader already knows. "Cell migration is the directed movement of cells in response to..." — your audience knows. Cut.
