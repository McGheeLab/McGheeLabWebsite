Microscope Basics

Resolution, NA, Köhler, contrast mechanisms — the optical concepts you need before you touch a specific scope. Capstone (Tab 5) is your sign-off sheet for first-time Nikon AX-R confocal access.

1. Resolution & the diffraction limit

Imaging a packed tumor spheroid stained with DAPI on the AX-R, you'll often need to know how close two nuclei can be before they merge into one blob. That number is set by physics — diffraction — not by how good your camera is.

Abbe's diffraction limit

The smallest resolvable separation between two point sources is

d = λ / (2 · NA)

where λ is the emission wavelength and NA is the numerical aperture of the objective. Smaller d means finer detail — you want short λ and high NA.

The Rayleigh criterion

Two point sources are resolvable when the center of one Airy disk falls on the first dark ring of the other. The interactive below renders two Airy patterns at variable separation so you can see the canonical "just-resolved" → "merged blob" transition.

Worked example. Imaging GFP (λ ≈ 510 nm) with a 60×/1.4 oil objective gives d ≈ 510 / (2 · 1.4) ≈ 182 nm. Two nuclei closer than ~200 nm will appear as one — no matter how much you zoom your camera in.

Why this matters for confocal

Pixel size on the camera and zoom-in in software do not fix this — the limit is set before light ever reaches the sensor. Choose objective + λ at acquisition time. By the time you're processing in Fiji or Cell Tracker, the resolution is locked in.

2. Numerical aperture, magnification, working distance

Every objective is a compromise between four properties: resolution (driven by NA), light gathering (also NA), depth of field (inverse NA²), and working distance (how far above the sample the front lens sits). You cannot have them all.

Numerical aperture

NA = n · sin(θ)

n = refractive index of the immersion medium (air 1.00, water 1.33, glycerol 1.47, oil 1.515), θ = half-angle of the cone of light the objective can capture. Air objectives cap at NA ≈ 0.95; water reaches ~1.2; oil reaches ~1.5.

How NA flows into everything else

  • Resolution — Abbe (Tab 1): d ≈ λ/(2·NA). Doubling NA halves d.
  • Light-gathering — scales as NA². A NA 1.4 objective collects about 3.5× the light of an NA 0.75.
  • Depth of field — DOF ≈ (λ·n)/NA² + (n·e)/(M·NA) for a coarse rule. The NA² in the denominator means high-NA objectives produce paper-thin optical sections.
  • Working distance — generally inverse to NA. The 60×/1.4 oil has WD ~0.13 mm; the 4×/0.13 has WD ~17 mm.
Worked example. Imaging a 200 μm spheroid: you want to reach the equator. A 60×/1.4 oil objective has WD ~0.13 mm = 130 μm — you literally cannot reach the equator. The 25×/1.1 water objective on the AX-R (WD ~2 mm) does the job. NA dropped a bit, but you actually get to the data.

3. Köhler illumination

A uniform image field — no hot spots, no shadow of the lamp filament — requires that the lamp be defocused at the sample plane and focused at the back focal plane of the objective. This is Köhler illumination. Every well-aligned transmitted-light microscope in the lab is set up this way. Mis-Köhler is the #1 cause of "my brightfield images look weird" tickets.

The two diaphragms

  • Field diaphragm — conjugate to the sample plane. Closing it shrinks the illuminated area. Used to set how much of the field is lit.
  • Aperture diaphragm — conjugate to the back focal plane. Closing it shrinks the illumination cone. Used to trade contrast vs. resolution.

The alignment procedure

  1. Put a sample in focus with a 10× objective.
  2. Close the field diaphragm until you see its edges in the field of view.
  3. Adjust the condenser height until the edges are sharp.
  4. Re-center the field diaphragm with the condenser centering screws.
  5. Open the field diaphragm until it just disappears from view.
  6. Set the aperture diaphragm to ~80% of the back focal plane (look at the BFP via a Bertrand lens or by removing an eyepiece).
Quick gut check. If you can see the lamp filament as a shadow in your image, you are not Köhler-aligned — the lamp is focused at the sample plane instead of defocused. Adjust the lamp collector or, on the Ti2-U LED engines, swap in the diffuser.

4. Contrast mechanisms

"I can't see anything" usually means you chose the wrong contrast mechanism, not that there's nothing there. Different mechanisms are sensitive to different things and cost you different amounts of light, time, and sample-prep effort.

The four you'll use in this lab

  • Brightfield (BF) — transmitted white light, sensitive to absorption. Unstained cells: nearly invisible. Stained tissue sections: excellent.
  • Phase contrast — converts optical-path-length differences into amplitude. Live unstained cells become visible. Halo artifact around edges.
  • DIC (Differential Interference Contrast) — gradient of optical path length, looks pseudo-3D. Live cells, label-free thickness mapping. Polarizers + Wollaston prisms — fiddly to set up.
  • Fluorescence — only labeled molecules emit. High specificity, dark background. Costs you photons (= photobleach + phototoxicity).
When to choose what. Live-cell time-lapse over hours, no labels → DIC or phase. Fixed cells, one specific marker → fluorescence. Live cells with a marker AND you want morphology → fluorescence + phase overlay. Tissue section H&E stain → brightfield.

The cost

Fluorescence is rarely "free" — every photon you collect requires tens to hundreds of photons of excitation, and that excitation bleaches the dye and can damage live cells. Phase / DIC are "transmitted light" — basically no photodamage. If you can answer the question without fluorescence, do that.
