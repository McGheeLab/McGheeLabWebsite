Mechanical Indentation of Hydrogels

Module 3 — Ball-on-Gel: Contact, Fields & Relaxation · BME/AME 466·566 (Part II–III)

← Continuing from the TFM modules. There, you tracked beads to recover the unknown tractions a cell applies — an ill-posed inverse problem. Here we flip it around: we apply a known macroscopic load (a steel ball of known weight and size), run the same machinery (3D bead tracking, displacement & strain fields, a contact model), and read out the gel's own properties \(E\), \(\nu\), and its relaxation behavior.

Background: one ball, four independent measurements

A rigid steel ball is set on a soft hydrogel that contains fluorescent tracer beads and sits under a water layer. From this single experiment we capture four quantities:

  • The load \(F\) — the ball's buoyancy-corrected weight (known a priori).
  • The contact radius \(a\) — measured directly, because a high-molecular-weight fluorescent polymer in the water is size-excluded from the gel and squeezed out of the contact, leaving a dark projected disk.
  • The indentation \(\delta\) and the full 3D displacement field \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\) — from particle-tracking the beads before vs. after the ball is placed.

The Hertz contact model needs only two of these to give \(E\). Measuring all four makes the experiment over-determined — we can cross-check the model and detect when its assumptions break (Tab 7). That redundancy is the whole point.

$$\underbrace{F}_{\text{known}} \;\;+\;\; \underbrace{a,\ \delta,\ \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{measured}} \;\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{contact model}\;}\;\; E,\ \nu,\ \tau_{\text{relax}}$$

  • Indentation is a forward, over-determined measurement — the opposite of TFM's under-determined inverse.
  • The fluorescent-exclusion trick gives the contact area \(a\) independently of the displacement field.
  • Watching the response in time separates viscoelastic from poroelastic gels (Tab 8).
  1. Toggle Ball off → Ball on and watch the beads displace and the contact disk darken.
  2. Increase the ball diameter: the load grows as \(d^3\), but notice the contact radius and indentation grow much more slowly.
  3. Soften the gel (lower \(E\)): the same ball sinks deeper and spreads a wider contact.

Background: the load is the ball's weight — minus buoyancy

Because the ball rests on the gel under water, the force it presses with is its weight reduced by the buoyant force of the displaced fluid. For a sphere of radius \(R\):

$$F = \left(\rho_{\text{steel}}-\rho_{\text{fluid}}\right)\,\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}\,g$$

Where it comes from: a free body in equilibrium

The resting ball is in static equilibrium under three vertical forces: its weight \(W=\rho_{\text{steel}}Vg\) (down), the buoyant force \(F_b=\rho_{\text{fluid}}Vg\) (up), and the gel's contact reaction \(N\) (up). Balancing them and applying Newton's third law, the ball presses on the gel with exactly

$$N = W - F_b = (\rho_{\text{steel}}-\rho_{\text{fluid}})\,V g,\qquad V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3$$

Buoyancy is nothing more than the net of hydrostatic pressure \(p=\rho_{\text{fluid}}\,g\,h\) acting over the ball's surface — the fluid pushes up on the deeper bottom harder than it pushes down on the top, and for a fully submerged body that resultant is exactly \(\rho_{\text{fluid}}Vg\) (Archimedes), independent of depth.

Why "fully submerged" is precisely the point

Submersion is the condition that makes the correction matter, not one that removes it. In air the displaced fluid is air (\(\rho\approx1.2\) kg/m³), so the correction is \(1.2/7850\approx0.015\%\) — ignore it. Fully submerged in water (\(\rho\approx1000\) kg/m³) it is \(1000/7850\approx\) 13% of the weight. And because a steel ball cannot float, it always displaces its entire volume \(V\), so the 13% is fixed no matter how deep the dish sits. Since Hertz gives \(E^\ast=3FR/4a^3\) — linear in \(F\) — skipping the correction is a direct ~13% systematic error in every modulus you report.

Two subtleties. (1) The contact patch is dye-free (no fluid under it), so Archimedes is slightly violated — but the un-wetted fraction is \(\sim(a/R)^2\approx1\%\) of an already-13% term, i.e. ~0.1% of the load: negligible. (2) The water column presses on the gel the same before and after the ball is placed, so that uniform hydrostatic field cancels in the differential displacement we track — only the ball's net contact load deforms the gel. (Bonus: staying fully submerged also avoids the air–water meniscus line force a partly-emerged ball would add.)

A \(d=1\) mm steel ball, submerged: \(R=0.5\,\text{mm}\), \(V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3\approx 5.24\times10^{-10}\,\text{m}^3\), \(\Delta\rho\approx 6850\) kg/m³, so \(F\approx 6850\times 5.24\times10^{-10}\times 9.81 \approx 3.5\times10^{-5}\,\text{N} = 35\ \mu\text{N}\). The dry weight would be ~40 µN — the 5 µN difference is buoyancy.

  • The load on the gel is the net force on the ball: \(N=W-F_b=(\rho_{\text{steel}}-\rho_{\text{fluid}})Vg\).
  • Buoyancy = the resultant of hydrostatic pressure; for a fully submerged body it equals \(\rho_{\text{fluid}}Vg\), depth-independent.
  • Full submersion is what makes the correction ~13% (vs ~0.015% in air) — and \(E^\ast\propto F\), so ignoring it biases \(E\) by ~13%.
  • Load scales as \(d^3\): doubling the diameter gives 8× the force.
  1. Compare In air vs Submerged — read how much force buoyancy removes.
  2. Find the diameter that gives a target load of ~50 µN on the F–diameter curve.
  3. Try a less-dense ball (e.g. glass ~2500 kg/m³): how much does the net force drop?

Background: the Hertz sphere-on-flat contact

Near contact, a sphere's surface is a paraboloid: the gap is \(h(r)\approx r^2/2R\). Pressing it into an elastic half-space produces a circular contact of radius \(a\) carrying the hemispherical Hertz pressure

$$p(r)=p_0\sqrt{1-r^2/a^2},\qquad p_0=\frac{3F}{2\pi a^2}=\tfrac{3}{2}\,p_{\text{mean}}$$

The three relations that tie load, contact size, and indentation together are

$$a^3=\frac{3FR^\ast}{4E^\ast},\qquad \delta=\frac{a^2}{R^\ast},\qquad F=\tfrac{4}{3}E^\ast\sqrt{R^\ast}\,\delta^{3/2}$$

Learn it: sphere-on-flat vs sphere-on-sphere — one formula, two reductions

Hertz is more general than "a ball on a flat." Any smooth contact between two bodies maps onto an equivalent sphere-on-flat problem through two combined quantities — the reduced modulus \(E^\ast\) and the reduced radius \(R^\ast\). The relations above then hold unchanged; only how you build \(E^\ast,R^\ast\) from the two bodies (radii \(R_1,R_2\); moduli \(E_1,E_2\); Poisson \(\nu_1,\nu_2\)) differs:

$$\frac{1}{E^\ast}=\frac{1-\nu_1^2}{E_1}+\frac{1-\nu_2^2}{E_2},\qquad \frac{1}{R^\ast}=\frac{1}{R_1}+\frac{1}{R_2}$$

\(R^\ast\) is geometry — curvatures add. The relative gap near contact is \(h(r)\approx r^2/2R^\ast\), so \(R^\ast\) is the radius of the single equivalent sphere. A flat has \(R_2=\infty\) (\(1/R_2=0\)); two convex spheres add curvatures, so the gap closes faster (smaller \(R^\ast\)); a ball seated in a concave socket subtracts (\(R_2<0\), larger \(R^\ast\)).

\(E^\ast\) is compliance — they add in series. Both bodies deform and the softer one dominates. A rigid body (\(E\to\infty\)) contributes nothing, so \(E^\ast\) is set by the soft body alone.

Steel on a kPa gel is the first row: \(E_\text{steel}\approx200\) GPa \(\gg E_\text{gel}\), so \((1-\nu_\text{steel}^2)/E_\text{steel}\to0\) and only the gel's compliance survives \(\Rightarrow E^\ast=E_\text{gel}/(1-\nu^2)\); the flat gel has \(R_2=\infty\Rightarrow R^\ast=R_\text{ball}\).

What the toggle below shows (and the real trap). Two equal soft spheres is a genuinely different contact from a rigid ball on a soft flat. For the same \(R,E,F\) the contact radius \(a\) and peak pressure \(p_0\) come out identical (both \(E^\ast\) and \(R^\ast\) carry the same factor of 2, and \(a\propto (R^\ast/E^\ast)^{1/3}\)), but the mutual approach \(\delta=a^2/R^\ast\) doubles — because both spheres deform, not just one — and \(E^\ast\) is halved. The 2× error in an extracted modulus appears when you mismatch conventions: e.g. borrow the grain code's \(E^\ast=E/[2(1-\nu^2)]\) while keeping your flat-geometry \(R^\ast=R\). Always pair \(E^\ast\) and \(R^\ast\) from the same contact.
  • Hertz assumes small strain (\(a/R\ll1\)), frictionless, linear-elastic, half-space, no adhesion.
  • For a rigid sphere on a soft gel: \(E^\ast=E/(1-\nu^2)\), \(R^\ast=R\) — no factor of 2.
  • The geometry identity \(\delta=a^2/R\) holds with no modulus and no force — a free model check.
  1. Confirm the geometry check: read \(a\) and \(\delta\) and verify \(a^2\approx R\,\delta\) as you move every slider.
  2. Flip the convention toggle to the GELLS-DEM sphere-on-sphere form: \(a\) and \(p_0\) stay put, but \(E^\ast\) halves and the approach \(\delta\) doubles. Explain why (both spheres deform), then say why our steel-on-gel experiment is the rigid-on-soft case.
  3. Raise the load until \(a/R\) exceeds ~0.3: the small-strain assumption is now violated (see Tab 9).

Background: the stress lives below the surface

The contact pressure \(p(r)\) is a surface boundary condition. Superposing the Boussinesq point-load solution (the same Green's function used in TFM) over the contact disk gives the full sub-surface field. The vertical stress forms a compressive "bulb" that spreads and decays with depth:

$$\sigma_{zz}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{3z^3}{2\pi}\int_{\text{disk}}\frac{p(r')\,dA'}{\rho^5},\qquad \rho=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|$$

The famous result: the maximum shear stress is not at the surface but on the axis a little below it. Along the load axis (\(\zeta=z/a\)),

$$\frac{\sigma_z}{p_0}=-\frac{1}{1+\zeta^2},\quad \frac{\sigma_r}{p_0}=-\Big[(1+\nu)\big(1-\zeta\arctan\tfrac1\zeta\big)-\tfrac12\tfrac1{1+\zeta^2}\Big],\quad \tau_1=\tfrac12|\sigma_z-\sigma_r|$$

For \(\nu=0.3\), \(\tau_1\) peaks at \(z\approx0.48\,a\) with \(\tau_{\max}\approx0.31\,p_0\); the peak moves deeper (toward ~0.55 a) as \(\nu\to0.5\). This is why yielding, cracking, and cavitation in soft contacts often initiate below the surface.

  • The sub-surface field is the Hertz pressure convolved with the elastic Green's function.
  • Peak shear is sub-surface (~0.48 a for ν≈0.3) — damage starts where you cannot see it.
  • Higher ν (toward incompressible) pushes the shear maximum deeper.
  1. Read the depth of the max-shear marker on the right-hand curve, then find it on the stress bulb at left.
  2. Raise ν toward 0.5 and watch the shear peak move deeper.
  3. Switch the bulb between σ_zz and the on-axis shear to see how the "danger zone" sits beneath the contact.

Background: reading the contact area with a dye that cannot get in

To measure \(a\) directly, the water phase is loaded with a high-molecular-weight fluorescent polymer (e.g. large dextran). Its hydrodynamic radius \(R_h\) is bigger than the gel mesh size \(\xi\), so it is size-excluded from the gel — the partition coefficient \(K\to0\):

$$K \approx \exp\!\big[-(R_h/\xi)^2\big]\;\xrightarrow{R_h\gg\xi}\;0$$

The probe stays in the water and is squeezed out of the ball–gel contact. In a confocal section at the contact plane the contact appears as a dark disk on a bright field; its projected radius is \(a\). (This is the molecular-probe cousin of particle exclusion microscopy, Schulze et al. 2016, where colloidal particles are excluded from a soft contact; Johannes et al. 2019 image the same contact in 3D with fluorescent beads and confocal sectioning.) Three things bias the measured \(a\):

  • Optical resolution (PSF): the edge is blurred over ~the PSF width, so the apparent radius depends on your intensity threshold.
  • Meniscus: a thin fluid wedge just outside contact reads slightly dark, inflating \(a\).
  • Photobleaching: long dwell dims the surrounding fluid, mimicking exclusion.
  • Exclusion gives \(a\) without any mechanical model — the independent leg of the over-determined measurement.
  • Pick a consistent threshold (e.g. half-max) and report it; the absolute edge is fuzzy at the PSF scale.
  • Meniscus and bleaching both push the measured \(a\) up, biasing \(E\) downward.
  1. In Ideal, sweep the threshold and find the value that recovers the true \(a\) (dashed ring).
  2. Turn on Meniscus and Photobleach and watch the measured \(a\) over-shoot the truth.
  3. Increase the PSF width (worse optics): the edge softens and the threshold matters more.

Background: beads turn deformation into a displacement field

Fluorescent beads embedded in the gel are imaged before and after the ball is placed. The lab's SerialTrack algorithm matches each bead between the two 3D stacks (topology-based matching inside an ADMM loop) and returns the displacement field \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\). Strain is its spatial gradient:

$$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\tfrac12(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\top})\quad\text{(small strain)},\qquad \mathbf{F}=\mathbf{I}+\nabla\mathbf{u}\quad\text{(finite)}$$

For a Hertz contact, the surface normal displacement has a closed form we can compare the data against directly:

$$u_z(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\pi p_0}{4E^\ast a}\,(2a^2-r^2), & r\le a\\[6pt]\dfrac{p_0}{2E^\ast a}\big[(2a^2-r^2)\arcsin\tfrac{a}{r}+a\sqrt{r^2-a^2}\big], & r\ge a\end{cases}$$

with \(u_z(0)=\delta\). The measurement quality is set by bead density (how finely the field is sampled — the steep gradient near the contact edge needs dense seeding) and localization precision (axial \(z\) is always worse than lateral, exactly as in the TFM confocal tab).

→ See TFM Module: the bead-size/density and confocal-vs-epifluorescence trade-offs you learned there apply identically to the tracer beads here.
  • Tracking gives \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\); strain and stress follow by differentiation and the constitutive law.
  • Comparing the measured \(u_z(r)\) to the analytic Hertz curve validates that the gel really is a Hertzian half-space.
  • Sparse or noisy beads wash out the sharp near-edge field — the most information-rich region.
  1. In Dense, the bead samples (dots) trace the analytic \(u_z(r)\) curve closely.
  2. Switch to Sparse and watch the steep near-edge displacement get undersampled.
  3. Add Noise and see the reconstruction RMSE rise — especially where the field is steep.

Background: a well-posed inverse — and why redundancy matters

Unlike TFM's ill-posed deconvolution, fitting a contact model is a low-dimensional, over-determined regression. From the four measurements we get three independent estimates of \(E^\ast\):

$$\text{(i)}\;E^\ast=\frac{3FR}{4a^3}\quad\text{(ii)}\;E^\ast=\frac{3F}{4\sqrt{R}\,\delta^{3/2}}\quad\text{(iii)}\;E^\ast=\arg\min_{E^\ast}\lVert u_z^{\text{model}}-u_z^{\text{meas}}\rVert^2$$

If all three agree, every Hertz assumption is validated at once. If they disagree, the pattern localizes the broken assumption (e.g. adhesion inflates \(a\), so (i) drops below (ii)).

Recovering ν needs the full field

The force-based routes only ever constrain the combination \(E^\ast=E/(1-\nu^2)\) — \(E\) and \(\nu\) are degenerate. The misfit landscape has a flat valley along \(E/(1-\nu^2)=\text{const}\). Only the shape of the 3D field (lateral/radial displacement, depth decay) breaks that degeneracy and pins \(\nu\) separately.

  • Three independent \(E^\ast\) estimates that agree = a validated measurement; disagreement = a diagnostic.
  • Vertical surface data fixes \(E^\ast\); only full 3D field shape recovers \(\nu\).
  • This is the over-determination payoff promised in Tab 1.
  1. With low noise, confirm the three \(E^\ast\) bars line up (green) — a consistent, trustworthy fit.
  2. Raise the noise and watch the bars scatter; the agreement check turns amber/red.
  3. Toggle Field-only → +Lateral data and watch the misfit valley collapse to a single minimum that pins \(\nu\).

Background: why the force keeps changing after you stop pushing

Hold the indentation fixed and the force decays. Two physically distinct mechanisms can cause this, and the experiment can tell them apart:

Viscoelastic — intrinsic, size-independent

Polymer chains and crosslinks rearrange; internal friction dissipates energy. A Standard Linear Solid gives

$$F(t)=F_\infty+(F_0-F_\infty)\,e^{-t/\tau_v},\qquad \tau_v\ \text{is a material constant — independent of contact size }a$$

Poroelastic — solvent must flow out

The compressed contact raises pore pressure; solvent has to drain out of the contact zone through the mesh. The relaxation is diffusion over the contact length scale:

$$\tau_p \sim \frac{a^2}{D},\qquad D=\frac{2(1-\nu)}{1-2\nu}\,\frac{G\,k}{\eta}\ \ (G\ \text{shear modulus},\ k\sim\xi^2\ \text{permeability},\ \eta\ \text{solvent viscosity})$$

Hu et al. (2010) showed that the force relaxation collapses onto a single master curve when plotted against the normalized time \(\hat t = Dt/a^2\): \(\;[F(t)-F_\infty]/[F_0-F_\infty]=g(Dt/a^2)\), with \(F_0/F_\infty=2(1-\nu)\) (undrained vs. drained). The diagnostic: repeat with different ball sizes (different \(a\)) and plot \(\tau\) vs \(a^2\): a flat line means viscoelastic (intrinsic); a line through the origin with slope \(1/D\) means poroelastic. This ties the instantaneous/equilibrium moduli to the undrained (\(\nu\to0.5\)) / drained limits.

  • Viscoelastic τ is size-independent; poroelastic τ scales as \(a^2\).
  • Vary the ball size to separate the two — a single curve cannot.
  • Poroelastic drainage links the time response to the mesh size and permeability.
  1. In Viscoelastic, set two different ball sizes — the normalized relaxation curves overlap.
  2. Switch to Poroelastic — now the larger ball relaxes slower, and the τ-vs-a² plot becomes a straight line.
  3. From the poroelastic slope, read off the diffusivity \(D\).

Background: knowing when to stop trusting Hertz

Hertz is the small-strain, frictionless, half-space, no-adhesion, linear-elastic limit. Real soft-gel indentation drifts out of it in five common ways, each with a correction:

  • Finite thickness (\(a/h\gtrsim0.1\)): a gel film stiffens (bonded) or softens (free). Use the Dimitriadis et al. (2002) polynomial correction in \(\chi=\sqrt{R\delta}/h\), with separate coefficients for bonded vs. unbonded.
  • Adhesion: a sticky soft contact follows JKR — the contact persists under tension and pulls off at \(F_{\text{po}}=\tfrac32\pi W R^\ast\). The Tabor parameter \(\mu_T\gg1\) (soft gels) selects JKR over DMT.
  • Large strain (\(a/R\gtrsim0.3\)): the parabolic gap and linear elasticity both fail — go neo-Hookean (the law from the TFM module).
  • Elastocapillarity: for very soft gels, solid surface tension \(\gamma\) rounds the contact when \(a\lesssim\gamma/E\).
  • Drainage state: fast loading reads the undrained modulus (\(\nu\to0.5\)), slow loading the drained one — there are two valid Hertz moduli (Tab 8).

$$\text{JKR:}\quad \delta=\frac{a^2}{R^\ast}-\sqrt{\frac{2\pi W a}{E^\ast}},\qquad F=\frac{4E^\ast a^3}{3R^\ast}-\sqrt{8\pi W E^\ast a^3}$$

  • The geometry check \(a^2=R\delta\) from Tab 3 is your first alarm: adhesion or thickness breaks it.
  • Walk the dimensionless checks — \(a/R\), \(a/h\), \(\mu_T\), \(a\) vs \(\gamma/E\), rate vs τ — before defaulting to Hertz.
  • Disagreement among the three \(E^\ast\) estimates (Tab 7) tells you which correction you need.
  1. Increase δ/R and watch the neo-Hookean and Hertz curves diverge (large strain).
  2. Lower the thickness h until the bonded thin-film curve stiffens well above Hertz.
  3. Raise the adhesion W and see the JKR curve develop a tensile (pull-off) tail; watch the decision flowchart light up the active regime.
