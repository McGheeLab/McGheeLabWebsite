Background: where does a "spring" come from?

You already read force–displacement plots for springs. But a spring is not fundamental — it is what any chemical bond looks like when you barely disturb it. Take two atoms a distance \(r\) apart. Their interaction energy is well described by the Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential: a steep short-range repulsion (electron clouds cannot overlap, the \(r^{-12}\) term) plus a softer long-range van der Waals attraction (London dispersion, the \(r^{-6}\) term).

$$U(r) = 4\varepsilon\!\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right]$$

\(\varepsilon\) is the well depth (bond energy scale) and \(\sigma\) is the distance where \(U=0\).

From energy to force, and the spring

The force is minus the slope, \(F(r) = -\,dU/dr\). It vanishes at the energy minimum, the equilibrium bond length \(r_0 = 2^{1/6}\sigma\approx1.122\sigma\), where \(U(r_0)=-\varepsilon\). Taylor-expand about \(r_0\): the slope is zero there, so the leading term is parabolic and the restoring force is linear:

$$F(r) \approx -\,U''(r_0)\,(r-r_0) = -k\,(r-r_0),\qquad k = U''(r_0)=\frac{72\varepsilon}{r_0^{2}}$$

That is Hooke's law, with the spring constant being the curvature of the well. It is not special to LJ — every smooth bond is parabolic near its minimum, so small displacements always give a linear restoring force, and macroscopic small-strain elasticity is the sum of these atomic springs.

The LJ well is asymmetric — steep in compression, soft in tension. Pull the bond and the true force falls below the straight Hooke line, peaks at the maximum-attraction distance (\(r\approx1.24\sigma\)), then drops toward zero: the bond breaks. A real spring would just keep pulling. That anharmonicity is exactly what Hooke cannot capture.

  • \(F=-dU/dr\); at the minimum the slope is zero, so \(F\approx-k(r-r_0)\) with \(k=72\varepsilon/r_0^2\).
  • Hooke's law is the universal small-displacement limit of any smooth bond, not a property of LJ.
  • The harmonic spring fails at large displacement: the well is anharmonic and the bond has a finite breaking force.
  1. Start at Tiny wiggle: the red dashed parabola sits exactly on the LJ well and the Hooke line on the true force.
  2. Go to Moderate then Large pull and watch the orange (true) and hollow (Hooke) markers separate — read the force error climb.
  3. Change \(\varepsilon\) and \(\sigma\): the well depth and \(r_0\) move, and the spring constant \(k\) rescales.

Background: a hydrogel is a tangle of noodles

Zoom into any hydrogel or tissue and you find the same cartoon: long, noodle-like polymer strands threaded through water. What changes is only (i) how floppy each noodle is — its persistence length \(L_p\), the distance over which a strand stays roughly straight — and (ii) how the noodles are joined — by fixed covalent crosslinks or by transient physical entanglements and branch points. Those two micro-choices set the entire macroscopic character.

Three networks, side by side

Polyacrylamide (PAAM). Thin, flexible chains (\(L_p\sim\)nm ≪ mesh size) joined at fixed covalent junctions by bis-acrylamide → an isotropic random mesh. Each strand is an entropic spring; the network is a linearly elastic swollen rubber whose stiffness is set independently by monomer and crosslinker fractions. Bioinert; the canonical tunable substrate (Tse & Engler 2010).

Collagen-I gel. Thick semiflexible fibrils (\(L_p\sim\) mesh size) in a sparse, branched / entangled network. At low load it is fibril bending (soft); under load the network reorients and pulls fibrils into stretching → dramatic strain-stiffening (Licup et al. 2015).

Extracellular matrix (ECM). The biological generalization: heterogeneous fibrous composite (collagen + fibronectin + elastin + proteoglycans), mixed physical and enzymatic crosslinks, anisotropic and actively remodeled (Frantz et al. 2010; Mouw et al. 2014).

$$G \approx n\,k_B T\ \ \text{(flexible: stiffness}\propto\text{strand density } n);\qquad K(\gamma)=\frac{d\sigma}{d\gamma}\ \text{(collagen/ECM: } K\sim\sigma)$$

A flexible PAAM strand of \(N\) Kuhn segments is a Hookean entropic spring \(k=3k_BT/(Nb^2)\); summed over \(n\) strands the modulus \(G\approx nk_BT\) is constant in strain → a straight stress–strain line. A semiflexible collagen fibril bends cheaply at first, but once taut its tangent modulus \(K\) climbs >100-fold and becomes nearly concentration-independent, scaling as \(K\sim\sigma\) (Licup et al. 2015). Same noodles, opposite signatures — set purely by \(L_p\) and junction type.

  • PAAM: flexible + covalent crosslinks → isotropic mesh → linear, \(G\approx nk_BT\), tunable, bioinert.
  • Collagen: semiflexible + entanglements/branches → bending then stretching → strong strain-stiffening.
  • ECM: heterogeneous multi-protein fibrous network, mixed crosslinks → anisotropic, nonlinear, remodeled.
  1. Switch between PAAM, Collagen, and ECM: watch the junctions change from filled covalent dots to hollow entanglements, and the strands from thin/flexible to thick/semiflexible.
  2. Drag the applied strain γ up: PAAM stays a straight stress line; collagen/ECM peel away from the dashed linear reference (strain-stiffening).
  3. Change persistence length and crosslink density and read how the tangent modulus K responds.

Background: the entropic spring

A single hydrogel chain (a "noodle string") is bombarded by solvent and constantly jiggles with thermal energy \(k_BT\). Left alone it samples a huge number of crumpled shapes; far more of them have the two ends close together than far apart, so the coil is "entropically happy" balled up.

Grab the two ends and pull. You are not stretching any chemical bond (the contour length is unchanged) — you are forcing the chain into rare, aligned shapes, reducing its conformational entropy \(S\). Since \(F=U-TS\) and the internal energy \(U\) barely changes, the chain pushes back with a purely entropic restoring force \(f=-T\,dS/dx\).

Why warmer pulls harder

Because \(f\propto -T\,dS/dx\), the force scales linearly with absolute temperature: hotter chains fluctuate more, resist alignment more, and pull back harder. (A stretched rubber band actually contracts when heated — the Gough–Joule effect.) This is the opposite of an energetic bond spring, which softens when warm.

FJC Gaussian / low-force limit — a true Hookean spring:

$$ f = \frac{3 k_B T}{N b^2}\,x,\qquad k_{\text{spring}}=\frac{3 k_B T}{N b^2}=\frac{3k_BT}{\langle R^2\rangle_0},\quad b=2L_p $$

WLC (Marko–Siggia) for semiflexible filaments (collagen, dsDNA):

$$ f = \frac{k_B T}{L_p}\!\left[\frac{1}{4(1-x/L)^2}-\frac14+\frac{x}{L}\right] $$

Low force \(f\approx\frac{3k_BT}{2L_p}\frac{x}{L}\) (linear); near full extension the force diverges as \(1/(1-x/L)^2\).

dsDNA-like: \(L_p=50\) nm, \(L=1000\) nm, \(k_BT\approx4.1\) pN·nm. The WLC force scale is \(k_BT/L_p\approx0.08\) pN. At 10% extension the bracket \(\approx0.16\) so \(f\approx0.013\) pN; at 90% the first term explodes (bracket \(\approx25.6\)) so \(f\approx2.1\) pN — a ~160× stiffening from the same thermal spring.

  • A chain is an entropic spring: \(f=-T\,dS/dx\), from lost conformational entropy, not stretched bonds.
  • At small extension it is Hookean, \(k=3k_BT/(Nb^2)\): longer chains are softer, and the spring is stiffer when hotter.
  • The WLC adds finite extensibility: force diverges as \(x\to L\).
  • Summed over a network this gives the rubber modulus \(G\approx\nu k_BT\), also rising with temperature.
  1. Watch the chain jiggle (it animates). In Relaxed coil it crumples and wiggles freely; in Near full extension it pulls taut and the force arrow grows.
  2. Compare the WLC curve (solid) to the FJC Hookean line (dashed): they overlap at small x/L and split as you stretch.
  3. Hit Heat it up vs Freeze it: both force curves rise/fall with temperature — the entropic-spring signature.

Background: every network is just springs and dashpots

Before any constitutive law, see the mechanical elements inside the tangle. Two questions decide what each strand is: are both ends pinned to permanent crosslinks? and does its motion cost frictional energy?

A strand fixed at both ends between two crosslinks is an entropic spring: pulling it lowers entropy, \(f=-T\,dS/dx\), and for a Gaussian chain this is Hookean, \(k=3k_BT/(Nb^2)\). It stores energy reversibly, so it carries load forever and sets the equilibrium modulus.

Anything whose stress relaxes by frictional sliding is a dashpot: (1) physical entanglements, where strands hook and only temporarily constrain each other (they slip past, dragging through neighbors); and (2) dangling ends and free chains, attached at one end (or none) so they bear no equilibrium load but still drag through the mesh. Both convert work to heat — the molecular origin of viscosity \(\eta\).

$$\underbrace{f=\frac{3k_BT}{Nb^2}x}_{\text{strand = spring}}\qquad\qquad \underbrace{\sigma=\eta\,\dot{\varepsilon}}_{\text{entanglement / end = dashpot}}\qquad G_{\text{eq}}=\nu\,k_BT$$

Of 100 strands, suppose 60 are pinned at both ends, 25 entangled-only, 15 dangling. Only the 60 bridging strands set the long-time stiffness: \(G_{\text{eq}}=\nu k_BT\) uses \(\nu\propto60\), not 100. The other 40 add transient resistance (loss, a viscous \(\eta\)) that decays away — they make the gel feel stiffer when poked fast, then flow out of the load path. Convert a dangling end into a second crosslink and it "promotes" to a spring: \(\nu\) and \(G_{\text{eq}}\) rise.

  • Spring = strand pinned at both ends. Entropic, reversible, sets \(G_{\text{eq}}=\nu k_BT\).
  • Dashpot = entanglement or dangling/free end. Temporary constraint or load-free drag → viscosity \(\eta\).
  • Only elastically effective strands enter \(\nu\); dangling ends are invisible to equilibrium stiffness but visible to loss/relaxation.
  • This bookkeeping bridges microstructure to the lumped viscoelastic models (next tab).
  1. Use Highlight springs / Highlight dashpots to isolate the load-bearing strands vs the dissipative entanglements and dangling ends.
  2. Classify color-codes everything: green springs, amber entanglements, red dangling ends.
  3. Add strands (more springs → higher elastic fraction and G_eq) or add entanglements/ends (more dashpots → more viscosity, same G_eq).

Background: averaging the mesh into one element

The previous tabs zoomed all the way in: each strand is an entropic spring \(k=3k_BT/(Nb^2)\), and wherever stress leaks away by frictional sliding there is a dashpot. A real hydrogel has millions of these, all different. We never solve each one — we lump them into one average spring + dashpot element whose stress \(\sigma\) and strain \(\varepsilon\) are the volume averages.

  • Springs — strands tied at both ends store free energy reversibly → the equilibrium modulus \(E_\infty\) (\(G=\nu k_BT\)).
  • Dashpots — dangling ends, sticky/transient links, and reptating entangled segments that slide → each carries a relaxation time \(\tau\) and a modulus that decays as \(e^{-t/\tau}\).

$$\text{spring: }\sigma=E\varepsilon\qquad \text{dashpot: }\sigma=\eta\dot{\varepsilon}\qquad (\tau=\eta/E)$$

The lumped model: a Standard Linear Solid

The minimal element with all three features — an instantaneous \(E_0\), a smaller equilibrium \(E_\infty\), and a relaxation time \(\tau\) — is the Standard Linear Solid (SLS / Zener): a lone spring \(E_\infty\) parallel to a Maxwell arm (spring \(E_2\) + dashpot \(\eta\)):

$$E(t)=E_\infty+(E_0-E_\infty)e^{-t/\tau_\sigma},\ \ E_0=E_\infty+E_2,\ \tau_\sigma=\eta/E_2$$

$$J(t)=\frac1{E_\infty}-\Big(\frac1{E_\infty}-\frac1{E_0}\Big)e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon},\ \ \tau_\varepsilon=\tau_\sigma\frac{E_0}{E_\infty}>\tau_\sigma$$

Because the permanent springs never let go, \(E_\infty>0\): a viscoelastic solid (relaxes but does not flow to zero). If every link were transient (\(E_\infty=0\)) we'd get a Maxwell fluid — an uncrosslinked melt. A real network is a spectrum of \(\tau_i\); one SLS is the single-time approximation (exact for a narrow spread).

\(E_\infty=2\), \(E_2=8\) → \(E_0=10\) kPa, \(\eta=24\) kPa·s. Then \(\tau_\sigma=\eta/E_2=3\) s and \(\tau_\varepsilon=\tau_\sigma E_0/E_\infty=15\) s. Hold strain: stress decays from \(E_0=10\) to \(E_\infty=2\) kPa over ~3 s. Hold stress: the gel creeps from \(1/E_0=0.1\) to \(1/E_\infty=0.5\) (1/kPa) over the slower ~15 s.

  • Lumping = volume-averaging millions of micro springs/dashpots into one element.
  • Strands fixed at both ends → springs (\(E_\infty\)); dangling ends, sliding/sticky links → dashpots (\(\tau\)).
  • SLS is the minimal faithful lump: \(E_0\), \(E_\infty>0\) (solid), \(\tau_\sigma=\eta/E_2\), with \(\tau_\varepsilon>\tau_\sigma\).
  • A broad \(\tau_i\) spectrum needs multiple Maxwell arms; one SLS is the single-time approximation.
  1. Scrub Lump progress from 0→1 to watch the micro noodle-soup pour into one SLS schematic.
  2. Toggle Stress relaxation vs Creep; move E₂ and η to shift the relaxation time τσ (marked).
  3. Set E∞→0 (fluid) and watch stress relax to zero; widen the τ-spread and see the single-SLS line deviate from the population mean.

Background: a local pull, felt less and less with distance

A cell grips the hydrogel through integrin receptors that cluster into focal adhesions, physically linking the cell's contractile actomyosin to the matrix and delivering a roughly localized pull (a traction) at the adhesion (Geiger, Spatz & Bershadsky 2009).

Picture the network as a chain of linked noodles anchored there. The strand at the integrin is pulled most; each strand farther down is dragged a little less, because the load spreads through the elastic medium. The displacement decays with distance.

How fast? The Boussinesq 1/r law

For a concentrated normal force \(P\) on a soft linear-elastic half-space, classical elasticity gives the surface displacement at distance \(r\):

$$u_z(r)=\frac{P(1-\nu^2)}{\pi E r}\ \propto\ \frac{1}{r}$$

Double the distance, halve the displacement. It also scales as \(1/E\): a stiffer matrix moves less for the same pull — exactly how cells "feel" rigidity.

A strand 2 µm from the integrin moves \(u_0\). Where does a strand move only \(\tfrac14u_0\)? Since \(u_z\propto1/r\), quarter the displacement = four times the distance, \(r=8\) µm. Stiffen the gel 5→20 kPa at fixed force and every displacement shrinks 4× (\(u_z\propto1/E\)).

  • Integrins/focal adhesions transmit the cell's contractile force as a localized pull.
  • On a linear-elastic substrate, surface displacement decays as \(u_z\propto1/r\).
  • Displacement also scales as \(1/E\): stiffer gels deform less (rigidity sensing).
  • The point-force law is a far-field idealization (singular at \(r=0\), small strain, linear, thick gel); fibrous matrices can carry force farther.
  1. Watch the chain: the arrow at the integrin is longest, and each node down the line displaces less — the 1/r falloff.
  2. Toggle Soft vs Stiff gel: the whole decay curve scales by 1/E.
  3. Move the probe r* and read the ratio u_z(r*)/u_z(2µm); try Compare 1/r vs 1/r².

Background: many integrins → one deformation field

Step back to the scale of a whole cell. A spread cell does not pull at one point — it grips the matrix through many focal adhesions around its footprint, each applying a localized inward traction (Geiger et al. 2009). Two facts make the macroscopic picture tractable on a soft (linear-elastic) gel:

$$u_z(r)=\frac{P(1-\nu^2)}{\pi E r}\ \ \text{(one adhesion, Boussinesq)};\qquad \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\frac{(1-\nu^2)P_a}{\pi E\,|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_a|}\,\hat{\mathbf{P}}_a$$

First, each adhesion's displacement decays as \(1/r\). Second — the key idea — because the gel is linear, the fields simply superpose (add). The macroscopic deformation TFM measures is the vector sum of every adhesion's local contribution (Johnson 1985; Landau & Lifshitz).

One adhesion, \(P=10\) nN, \(E=10\) kPa, \(\nu=0.5\): at \(r=5\,\mu\)m, \(u_z\approx48\) nm; at \(r=10\,\mu\)m, \(\approx24\) nm — exactly half (\(1/r\)). On a stiffer \(E=40\) kPa gel the \(5\,\mu\)m value drops to \(\approx12\) nm. These tens-of-nm displacements over microns are what bead-tracking TFM resolves.

  • A cell pulls through many discrete adhesions, each a localized inward traction.
  • Linear elasticity ⇒ the per-adhesion \(1/r\) fields superpose into the bulk deformation.
  • Displacement scales as \(P/E\): stronger pulls grow the field, stiffer gels shrink it uniformly.
  • A symmetric ring gives net force ≈ 0 yet large stored strain energy — the signature of contractility.
  1. Few, strong adhesions → you see individual 1/r dimples; Many, weak → a smooth inward contraction ring.
  2. Compare Soft gel vs Stiff gel: the deformed (teal) grid relaxes back toward the reference as E rises.
  3. Change the cell radius and the per-adhesion force and watch the whole field scale.

Background: the forward map, read backwards

The cell pulls with a contractile force \(F\) we cannot see — but we can see embedded beads shift toward the adhesion. The question: if we know how stiff the gel is, can the measured displacements tell us the force?

The forward map needs the material

For one concentrated pull \(F\) on a linear-elastic half-space (Boussinesq), the surface displacement at distance \(r\) is

$$u_z(r)=\frac{F(1-\nu^2)}{\pi E r}\,,\qquad u_z\propto\frac1r,\ \ u_z\propto\frac1E.$$

Given \(F\) and the material \((E,\nu)\) this predicts every bead's motion — a unique, smooth field.

Inferring the force: invert the same equation

We know the material and measure a bead's displacement \(u_{\text{meas}}\) at known \(r_{\text{meas}}\). Because the forward law is just multiplication, solve for \(F\):

$$\boxed{\,F_{\text{inferred}}=\dfrac{\pi E\,r_{\text{meas}}}{1-\nu^2}\,u_{\text{meas}}\,}$$

That is the whole idea of Traction Force Microscopy. Two consequences: the inferred force is directly proportional to the assumed \(E\) (guess stiffness 2× high → report force 2× high); and dividing by a tiny far-field displacement amplifies noise — the first whiff of an ill-posed inverse.

On \(E=10\) kPa, \(\nu=0.45\): a bead moves \(0.81\,\mu\)m at \(r=5\,\mu\)m → \(F=\pi(10\,000)(5\times10^{-6})(0.81\times10^{-6})/(1-0.45^2)\approx160\) nN — right in the measured range. If the gel were actually 20 kPa, the same beads imply 320 nN. The displacements never changed; only the assumed material did.

  • Forward (force→displacement) needs the material; it is unique and smooth.
  • Inferring force = inverting that law: \(F=\pi E r\,u/(1-\nu^2)\).
  • Recovered force scales linearly with the assumed \(E\): wrong modulus → wrong force, one-for-one.
  • The inverse is ill-posed: noise on small far-field displacements blows up. Real TFM fits the whole field and regularizes (Module 2).
  1. Material known exactly + no noise: the dashed inferred field lands on the true field and F_inferred = F_true.
  2. Wrong stiffness: displacements are perfect but F_inferred is off by exactly E_assumed/E_true.
  3. Noisy: push the measurement bead far out (small u_z) and watch the inferred force swing wildly.

Background: a material-independent, local description

A spring is one force \(F\) and one displacement \(x\) — fine for one spring, useless for comparing materials. A 10 nN pull on a 5 µm contact means something different from 10 nN over 50 µm, and "the gel moved 2 µm" is meaningless without knowing over what distance. So we normalize.

Force → traction stress; displacement → strain

$$\sigma=\frac{F}{A}\ \ [\text{Pa}],\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{\Delta x}{L}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\ \ [\text{dimensionless}],\qquad \sigma=E\,\varepsilon$$

Both are now intensive and local, so tractions from a tiny and a large cell sit on the same axis, and the cell becomes a traction field, not one arrow. Cells generate tractions of order \(10^2\!-\!10^3\) Pa.

The crux: strain is geometry, but stress is material. Impose the same displacement (same strain) on a soft and a stiff gel and you get different stress, because \(\sigma=E\varepsilon\) scales with \(E\). Equivalently, for a fixed cell force a stiffer gel deforms less (\(u\propto1/E\)). Normalizing through \(\sigma=E\varepsilon\) makes the cell's force description portable between materials. (A uniform shift of the whole gel has zero gradient → zero strain → zero stress: only differences in displacement carry load.)

\(F=20\) nN over \(A=20\,\mu\)m² → \(\sigma=F/A=1\) kPa. On a soft \(E=1\) kPa gel that implies \(\varepsilon=\sigma/E=1.0\) (huge — beyond linear); on a stiff \(E=20\) kPa gel only \(\varepsilon=0.05\). Same force per area, very different deformation — the material dependence, laid bare.

  • Normalize: force/area → traction stress (Pa); displacement/length → strain (dimensionless).
  • Both are intensive and local, so different-size cells/gels compare on common axes — the cell is a traction field.
  • Strain is geometry; stress needs the material via \(\sigma=E\varepsilon\). Same displacement, different \(E\) ⇒ different stress.
  • This is the bridge into Module 2: measure the displacement field, normalize, and recover the traction field.
