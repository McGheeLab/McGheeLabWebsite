Background: the substrate is a spring you can read

TFM works because the cell sits on a soft, elastic material whose deformation we can measure. To turn that deformation back into force, we first need the material's force–displacement law. Start with the spring you already know from statics:

$$F = k\,x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma = E\,\varepsilon \;\;\text{(linear elastic, 1D)}$$

where \(\sigma\) is stress (force per area), \(\varepsilon\) is strain (fractional length change), and \(E\) is the Young's modulus — the substrate's stiffness.

Hydrogels (polyacrylamide, PEG, PDMS) used in TFM are hyperelastic: at large stretch they stiffen and Hooke's law breaks down. The simplest model that captures this is the incompressible neo-Hookean solid, whose true (Cauchy) stress under uniaxial stretch \(\lambda\) is

$$\sigma_{\text{neo}} = \mu\left(\lambda^2 - \frac{1}{\lambda}\right), \qquad \mu = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} \xrightarrow{\nu\to 0.5} \frac{E}{3}$$

Expand the neo-Hookean law for a small stretch \(\lambda = 1+\varepsilon\): \(\;\lambda^2-\tfrac1\lambda \approx (1+2\varepsilon)-(1-\varepsilon)=3\varepsilon\). So \(\sigma_{\text{neo}}\approx 3\mu\,\varepsilon = E\,\varepsilon\). The neo-Hookean curve is tangent to Hooke's law at the origin — linear elasticity is just its small-strain limit.

  • Linear elasticity (\(\sigma=E\varepsilon\)) is the leading-order approximation of any hyperelastic law.
  • For the small strains typical of TFM, treating the gel as linear elastic is justified — that is what makes the force recovery tractable.
  • At large stretch the neo-Hookean solid stiffens; using a linear model there underestimates stress.
  1. Start in Small strain. Notice the Hookean line and neo-Hookean curve sit almost on top of each other.
  2. Switch to Large strain and watch them separate. Read the % difference at maximum stretch.
  3. Change the modulus \(E\): does it change where the two models diverge, or only the vertical scale?

Background: strain is a derivative of the displacement field

TFM never measures strain directly — it measures the displacement field \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\) (how far each bead moved). Strain is the spatial gradient of that field:

$$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \tfrac12\!\left(\nabla\mathbf{u} + \nabla\mathbf{u}^{\top}\right),\quad \varepsilon_{xx}=\frac{\partial u_x}{\partial x},\quad \gamma_{xy}=\frac{\partial u_x}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial u_y}{\partial x}$$

The trace of the strain tensor is the fractional area change (the link between "change in area" and "change in displacements"):

$$\frac{\Delta A}{A} \approx \varepsilon_{xx}+\varepsilon_{yy} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$$

A uniform displacement (every bead shifts the same amount) has zero gradient ⇒ zero strain ⇒ zero stress. Only differences in displacement deform the material. That is why TFM needs a dense, well-sampled field, not a single arrow.

  • Strain comes from gradients of displacement, computed by differencing neighboring beads.
  • Strain is pure geometry; converting it to stress requires the material law and the modulus \(E\) from Tab 1.
  • Same displacement field on a 1 kPa vs a 40 kPa gel ⇒ very different stress.
  1. Apply Uniaxial stretch and watch the marker grid extend; read \(\varepsilon_{xx}\) and the area change.
  2. Switch to Simple shear — note \(\varepsilon_{xx}=\varepsilon_{yy}=0\) but the shear strain \(\gamma_{xy}\) is nonzero, and area barely changes.
  3. Raise the modulus \(E\): the strain is unchanged (it is geometry) but the stress scales up.

Background: what a 2D vs a 3D measurement can recover

A planar substrate imaged in one focal plane gives the in-plane displacement \((u_x,u_y)\) only. But cells also push and pull normal to the surface (the \(z\) direction). A 2D measurement is blind to that component.

Maskarinec et al. (2009) tracked the full 3D field with confocal microscopy + digital volume correlation and found that migrating fibroblasts produce significant normal (z) displacements even on nominally 2D substrates, and that normal forces "play important roles even in 2-D cell migration." A 2D-only measurement misses a real part of what the cell does.

$$\text{recovered }|\mathbf{t}|_{\text{2D}} = \sqrt{1-f_z^{\,2}}\;|\mathbf{t}|_{\text{true}}, \qquad f_z=\frac{|t_z|}{|\mathbf{t}|}$$

  • 2D TFM recovers tangential tractions; the normal component is invisible to it.
  • The more out-of-plane the cell pulls (\(f_z\) large), the more force a 2D measurement misses.
  • Rounded, invading, or 3D-embedded cells require a 3D measurement.
  1. Start with a Spread cell (mostly tangential pull). The 2D measurement recovers nearly all of the force.
  2. Switch to a Rounded / invading cell and watch the recovered-force bar drop as the normal fraction grows.
  3. Drag the normal fraction \(f_z\) and read the % of traction magnitude a 2D measurement would miss.

Background: beads sample the field — size and density set the limits

Fluorescent beads are the fiducial markers whose motion is the displacement measurement. Two independent properties matter:

Density → spatial resolution. Beads sample \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\) at discrete points. The mean bead spacing sets the finest traction feature you can resolve — sparse seeding undersamples the field and washes out sharp features (a Nyquist limit: features narrower than \(\sim\!2\times\) the spacing are lost).

Size vs PSF → localization precision. Each bead is localized to sub-pixel precision by fitting its intensity centroid. A bead near the size of the optical point-spread function (PSF) localizes best; far below it the bead is dim and noisy, far above it the blur hides the true center. There is a sweet spot.

The trade-off: packing in more, bigger beads sounds better, but once beads overlap you can no longer match each one between the stressed and reference images — tracking fails.
  • Traction resolution ≈ bead spacing; increase density to resolve finer features.
  • Localization precision is best when bead size ≈ a small multiple of the PSF.
  • Too sparse → undersampled; too dense/large → overlap and lost correspondence.
  1. In Sparse, see the reconstructed displacement profile miss the true peak (high error).
  2. Move to Optimal — the reconstruction tracks the truth and the error drops.
  3. Push to Over-seeded and increase bead size until the overlap warning appears and error rises again.

Background: the microscope decides whether 3D is even possible

To localize a bead in \(z\) you must know which depth its light came from. An inverted confocal microscope uses a pinhole to reject out-of-focus light — optical sectioning — producing a sharp axial intensity peak you can localize. This is what makes 3D bead tracking (and 3D TFM) possible.

An epifluorescence (widefield) microscope collects in-focus and out-of-focus light at once, so a bead's signal is essentially a z-projection: lateral \((x,y)\) position is fine, but the axial response is broad and ambiguous. Practically, epifluorescence limits you to a 2D measurement.

$$\sigma_z \;>\; \sigma_{xy}\quad\text{(optical microscopes always resolve worse along the axis than laterally)}$$

  • Confocal optical sectioning → resolvable bead \(z\) → 3D displacement fields.
  • Epifluorescence → projected signal → unreliable \(z\) → 2D-only.
  • Axial resolution is intrinsically worse than lateral — \(z\) is always the hard direction.
  1. In Confocal, note the sharp axial intensity peak and that recovered \(u_z\) follows the true value.
  2. Switch to Epifluorescence: the axial signal flattens and the recovered \(u_z\) collapses toward noise.
  3. Sweep the true \(u_z\) and compare how well each modality tracks it.

Background: from displacement to traction — and whether to trust it

The substrate mechanics give a forward map from traction to displacement (a convolution with the elastic Green's function): \(\;u_i(\mathbf{x})=\int G_{ij}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')\,t_j(\mathbf{x}')\,d\mathbf{x}'\). TFM needs the inverse: recover \(\mathbf{t}\) from the measured \(\mathbf{u}\). That deconvolution is ill-posed — it amplifies high-frequency noise, so tiny displacement errors explode into huge spurious tractions.

The fix is regularization (Tikhonov): minimize \(\lVert G\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{u}\rVert^2+\lambda^2\lVert\mathbf{t}\rVert^2\). The parameter \(\lambda\) trades resolution against noise:

$$\lambda\ \text{too small} \Rightarrow \text{noisy, spiky tractions}; \qquad \lambda\ \text{too large} \Rightarrow \text{smoothed, } \textbf{underestimated}\ \text{forces.}$$

A free cell exerts no net force, so a trustworthy recovered field must satisfy force balance \(\sum \mathbf{t}\,\Delta A \approx 0\). A large residual flags a bad (drifted / not-force-free) reference image. (The demo below is a 1-D toy with a Gaussian forward kernel standing in for the Green's function — the ill-posedness and the regularization trade-off are real.)

  • The forward problem is easy and well-posed; the inverse TFM needs is ill-posed.
  • A traction number is only meaningful with its assumptions (\(E,\nu\), reference), resolution, and uncertainty.
  • Check force balance; report the regularization choice; stiffer gels lower the displacement SNR.
  1. In Clean data, sweep \(\lambda\): too small is spiky, too large flattens the peaks. Find the value that best matches the true (dashed) tractions.
  2. Switch to Noisy data — now small \(\lambda\) is unusable; you must regularize more, accepting lower resolution.
  3. Try Drifted reference and watch the force-balance residual blow up — the read-out that tells you the measurement is untrustworthy.
  4. Raise \(E\): stiffer gel → smaller displacements → worse signal-to-noise.
