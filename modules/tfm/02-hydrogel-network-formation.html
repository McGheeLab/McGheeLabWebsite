What is a hydrogel — and how does one form?

A hydrogel is a three-dimensional network of polymer chains that is mostly water — often 70–99% by weight — tied together by crosslinks so it can swell but never dissolves. The result is a soft, jelly-like solid: it holds a shape and pushes back when you poke it, yet small molecules diffuse freely through its water-filled pores. That mix — soft, wet, permeable, and tunable — is why hydrogels are everywhere in bioengineering: contact lenses, wound dressings, drug-delivery depots, superabsorbents, 3-D-printed bio-inks, and the soft scaffolds we grow cells in to mimic tissue. For this course they matter because their stiffness and mesh size can be dialed to match real tissue, making them model mechanically-active biomaterials.

How one forms: liquid → solid

You begin with a liquid — small monomers dissolved in water, a little crosslinker, and a light-sensitive initiator. Shine light and a free-radical chain polymerization kicks off: radicals stitch the monomers into long chains and bridge those chains together, until at the gel point the liquid sol abruptly becomes one sample-spanning molecule — a solid gel. That cure is really four steps happening at once:

The four steps (animated just below)

  1. 1 ·Initiation — light cleaves the initiator into free radicals (R•). This is the spark: no radical, no reaction.
  2. 2 ·Propagation — a radical opens a monomer's C=C double bond and relocates to the new end, adding monomer after monomer. Chains grow, thousands of units long.
  3. 3 ·Crosslinking — a divinyl crosslinker spends its second C=C on a different chain, welding two chains together. These bridges are what turn a soup of separate chains into one connected network.
  4. 4 ·Termination — two radicals collide and pair up (or one gets buried in the network) and that chain stops growing. This is what ends the cure — usually before 100% of the monomer has reacted.

These four run simultaneously and in competition throughout the cure, and their balance decides the network you get — ideal, or riddled with defects.

So what can happen? The same four steps, mistuned, give very different gels: too little initiator → a few very long chains that gel early then stall (under-cured, leaky); too much initiator → many short chains and wasted loops (cyclization); too little crosslinker → no gel at all; leftover dangling ends and unreacted sol; or chains that simply entangle into a transient gel with no chemical crosslink. Watching, motivating, and tuning each of these is what the rest of this page (Modules A–G) and the Recipe tab are for.
  • A hydrogel = a water-swollen, crosslinked polymer network — a soft, permeable solid.
  • It forms by free-radical chain polymerization in four overlapping steps: initiation → propagation → crosslinking → termination.
  • The gel point is the instant one network first spans the sample (liquid sol → solid gel).
  • The balance of the four steps — set by initiator, crosslinker, and monomer — decides whether the gel is ideal or defect-heavy (loops, dangling ends, sol).

Background: a hydrogel is a recipe of vinyl monomers

Almost every synthetic hydrogel you will meet — polyacrylamide cell-culture gels, PEG-diacrylate bio-inks, poly(acrylic acid) superabsorbents, pHEMA contact lenses — is built the same way: dissolve small vinyl monomers (molecules carrying one or more reactive carbon–carbon double bonds, C=C) in water, add an initiator, and let a free-radical chain polymerization stitch them into one giant covalent network.

Two jobs: chain-formers vs. crosslinkers

What a monomer does to the topology is set by its number of double bonds:

  • Monovinyl monomers (one C=C → functionality \(f=2\)): acrylamide (AAm), acrylic acid (AAc), HEMA, NIPAM, PEG-monoacrylate (PEG-A). When the double bond opens it bonds to the unit before and the unit after — so each monovinyl is a backbone link. By themselves they make only long, soluble linear chains: no gel.
  • Divinyl crosslinkers (two C=C → functionality \(f=4\)): N,N′-methylene-bis-acrylamide ("bis"), PEG-diacrylate (PEG-DA), EGDMA. One double bond ties the molecule into one chain; the second can later tie into a different chain — that bridge is a crosslink, and it is what turns a soup of chains into a solid.

The identity within each class then tunes the physics: AAc adds fixed –COO⁻ charges (pH-driven swelling); PEG-A and PEG-DA are long and hydrophilic (open mesh, water-loving); NIPAM is thermoresponsive; bis is short (tight mesh) while PEG-DA is long (open mesh) (Peppas et al. 2000; Lin & Metters 2006).

$$\bar f_n=\frac{\sum_i n_i f_i}{\sum_i n_i},\qquad \bar f_w=\frac{\sum_i n_i f_i^{2}}{\sum_i n_i f_i},\qquad \text{(}f=2\text{ chain-former, }f=4\text{ crosslinker)}$$

The single most important topology number is the crosslinker mole fraction. Push it up and the network tightens (smaller mesh, stiffer, swells less); drop it to zero and you never gel. Everything in the next tabs follows from the recipe you set here.

The third ingredient: a photoinitiator (LAP)

Monomers don't react until something makes the first radical. We use LAP (lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate), the standard water-soluble Type-I photoinitiator for cell-laden hydrogels. Under 365–405 nm light each LAP molecule cleaves into two radicals that start growing chains; it is fast (≈10× I2959) and cytocompatible, which is why it dominates GelMA/PEGDA bioprinting (Fairbanks et al. 2009). Its concentration (typically ~0.05–0.5 wt%, a few mM, here entered as mol% of monomer) sets the number of radicals, hence the number of kinetic chains: more LAP → many short chains (faster, more complete cure, but lower molecular weight and more chain ends); less LAP → few long chains (gels at low conversion, then traps radicals and stalls). The crosslinker decides whether/where it gels; the initiator decides how fast and how many chains build it.

A second kind of crosslink: physical entanglements

There is a second, non-chemical way to build a network. As long chains grow in a crowded solution they curl around one another, and because two chains cannot pass through each other in 3-D, these entanglements act like crosslinks — except they can slide along the chain and slowly release as the chain reptates out of the tangle. So a gel held only by entanglements is transient and viscoelastic: solid on short timescales, flowing on long ones. Crucially, a recipe with no crosslinker at all — 100% acrylamide or PEG-acrylate — can still form a gel once the chains are long and concentrated enough to entangle (above the entanglement length \(M_e\)) (de Gennes 1971; Rubinstein & Colby 2003). The simulation detects these slip-links and shows that physical gel on the Anatomy and 3-D tabs.

A classic stiff polyacrylamide gel: 8 parts AAm + 0.4 parts bis + ~0.5 mol% LAP. By moles \(x_{\text{cross}}=0.4/8.4\approx4.8\%\) crosslinker; \(\bar f_n\approx2.10\) — barely above the linear value of 2, yet enough to span the sample. Halve the LAP and the same recipe cures slower into a higher-MW, lower-conversion network; double it and it cures fast but with more dangling ends.

  • Hydrogels = vinyl monomers + crosslinker + photoinitiator, joined by free-radical chain polymerization.
  • Topology is set by double-bond count: monovinyl (\(f=2\)) builds chains; divinyl (\(f=4\)) builds crosslinks.
  • Pure monovinyl never gels; a few mol% crosslinker is enough to span the sample.
  • LAP concentration sets radical/chain count: more → faster, shorter chains; less → fewer, longer chains.
  • Physical entanglements are a second crosslink type — long chains curl around each other; even with zero crosslinker they form a transient (viscoelastic) gel.

Module A · Meet the monomers — where the reactive bond lives

Every monomer below carries one or more carbon–carbon double bonds (C=C, drawn in red). That double bond is the only part that reacts during polymerization — everything else is the monomer's "identity group" that tunes water-affinity, charge, or bridge length. Count the red bonds: one C=C (f = 2) builds a chain; two C=C (f = 4) can bridge two chains into a network. Look at where the red bonds sit — for the crosslinkers they are at opposite ends, which is exactly why they can tie two different chains together.

Acrylamide (AAm)1 vinyl · f = 2

Reactive site: the single vinyl C=C. Identity: a neutral amide (–CONH₂), the workhorse of cell-culture (TFM) gels. One C=C ⇒ it can only add to a chain — never bridges.

Acrylic acid (AAc)1 vinyl · f = 2

Reactive site: the single vinyl C=C. Identity: a carboxyl (–COOH ⇌ –COO⁻ + H⁺) that carries fixed negative charge ⇒ pH-driven swelling (super-absorbents).

HEMA1 vinyl · f = 2

Reactive site: a methacrylate C=C (note the extra –CH₃ on the reacting carbon — bulkier, slightly slower). Identity: a hydroxyl tail (–OH); the contact-lens polymer.

NIPAM1 vinyl · f = 2

Reactive site: the single vinyl C=C. Identity: an isopropyl amide that drives a temperature switch (LCST ≈ 32 °C) ⇒ thermoresponsive gels.

PEG-acrylate (PEG-A)1 vinyl · f = 2

Reactive site: the single vinyl C=C — at one end only. Identity: a long, water-loving PEG tail that just dangles. One reactive end ⇒ long strands, no bridges (see Module C).

N,N′-methylene-bis-acrylamide (bis)2 vinyls · f = 4 · short bridge

Reactive sites: a vinyl C=C at each end. Identity: a single –CH₂– (methylene) bridge — very short ⇒ a tight mesh. One end joins chain A, the other end joins chain B = a crosslink.

PEG-diacrylate (PEG-DA)2 vinyls · f = 4 · long bridge

Reactive sites: a vinyl C=C at each end, separated by a long flexible PEG bridge. Two reactive ends ⇒ it bridges two chains ⇒ an open mesh (long bridge = large void).

EGDMA2 vinyls · f = 4 · short, hydrophobic

Reactive sites: a methacrylate C=C at each end. Identity: a short, hydrophobic ethylene-glycol bridge ⇒ a tight, water-shedding mesh.

Module B · What a free radical does — it opens the C=C and moves on

A free radical (R•) is an atom or group with one unpaired electron — it is desperate to pair that electron, which makes it extremely reactive. The electron-rich C=C double bond is the easiest place to grab a partner. When R• reacts with a C=C, it converts one weak π bond into two strong σ bonds: R bonds to the near carbon, and the leftover electron becomes a new radical on the far carbon. The radical is never used up — it simply relocates to the new chain end and attacks the next monomer. That relay, repeated thousands of times, is chain propagation, and it is what stitches monomers into a polymer.

Module G · How much initiator? — the Goldilocks dose of LAP

Module B showed what one radical does. The photoinitiator (LAP) sets how many radicals you light at once — and each radical starts exactly one growing chain, so the LAP dose ≈ the number of kinetic chains. The crosslinker decides whether you get a gel; the initiator decides how many chains build it, and how long they are. Crucially, more chains means shorter chains — the same monomer is shared among more growing ends. There is a sweet spot: too little and too much both give a worse, less-ideal network.

Too little LAP → only a handful of radicals, so a few very long chains. It cures slowly and reaches the gel point at low conversion, but then those few radicals get buried in the forming network and stall — leaving unreacted monomer and a leaky, under-cured gel. Too much LAP → a flood of radicals making many short chains: short chains carry more dangling chain ends (defects), and a growing tip in that crowd keeps reacting with pendant double bonds on its own nearby chain — cyclization, which wastes crosslinker on primary loops instead of load-bearing bridges. The network ends up loopy, defect-heavy and weaker. Just right (an intermediate dose, ~0.1–0.5 wt% LAP) makes enough medium-length chains to cure fully and fast, yet long enough to bridge into a well-connected, low-defect mesh (Fairbanks et al. 2009; Bansil et al. 1986; Wen et al. 2003).

  • LAP dose = number of radicals = number of growing chains; more LAP → more but shorter chains.
  • Too little: a few very long chains; gels early then stalls → under-cured, leftover sol.
  • Too much: many short chains → more chain-end defects and more loops (cyclization), fewer long bridging chains.
  • The ideal is an intermediate dose — fast, complete cure into a low-defect network. (Set LAP on the Recipe rail and grow it to see this.)

Module E · The endgroup — same C=C, a different personality

Every monovinyl reacts through the same vinyl, so they all build the same kind of backbone. What makes AAm, AAc, HEMA, NIPAM and PEG-A behave so differently is the endgroup — the side group R dangling off each unit. Three properties of R tune the gel: charge (osmotic swelling), bulk / steric size (how fast it polymerizes and how stiff the chain is), and water affinity (how much it swells, and whether it collapses with heat).

Charge → swelling. AAc's –COO⁻ (above pH≈4.5) makes the network a polyelectrolyte: trapped counter-ions pull water in by Donnan osmosis, so poly(AAc) is super-absorbent (100–500× its dry weight). Add salt or lower the pH and it collapses (Flory–Rehner 1943; Peppas et al. 2000).

Bulk → rate & stiffness. The bulky α-methyl of methacrylates (HEMA) and NIPAM's isopropyl crowd the C=C, so they propagate ~8–10× slower than acrylates and give stiffer backbones (Bevington 1967).

Water affinity → swelling & response. PEG-A is very hydrophilic (gels are 90–95% water); NIPAM is hydrophilic below ~32 °C but its isopropyls expel water above it — the chain collapses (LCST ≈ 32 °C), the gel de-swells: a thermoswitch (NIPAM LCST; Sci. Rep. 2015).

Honest note. Charge, water-affinity and bulk are shown as relative indices (the recipe feeds them into the swelling/transport readouts on the Properties subtab) — didactic scalings grounded in the cited theory, not fitted values.

Module C · One vinyl vs. two — why PEG-A makes strands but PEG-DA makes a mesh

Both molecules use the same acrylate chemistry — the difference is purely how many reactive ends they have. PEG-A has one, so it can only lengthen a single chain (the PEG just hangs off as a pendant). PEG-DA has two, at opposite ends, so each end can be captured by a different growing chain — and that one molecule becomes a covalent bridge tying the two chains together. Stack up millions of bridges and the separate chains fuse into one connected — if irregular — network: the mesh.

PEG-A (1 vinyl) → long strands

Each PEG-A adds to one backbone; its PEG tail dangles. You get long, water-soluble strands. They curl around each other (entanglements) and can form a weak, transient gel — but there is no covalent junction, so nothing is permanently tied together.

PEG-DA (2 vinyls) → mesh network

A PEG-DA's first end joins chain A; later its second end is grabbed by chain B — a permanent crosslink bridge (gold). Bridge after bridge welds the separate chains into one sample-spanning, irregular network with water-filled voids between strands — that is the mesh.

Module F · PEG by the numbers — molecular weight & multi-arm stars

PEG crosslinkers are not one thing — you buy them by the molecular weight of the PEG chain (PEG-DA 700, 3.4k, 10k, 20k…) and by architecture. A longer PEG chain is a longer bridge between junctions, so higher MW → bigger mesh (a PEG coil's size scales like ~√MW). Beyond linear PEG-A / PEG-DA, PEG is sold as 4-arm and 8-arm stars: one core with f reactive arms. Because each arm makes exactly one junction, star PEGs build remarkably uniform, near-ideal networks with few loops or dangling defects — the "tetra-PEG" gels.

Architecture sets functionality f

PEG-A (1 end, f=2) only lengthens a chain; PEG-DA (2 ends, f=4) bridges two; 4-arm / 8-arm stars are f=4 / f=8 junctions. Higher f gels at lower conversion — Flory: p꜀ = 1/(f−1) (Nishi/Sakai 2012; Flory 1941).

Molecular weight sets mesh size

Same architecture, rising MW = longer bridges = larger pores → softer, more swelling, faster transport (Canal & Peppas 1989; Lin & Metters 2006; Richbourg & Peppas 2021). Set architecture + MW in the recipe rail, then grow it to watch the mesh open up.

In the simulation: each PEG is laid onto the lattice as a real multi-cell strand/star — the body is inert, only the acrylate tips react — so molecular weight literally lengthens the strands and opens the mesh (more crosslinker still tightens it). The lattice is a coarse, nearest-neighbour kinetic model; real PEG is a continuous random coil, but the lattice captures the topology and defect physics (Herrmann–Stauffer–Landau 1983; Bansil et al. 1984).

Module D · The network zoo — what builds the mesh, and what each part does to behavior

Once the network is built, not every strand pulls its weight. The same five features show up in every hydrogel, and each one changes how the gel behaves. Here they are on one map; the table says what each does.

Crosslink A junction where ≥3 strands meet — only a divinyl monomer can make one (its 2nd C=C bridges into another chain). Sets stiffness. More crosslinks ⇒ smaller mesh, higher modulus, less swelling.
Elastic strand A chain segment tied to a different crosslink at each end — a load-bearing "entropic spring." The only thing that carries load: \(G\approx\nu k_BT\) counts these and nothing else.
Free / dangling end A strand attached at only one crosslink; the other end is a loose chain end. Carries no equilibrium load. Adds floppiness and viscous loss; can leach out — a defect.
Loop A strand whose two ends close back onto the same crosslink (a wasted bridge). Wastes crosslinker and stores no load ⇒ delays gelation and softens the gel.
Entanglement Two chains threaded around each other; they can't pass through one another, so it acts like a crosslink — but it can slide and release. A temporary (physical) crosslink: makes the gel viscoelastic — solid on short timescales, flows on long ones.
Mesh size ξ The average open void enclosed by a ring of strands — the "pore" a solute must thread. Gatekeeper of transport & swelling: molecules larger than ξ are excluded; smaller ξ ⇒ slower diffusion, less swelling.

Background: growing the network, one reaction at a time

This is a kinetic Monte-Carlo realization of the kinetic gelation model for free-radical crosslinking copolymerization (Herrmann, Stauffer & Landau 1983; Bansil, Herrmann & Stauffer 1984, 1985). Monomers sit on a lattice (the dark cells are water) that is a thin 3-D slab with periodic boundaries — periodic in-plane so growth is never wall-limited, and several layers thick so the network has true 3-D connectivity. Crucially, the slab is auto-sized to ≈3.5× the expected mesh, so the gel is never confined below its own correlation length. We then view that slab face-on as a single depth-shaded plane. A few initiator radicals light up; each step a radical reacts with a neighboring monomer, opens a double bond, forms a covalent bond, and the radical hops onto the new unit — propagation, which grows a chain.

Between reactions, unreacted monomers and solvent diffuse (the mobile kinetic gelation model), feeding fresh monomer to the growing front so conversion climbs to realistic values instead of freezing early. When a growing radical reaches a crosslinker that already sits in another chain (it still has an unreacted "pendant" double bond), the new bond bridges two chains → a crosslink. Radicals die by combination (two radicals meet) or by getting trapped (buried with no free neighbor) — which is why real free-radical gels stall below 100% conversion.

$$\text{R}\!\cdot\;+\;\text{M}\;\xrightarrow{\;k_p\;}\;\text{R–M}\!\cdot\qquad p=\frac{\text{reacted double-bond ends}}{\text{total double-bond ends}}$$

Watch three things as it runs: the conversion \(p\) climb, isolated clusters merge, and — at the gel point — one cluster suddenly spans the whole box (it turns gold). Below the gel point you have a pourable sol of finite molecules; above it, a single sample-spanning molecule: the gel.

  • Initiation lights radicals; propagation grows chains; the radical hops unit-to-unit.
  • A crosslinker's second double bond, reacted later, bridges two chains = a crosslink.
  • Radicals die by combination or burial → conversion stalls (auto-deceleration / trapping).
  • The gel point is a percolation transition: a single cluster spans the sample.

Background: reading the finished network

Once growth stops, the same lattice tells you everything a rheologist or drug-delivery scientist cares about. We classify every covalent bond and node:

  • Crosslinks (junctions, degree ≥ 3): where ≥3 strands meet — only divinyl units can do this. Their density sets stiffness.
  • Elastically effective strands: a chain segment tied to a different crosslink at each end. These are the load-bearing "entropic springs" — only these count toward the modulus (Flory 1953).
  • Dangling ends / pendant chains: a strand attached to the network at only one crosslink, with a free chain end. It carries no equilibrium load — a defect that adds floppiness and loss.
  • Meshes (the voids): every closed ring of strands encloses an open void — that ring is a mesh. We find all of them (the network's independent cycles) and measure the average void size; this is the open space a solute must thread. More crosslinker → more, smaller meshes.
  • Primary loops (a defect — NOT a mesh): a strand whose two ends close back onto the same crosslink. Unlike a mesh (which is bounded by several strands enclosing a void), a primary loop wastes one crosslink on itself — a cyclization defect that delays gelation (Bansil et al. 1986; Wen et al. 2003).
  • Sol fraction: finite molecules and unreacted monomer not attached to the spanning gel — they wash out.

Mesh size — the average void the network encloses

A mesh is a closed perimeter of polymer enclosing a void; the mesh size is the average void diameter — the "pore" a drug molecule or nutrient must thread. We enumerate every mesh as an independent cycle (ring) of strands and take the mean spatial diameter \((2R_g)\) of those rings. Their count is the network's independent-cycle (cyclomatic) number \(E-V+1\), minus any primary-loop defects (which enclose no real void). As a cross-check, the Peppas/Flory correlation length between crosslinks \(\xi\approx a\sqrt{\bar N_c}\) tracks the same trend (Canal & Peppas 1989; Peppas et al. 2000):

$$N_{\text{mesh}} \approx (E - V + 1) - N_{\text{primary loops}},\qquad \text{mesh size} = \langle 2R_g\rangle_{\text{rings}}\qquad(\text{cross-check } \xi\approx a\sqrt{\bar N_c})$$

\(a\) = monomer size (one lattice cell), \(\bar N_c\) = backbone units per bridging strand. More crosslinker → more rings, each smaller → smaller mesh / smaller voids.

  • A mesh = a closed ring of strands enclosing a void; we identify all of them and report the count + mean void size.
  • Only strands bridging two distinct crosslinks bear equilibrium load → set the modulus.
  • Dangling ends, primary loops (≠ meshes), and sol are defects that don't stiffen the gel.
  • Mesh (void) size shrinks as crosslinker rises; it controls what diffuses through and how much the gel swells.

A gel is not frozen — it jiggles. Press Bring to life and every strand fluctuates under thermal energy (\(k_BT\)). Background: when does the soup become a solid?

The fundamental idea: percolation (sol → gel)

Strip away the chemistry first. Picture the monomers as dots in a box and every new covalent bond as a link drawn between neighbors. Early on you get only small, separate clusters drifting in liquid — a sol that pours. As more bonds form, clusters grow and fuse, until at one critical moment a single cluster stretches all the way across the box: it percolates. That instant is the gel point. One bond past it and the sample is a single, sample-spanning molecule — a gel that holds its shape. Nothing about the molecules changed discontinuously; their connectivity did. (It is the very same "turns gold the moment it spans the box" event you watch live on the Grow subtab.)

Three snapshots of the same sample as more bonds form (left → right). Gray = finite clusters (the sol); gold = the one cluster that spans the box (the gel). The middle frame is the gel point — the first instant a spanning cluster exists.

In the gel, the control knob is conversion \(p\)

As conversion \(p\) rises, molecules grow and merge. At a critical conversion \(p_c\) — the gel point — the weight-average molecule size diverges and one cluster suddenly spans the whole sample. This is a percolation transition: the gel fraction is the order parameter, rising from zero at \(p_c\); the average finite-cluster size peaks right at \(p_c\) (Stauffer; Bansil et al. 1984).

The mean-field benchmark — and why reality lags it

Classical Flory–Stockmayer theory ignores space: it assumes any two unreacted groups can find each other. For a crosslinker of functionality \(f\), gelation needs only a fraction

$$\alpha_c=\frac{1}{f-1}=\frac13\quad(f=4)$$

of a crosslinker's branch potential to be reacted (Flory 1941; Stockmayer 1943).

On a lattice — and in real gels — reactions are local. A growing radical keeps finding the same chain's pendant groups, closing loops instead of new bridges. Those loops waste crosslinker, so the sample gels later (higher \(p_c\)) than mean field predicts, and the network is spatially heterogeneous (Bansil et al. 1986; Wen et al. 2003; Richbourg & Peppas 2021). The simulation measures both the real percolation \(p_c\) and how far past \(1/3\) the crosslinkers had to react — its loop fraction is the wasted-crosslinker bill.

  • Gelation is percolation: gel fraction rises from 0 at \(p_c\); finite-cluster size peaks at \(p_c\).
  • Flory–Stockmayer mean field: a tetrafunctional crosslinker needs only \(\alpha_c=1/3\) reacted to gel.
  • Real/simulated gels gel later — cyclization (loops) wastes crosslinker; networks are heterogeneous.
  • Less crosslinker (or poor packing) pushes \(p_c\) up, or removes the gel point entirely.
  1. Press Sweep: the gel fraction (gold) climbs from zero and the finite-cluster size (purple) spikes — that spike marks the simulated \(p_c\).
  2. Compare the simulated \(p_c\) to the mean-field line; the verdict reports how much extra conversion loops cost.
  3. Try the low / medium / high crosslinker presets and watch \(p_c\) march left as crosslinker rises.

Background: from network structure to gel behavior

The structure you grew dictates the three properties that matter for biomechanics and delivery. All three trace back to two measured numbers: the elastic strand density \(\nu\) and the mesh size \(\xi\).

  • Stiffness (shear modulus). Rubber-elasticity / affine-network theory: only elastically effective strands store energy, so \(G\approx \nu\,k_BT\) (Flory 1953; Lin & Metters 2006). More crosslinker → more strands → stiffer. This is the modulus the TFM module turns into a measurable cell force.
  • Swelling. Flory–Rehner: a gel swells until the osmotic drive to mix with water balances the elastic stretch of the strands; ionic monomers (AAc) add a large Donnan osmotic term, so charged gels are super-absorbent and pH-responsive (Flory & Rehner 1943). Tighter mesh ⇒ less swelling.
  • Solute transport. A molecule diffuses freely only if it is smaller than the mesh; as the solute radius \(r_s\to\xi\) diffusion is throttled and then excluded \(\;D/D_0\) falls (Canal & Peppas 1989; Peppas et al. 2000). This is the diffusion–reaction setting of the molecular-scale lectures.

$$G\approx \nu\,k_BT,\qquad \frac{D}{D_0}\approx\exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{r_s}{\xi}\right)^{2}\right]\;(r_s<\xi),\qquad Q_{\text{swell}}\uparrow\ \text{with charge, hydrophilicity, mesh}$$

  • \(G\approx\nu k_BT\): stiffness is set by the density of load-bearing strands, not total polymer.
  • Swelling rises with hydrophilicity and fixed charge, falls with crosslink density.
  • Solutes larger than the mesh are excluded; \(D/D_0\) collapses as \(r_s\to\xi\).
  • One recipe knob — crosslinker — trades stiffness against mesh, swelling, and permeability.
  1. Grow a network on Tabs 1–3, then come here: the modulus, swelling index, and diffusion curve are computed from your measured \(\nu\) and \(\xi\).
  2. Move the solute size and watch where it crosses the mesh — that is the molecular-weight cutoff of your gel.
  3. Add acrylic acid on Tab 1, re-grow, and see the swelling index jump (fixed charge) while stiffness barely moves.

Background: you are the free radical — build the network by hand

Everything in Tabs 1–6 happened automatically. Here you make the topological decisions a growing radical makes. Drop monomers into the sample box (the dashed vessel), then drag one glowing C=C onto another to react them. A glowing, pulsing ring is a reactive site — a carbon–carbon double bond with a free slot. Each C=C opens into two σ bonds (just like the propagation animation on Tab 1), so a monovinyl (one C=C, \(f=2\)) can only string into a chain, while a divinyl (two C=C, \(f=4\)) can tie its second end into a different chain — a crosslink — and turn separate strands into one connected mesh.

When a site's two slots are used it stops glowing (it is "spent"). Build chains, bridge them into meshes, leave defects, or thread two long chains for a physical (entanglement) crosslink with the Entangle tool. The live readout names every feature you create — chains, crosslinks, elastic strands, dangling ends, loops, mesh voids, entanglements — and a behavior card turns your structure into stiffness / swelling / transport, tying back to the Structure → Properties tab.

Model assumptions & honesty notes. This is a deliberately simplified, didactic lattice model. (1) Topology is set by functionality (monovinyl \(f=2\), divinyl \(f=4\)) under an equal-reactivity assumption; real monomers have different reactivity ratios. (2) The lattice is a periodic quasi-3-D slab, several layers thick and auto-sized to ≈3.5× the expected mesh so the network is resolved in 3-D and is never wall-confined; we render it face-on as one plane. (3) Unreacted monomers and solvent diffuse (the mobile kinetic gelation model, Bansil et al. 1985); full radical reptation and chain mobility are not resolved. (4) Physical entanglements are modeled as slip-links between long chains (different kinetic chains) that cross transversely, at a density of ≈one per \(N_e\) backbone units (tube-model scaling); they let an uncrosslinked melt form a transient gel. This is a coarse proxy — it does not compute true topological linking numbers, and the entanglements are treated as static constraints for connectivity (their sliding/reptation relaxation is described, not dynamically simulated). (5) Mesh size, modulus, swelling, and diffusion are reported as scalings / relative indices grounded in the cited theory, not fitted absolute values. The qualitative trends (more crosslinker → smaller mesh, stiffer, less swelling; delayed gelation from loops) are robust and match experiment. (6) The 3-D "Bring to life" view runs overdamped Langevin (Brownian) dynamics on the covalent graph; the elapsed-time clock is an illustrative order-of-magnitude marker (≈1 ns per step, a representative monomer Brownian time), meant to convey the ns→µs timescale of mesh fluctuations, not a quantitative prediction.
