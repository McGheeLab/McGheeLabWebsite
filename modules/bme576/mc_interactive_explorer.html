Uncertainty & Error Propagation

Tumor Spheroid Growth and Drug Dose-Response — Module 10

Background: Why Uncertainty Matters in In Vitro Cancer Models

Every parameter you measure has uncertainty. Cell counts come from haemocytometers with operator-to-operator variation. Growth rates are inferred from noisy live-cell imaging. Drug IC50 values are estimated from replicate dose-response wells. When you push these noisy parameters through a mathematical model, the noise propagates into the prediction. How much noise reaches the output, and which input parameter dominates that uncertainty, are the central questions of this module.

Two Cancer Systems Used Throughout

This explorer toggles between two canonical in vitro models:

Gompertz tumor spheroid growth:

$$N(t) = K\,\exp\!\Bigl[\ln(N_0/K)\,e^{-r t}\Bigr]$$

where \(N_0\) is initial cell count, \(r\) is intrinsic growth rate (h-1), and \(K\) is carrying capacity. This describes 3D spheroid growth where proliferation slows as nutrient/oxygen become limiting.

Hill drug dose-response:

$$E(C) = E_{\max}\,\dfrac{C^n}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^n + C^n}$$

where \(E_{\max}\) is maximal effect, \(\mathrm{EC}_{50}\) is the half-maximal concentration, and \(n\) is the Hill coefficient. This is the standard model for cell viability assays.

Two Methods to Propagate Uncertainty

Partial-derivative (analytical) method: Linearize the model around your best parameter estimate using a first-order Taylor expansion. Variance contributions add as squares of partial derivatives times input variances. Fast, gives a Gaussian approximation, can be wrong when the model is nonlinear or the inputs are large.

Monte Carlo method: Sample parameter vectors from their distributions, run the model on each sample, look at the histogram of outputs. Captures the full nonlinear distribution — including skewness and heavy tails — at the cost of running the model thousands of times.

Four Scenarios You'll Compare

Use the Scenario bar above the panel to switch between four cases. They are designed to expose distinct UQ phenomena:

  • Spheroid Early (t = 72 h): Gompertz at intermediate time. Mild nonlinearity; analytical and MC will agree closely. Growth-rate uncertainty \(\sigma_r\) dominates here because by 72 h the trajectory has multiplied initial-count uncertainty almost entirely away.
  • Spheroid Late (t = 240 h): Same Gompertz biology, pushed to long time where the curve is approaching its plateau. Carrying-capacity uncertainty \(\sigma_K\) dominates — the spheroid is settling near \(K\), so variance in \(K\) maps almost one-to-one to variance in \(N(t)\). The local linearity in \(K\) means analytical and MC actually still agree well here.
  • Hill at EC50 (C = 5 µM): Drug response is on the steep middle of the curve. \(\sigma_{\mathrm{EC}_{50}}\) dominates because small horizontal shifts in EC50 cause large vertical changes in effect — and this asymmetry produces a noticeably skewed output distribution. This is the canonical case where MC reveals what analytical hides.
  • Hill saturated (C = 50 µM): At high dose, \(E_{\max}\) dominates — \(\mathrm{EC}_{50}\) sensitivity collapses because the curve is flat there. Effect is bounded above by \(E_{\max}\), so the MC distribution is left-skewed and the analytical Gaussian incorrectly leaks past 1.0.
  • Uncertainty quantification (UQ) answers two questions: how big is the prediction error, and which input parameter is responsible?
  • The partial-derivative method is fast and gives an analytical Gaussian; Monte Carlo is general but expensive.
  • They agree when the model is locally linear and the inputs are small. They diverge in strongly nonlinear regimes.
  1. Click Spheroid Early. Note the cell count prediction with its ±2σ band. The verdict reports the relative error and which parameter dominates.
  2. Switch to Spheroid Late. Watch the band swell — long time amplifies parameter uncertainty exponentially. The dominant parameter changes too.
  3. Switch to Hill at EC50. The system flips from cell counts to fractional drug effect. All three parameters contribute roughly equally.
  4. Switch to Hill saturated. Only \(E_{\max}\) contributes — the other two are invisible at this dose. Where you measure determines what you can identify.

Background: The Partial-Derivative (Delta) Method

If you know your parameter estimate \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) and its variances \(\sigma_i^2\), and the parameters are uncorrelated, the propagated variance of any model output \(f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) is given by a first-order Taylor expansion:

$$\sigma_f^2 \;\approx\; \sum_{i=1}^{p}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_i}\right)^{\!2}\!\sigma_i^2$$

If parameters are correlated (covariance matrix \(\Sigma_\theta\)), the full form is \(\sigma_f^2 \approx \mathbf{J}^T\,\Sigma_\theta\,\mathbf{J}\) where \(\mathbf{J}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f\). This module assumes independence; correlation is covered in Module 11 (calibration).

Two Diagnostic Quantities You Can Compute Once You Have the Partials

Variance fraction \(\;f_i \;=\; (\partial f/\partial \theta_i)^2\,\sigma_i^2 \,/\, \sigma_f^2\;\). Fraction of total variance attributable to parameter \(i\). The parameter with the largest \(f_i\) is the one whose σ you should reduce first.

Sensitivity coefficient \(\;S_i \;=\; (\theta_i/f)\,\partial f/\partial \theta_i\;\). Dimensionless local sensitivity (% change in output per % change in parameter). This is not the same as variance fraction.

Sensitivity ≠ Error Contribution

A parameter can be highly sensitive (the output responds strongly to it) yet contribute almost nothing to prediction error if it is measured precisely. Conversely, a weakly sensitive parameter can dominate error if its σ is large.

The interactive bar chart on this tab shows both quantities side-by-side. Compare the heights of the hollow bars (|sensitivity coefficient|) against the solid bars (variance fraction). Switch scenarios to see how the relative ordering changes — in some scenarios the rankings agree, in others they don't.

How the Partial Derivatives are Computed Here

For each scenario we evaluate \(\partial f/\partial \theta_i\) by central finite differences:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_i} \;\approx\; \frac{f(\theta_i + h) - f(\theta_i - h)}{2h}, \quad h = \max(\epsilon,\;|\theta_i|\cdot 10^{-6})$$

Step size is parameter-relative to handle widely different scales (\(N_0\sim 10^3\) vs \(r\sim 10^{-2}\)).

  • Variance fraction tells you which σ to shrink. Sensitivity tells you which parameter the model cares about. They can disagree.
  • The partial-derivative method is exact if the model is linear over the range of input uncertainty — otherwise it is a first-order approximation.
  • Computing partials via finite differences with parameter-relative step size is the safest default.
  1. Stay on Spheroid Early. Inspect the variance-fraction stacked area: at very early time almost all variance is from \(\sigma_{N_0}\); by \(t = 72\) h, growth-rate uncertainty \(\sigma_r\) has taken over.
  2. Compare the two right-hand bars: |sensitivity coefficient| (hollow) vs variance fraction (solid). The two bars rank parameters differently when one parameter has small \(\sigma\) but large sensitivity, or vice versa.
  3. Switch to Spheroid Late. The growth curve is now near plateau, so \(K\) sets the answer almost entirely — its variance fraction approaches 1.
  4. Switch to Hill saturated. \(E_{\max}\) takes the bulk of variance; sensitivity to \(\mathrm{EC}_{50}\) collapses because the dose is past the steep transition. Where you measure determines what you can identify.

Background: How Monte Carlo Works, From the Ground Up

So far you've heard about Monte Carlo as a way to push uncertainty through a model. This tab strips the method back to its bones. The core idea is simpler than it sounds: each measurement has a probability distribution describing its noise; if you resample those distributions and refit, you build up the distribution of the fitted parameters.

Cancer-Biology Setup — Doubling Time From a Growth Assay

You imaged a tumor cell line at several time points and counted cells. Exponential growth means the natural log of the count is linear in time:

$$\ln N(t) \;=\; \ln N_0 \;+\; k\,t, \qquad k \;=\; \frac{\ln 2}{T_d}$$

The slope \(k\) gives you the growth rate; \(T_d = \ln 2 / k\) is the doubling time. Each measurement \(y_i = \ln N(t_i)\) carries its own measurement noise.

The Five-Step Monte Carlo Recipe

  1. Collect noisy measurements \(y_i\) at known \(t_i\). Each comes with an assumed noise distribution.
  2. Pick a per-point distribution: Normal (calibration noise), Uniform (bounded readout), or Log-normal (multiplicative dilution/counting errors — right-skewed).
  3. Resample each \(y_i\) from its distribution. This produces one perturbed dataset.
  4. Refit the line to that resampled dataset, recording the slope and intercept.
  5. Repeat thousands of times. The histograms of slope and intercept are the parameter uncertainty.

Heteroscedasticity — When σ Varies Across Points

Real growth assays are not uniformly noisy. At high cell density, clumping, occlusion, and saturating fluorescence inflate measurement error. The Heteroscedastic toggle scales each point's σ with its time value to mimic this. Watch how late-time noise propagates more strongly into the slope estimate — later points have more leverage on the fit.

What MC Reveals That a Single Fit Cannot

One fit gives you a single line. MC gives you a distribution of lines. The width of the slope histogram is the uncertainty in the doubling time. If the histogram is symmetric, you can report \(\hat k \pm \sigma_k\). If it's skewed (e.g., from log-normal noise), you should report a 95% percentile-based CI instead — the asymmetry is real, and \(\pm\sigma\) misrepresents the upper tail.

  • MC error propagation works by resampling noise on the data and refitting. No closed-form Jacobian required.
  • The shape of the per-point noise distribution maps directly to the shape of the parameter distribution.
  • Heteroscedastic noise gives later points more leverage; their distribution dominates the slope uncertainty.
  • Fewer points × bigger σ = wider parameter histogram. There is no free lunch.
  1. Click Generate Data. A fresh dataset appears with error bars sized to the chosen σ.
  2. Click Run. Faint red lines fill in the plot — each is one MC iteration's refit. The slope and intercept histograms below build up. The MC mean (colored dashed) should converge on the true value (black dashed).
  3. Switch Error Distribution to Log-normal, then Generate Data and Run again. The slope histogram is now noticeably right-skewed; reporting ±σ would understate the upper tail.
  4. Toggle Heteroscedastic on. Late points now have larger error bars. Re-generate and run — the slope histogram widens because late points dominate the fit.
  5. Drop Number of points to 4. Re-generate and run. The slope histogram balloons — fewer measurements means each point's noise has nowhere to average out.

Background: When the Two Methods Agree (and When They Don't)

The two methods agree exactly when:

  • The model is linear in the parameters over the range of input variation, AND
  • The input distributions are Gaussian.

They disagree when nonlinearity matters — specifically when the second derivative of \(f\) times \(\sigma\) is appreciable compared with the first derivative.

Second-order Taylor correction (the term the delta method drops):

$$f(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}) \approx f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \mathbf{J}^T\delta\boldsymbol{\theta} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^T\,\mathbf{H}\,\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}$$

If \(\|\mathbf{H}\,\sigma\| \ll \|\mathbf{J}\|\), the linearization is fine. If not, the MC mean shifts away from \(f(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\) and the MC distribution becomes skewed. The Gompertz at long time is the canonical example: nested exponentials produce a multiplicative-like distribution.

Three Diagnostics That Tell You Which Method to Trust

  1. σ-ratio: if \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{MC}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{analytical}}| / \sigma_{\mathrm{analytical}} > 10\%\), nonlinearity matters. Trust MC.
  2. Mean shift: if MC mean differs from \(f(\hat\theta)\) by more than \(\sigma/10\), the bias from second-order terms is real. Trust MC.
  3. Skewness: if \(|\mathrm{skew}| > 0.3\), the output distribution is non-Gaussian. The analytical ±σ band misrepresents both tails. Trust MC.

Reading the Comparison Plot

The histogram is the MC output distribution; the purple curve is the analytical Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(f(\hat\theta), \sigma_{\mathrm{analytical}}^2)\). For Spheroid Early and Spheroid Late they overlap almost perfectly — the Gompertz dynamics are locally near-linear at both 72 h and the late-time plateau, so the analytical method works. For Hill at EC50 the histogram has a noticeable right tail the Gaussian can't capture; for Hill saturated the histogram is bounded above and left-skewed.

  • If MC and analytical σ agree within 10%, you can use the analytical method downstream — it is much faster.
  • If they disagree by more than 10%, or if skewness exceeds 0.3, report the MC distribution directly.
  • For decisions involving tail probabilities (failure rates, overshoot), always use MC.
  1. On Spheroid Early, click Run MC. The MC histogram and the analytical Gaussian (purple) sit on top of each other. σ-ratio is near 1.00 and skewness is near zero.
  2. Switch to Hill at EC50 and re-run. Now the Gaussian is too narrow under the right tail and too tall in the middle. Skewness climbs to about 0.5; the Q-Q plot bows away from the 1:1 line at both ends.
  3. Inspect the σ-comparison bar (right side). Each bar shows one method's σ. Equal heights signal the analytical method is reliable; a large gap is a warning.
  4. Switch to Hill saturated. The output is bounded (effect ≤ Emax) so the MC distribution is left-skewed and bounded above. The Gaussian leaks past 1.0, which is impossible.

Capstone: Use UQ to Plan Better Experiments

The previous tabs taught you how to measure uncertainty. This tab uses that machinery to reduce it. The basic loop:

  1. Compute the variance fraction \(f_i\) for every parameter (Tab 2).
  2. Identify the dominant parameter — the one with the largest \(f_i\).
  3. Improve that measurement (more replicates, better instrument, longer counting time).
  4. Recompute. The improvement in total σ is bounded by the dominant fraction.

What halving the dominant σ buys you:

$$\sigma_f^{\text{new}} = \sqrt{\sigma_f^2 - 0.75\,(\partial f/\partial \theta_d)^2\,\sigma_d^2} = \sigma_f\,\sqrt{1 - 0.75\,f_d}$$

If \(f_d = 0.80\) (one parameter takes 80% of variance), halving its σ cuts total σ to \(\sigma_f\sqrt{1-0.6}=0.63\,\sigma_f\) — a 37% improvement. If \(f_d = 0.30\), the same effort only gets you 12% improvement: it isn't worth shrinking that σ alone — you have to attack the next-largest contributor too.

The Practical Decision Tree

  • One parameter dominates (> 60% of variance): halve it. Do this first.
  • Two parameters share dominance (> 80% combined): halve both. Halving one is not enough.
  • Variance is spread across all parameters: the model itself may need re-formulation, or you may need to redesign the experimental conditions (e.g., measure at a different concentration where one parameter dominates — see Hill saturated scenario).

Synthesis Across Tabs 1–4

You used Tab 1 to see the prediction with its band. Tab 2 told you which parameter to attack. Tab 3 confirmed the σ from sampling. Tab 4 told you whether to trust the cheap analytical method or pay for MC. This tab closes the loop: it shows the prediction after the improved measurement, and quantifies the gain.

  • UQ is not just diagnosis — it's a planning tool. Variance fraction tells you exactly where extra experimental effort buys the most.
  • Improvement is bounded: cutting all of σd still leaves \(\sigma_f\sqrt{1-f_d}\). You can never get below the sum of the other terms.
  • If variance is spread evenly, parameter precision isn't the limit — the model or the experimental design is.
  1. On Spheroid Early click Halve dominant σ. The "After" curve shows a tighter band; the verdict reports the % reduction.
  2. On Spheroid Late the dominant parameter has switched to \(K\). Halving \(\sigma_K\) buys a much bigger fractional reduction here because \(f_K\) is close to 1.
  3. Try Halve all σ. Total σ falls by exactly \(\sqrt{1/4} = 1/2\) for a linear model. For nonlinear models the relationship is nearly the same when MC and analytical agree.
  4. Switch to Hill saturated. \(E_{\max}\) takes most of the variance — halving it is by far the most efficient action. Halving \(\sigma_{\mathrm{EC}_{50}}\) here would be largely wasted effort because the curve is flat at this dose.
  5. Click Restore to return to baseline before switching scenarios.
