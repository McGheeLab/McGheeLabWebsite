Model Validation

Validating a Cell Invasion Model Against Transwell Assay Data — Module 13

Background: Calibration vs. Validation

Connection to Modules 11–12: In Module 11, you learned to calibrate a model — fitting parameters to data. In Module 12, you learned to design experiments that efficiently test your model assumptions. Now you close the loop: does your calibrated model actually predict new data? Validation is the final, essential test before you trust a model for decisions. We use a different cancer system (cell invasion through Transwell membranes) to show that validation principles are universal.

Training vs. Validation Split

Calibration (training) fits model parameters to a subset of data. Validation tests the fitted model on unseen data it was never trained on. A model that fits training data perfectly but fails on validation data is overfit — it memorized the noise, not the signal. This is the most important concept in all of modeling.

Key Equations — Cell Invasion Model

Matrigel Transwell invasion model:

$$N_{\text{invaded}}(t) = N_0 \left(1 - e^{-D\,t / L^2}\right) e^{\mu t}$$

Parameter meanings:

  • \(N_0\) = cells seeded (fixed at 50,000)
  • \(D\) = invasion coefficient (cm²/h) — how fast cells penetrate the Matrigel
  • \(L\) = Matrigel thickness (cm) — the barrier cells must cross
  • \(\mu\) = proliferation rate (1/h) — cell division during the assay

Biology: The first term \((1 - e^{-Dt/L^2})\) models diffusion-like invasion through the membrane. The second term \(e^{\mu t}\) accounts for exponential proliferation. At early times, invasion dominates; at late times, proliferation amplifies the invaded population.

Why Hold Out Data?

Suppose you have 9 time points with 4 replicates each = 36 data points. You fit a 2-parameter model (\(D\) and \(\mu\)).

  • With 36 points and 2 parameters, you have 34 degrees of freedom for error — plenty for a good fit.
  • But can you trust R² = 0.99 on training data? No! A flexible enough model will always fit training data well.
  • Hold out 8 separate measurements at different time points. If R² > 0.95 on both training and validation, you can be confident the model captures real biology.
  • If training R² = 0.99 but validation R² = 0.70, something is wrong: overfitting, wrong model structure, or systematic experimental differences.

Four Failure Modes of Validation

Use the Validation Scenario bar at the top of the page to switch between four cases. All four use the same biology (\(\mu\) = 0.01, \(D\) = 2.5\(\times\)10-7) and the same data-generating process — only the train/validation split or the model form changes:

  • Proper Split (baseline): Random interleaved time points span the full range. Mechanistic fit. The model generalizes — this is what right looks like.
  • Improper Split: Sequential split (train on 2–8 h, validate on 24–48 h). Same biology and same model, but the early-time training data fails to constrain \(\mu\), so the fit drifts. Late-time validation collapses.
  • Overfit: Proper split, but the model is replaced by a degree-6 polynomial (7 free coefficients). Training R² goes to nearly 1.00 because the polynomial memorizes every noise wiggle, but predictions oscillate between data points and validation collapses. Classic bias-variance tradeoff failure.
  • Underfit: Proper split, but the model is replaced by a pure exponential \(N(t) = a\,e^{bt}\) — missing the diffusion-lag term \(1 - e^{-Dt/L^2}\). Both training and validation R² are mediocre. More data won't fix it: the model form is wrong. Residuals show systematic structure.

Plus a High Noise Preset button next to the noise slider: snaps noise to 0.35 to demonstrate what happens when the data itself is the limiting factor — even a correct model with a proper split can't be validated reliably under enough noise.

The pedagogical point: validation can fail in fundamentally different ways. The data can be poorly split, the model can be too flexible or too simple, or the measurements can be too noisy. Diagnosing which failure you have is the heart of model validation.

Linearized Validation: A Change of Basis

Raw R² on nonlinear data can be misleading — exponential growth at late times inflates apparent goodness-of-fit. A more rigorous test is to linearize the model first, then check R² in the transformed space.

Divide both sides by the diffusion factor \(N_0(1 - e^{-D_{\text{fit}}\,t/L^2})\) and take the natural log:

$$y_{\text{lin}} = \ln\!\left(\frac{N_{\text{obs}}}{N_0\,(1 - e^{-D_{\text{fit}}\,t/L^2})}\right), \qquad y_{\text{pred}} = \mu_{\text{fit}}\,t$$

If the model is correct, the linearized data falls on a straight line through the origin with slope μ. Deviations from this line — especially a different slope for validation data — reveal model-data mismatch that raw R² can mask. All R² values in this module are computed in this linearized space.

Background: Reading Residual Patterns

A good fit produces residuals (observed − predicted) that are random, centered at zero, roughly normally distributed, and show no systematic pattern. Patterns in residuals indicate the model is missing real biology.

Three Residual Patterns to Recognize (Residuals vs Time)

  • Good fit (random scatter): Points scatter randomly around zero — no trend, no pattern. The model captured everything systematic; only random noise remains.
  • Wrong model (U-shaped): Residuals are negative at early and late times but positive in the middle. The model shape is wrong — perhaps missing a saturation term.
  • Heteroscedastic (trumpet): Residuals grow in magnitude with time. The noise is proportional to the signal — consider weighted least squares or log-transform.

Q-Q Plot: Is the Residual Distribution Normal?

A quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plot compares the distribution of your residuals to a standard normal distribution. Sort your residuals, standardize them (z-score), and plot each against its theoretical quantile from \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

  • Points on the diagonal \(y = x\): Residuals are approximately normal — least-squares assumptions hold, confidence intervals are trustworthy.
  • S-shaped curve: Residuals are heavy-tailed (more extreme values than normal predicts) — a few outliers are pulling the fit.
  • Curved (banana shape): Residuals are skewed — systematic asymmetry, often a sign the model systematically over- or under-predicts on one side.

Why this matters: The classic residuals-vs-predicted plot looks nearly identical to residuals-vs-time for any monotonic model (like this invasion model), so it adds little. A Q-Q plot probes a different property — the distribution of residuals rather than their temporal structure — and is much more informative here.

Key Equations — Residual Statistics

Residual: \(r_i = y_i^{\text{obs}} - y_i^{\text{pred}}\)

RMSE: \(\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n r_i^2}\) — in the units of the data (cells)

Mean residual: should be near zero. If not, the model is biased (systematic over/under-prediction).

Skewness: measures asymmetry of the residual distribution. \(|S| < 0.5\) suggests approximate normality.

Key check: compare training residuals to validation residuals. If validation RMSE is much larger than training RMSE, the model may be overfit.

Background: The Gold Standard Validation Plot

The prediction vs. observation plot is the single most important validation figure. Plot predicted values on the x-axis and observed values on the y-axis. Points on the 1:1 diagonal indicate perfect prediction. Deviation from the diagonal quantifies model error.

Reading the Plot

  • 1:1 line (dashed) — perfect prediction reference
  • ±10% band (darker shading) — points within this band are "good enough" for most applications
  • ±20% band (lighter shading) — acceptable for early-stage models
  • Training points (blue circles) — usually tighter clustering around the 1:1 line
  • Validation points (red squares) — if they scatter more widely, the model overfits

Key Statistics

Pearson correlation: \(r = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2}}\)

\(r\) measures correlation, R² measures explained variance, RMSE measures magnitude of error, and Bias measures systematic over/under-prediction.

Bias: \(\text{Bias} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i^{\text{obs}} - y_i^{\text{pred}})\) — positive means model underpredicts on average

95% prediction interval: Sample 200 parameter sets from the covariance matrix, compute predictions for each, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. This captures parameter uncertainty (how well we know \(D\) and \(\mu\)), not measurement noise.

Background: k-Fold Cross-Validation

Sometimes you don't have enough data to set aside a separate validation set. Cross-validation solves this by systematically holding out different subsets, refitting the model each time, and predicting the held-out points. The average prediction error across all folds gives an honest estimate of generalization.

The CV Procedure

  1. Partition \(n\) time points into \(k\) folds
  2. For fold \(j = 1, \ldots, k\): fit model on all data except fold \(j\), predict fold \(j\)
  3. Compute R² and RMSE for each fold
  4. Average across folds for the overall CV estimate

When to Use Cross-Validation

  • Small datasets where holding out a fixed validation set wastes too much data
  • When you want an honest estimate of prediction error without a separate test set
  • To check sensitivity: if one fold has a much lower R², investigate those time points

Key Equations

CV estimate of prediction error:

$$\text{CV-R}^2 = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^k R_j^2, \qquad \text{CV-RMSE} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^k \text{RMSE}_j$$

Std(R²): If the standard deviation across folds is large, the model is sensitive to which data is used — a warning sign.

Background: Interpolation vs. Extrapolation

The strongest form of validation tests the model under different experimental conditions than it was trained on. This is extrapolation — predicting outside the training range. It is where models most often fail, and where failure is most informative.

Domain of Validity

  • Interpolation: new conditions within the convex hull of training data. Expected to be reliable for well-calibrated models.
  • Extrapolation: new conditions outside the training range. Model predictions here depend on assumptions that have NOT been tested.

Why Extrapolation Fails

Our invasion model assumes constant \(D\) and \(\mu\). But at higher cell densities, contact inhibition may reduce proliferation (\(\mu\) decreases). At different Matrigel thicknesses, the invasion mechanism may change. These are model assumptions that only break when tested outside the training range.

Connection to DOE (Module 12)

If your initial experiment shows the model fails at new conditions, you now know exactly where to design your next experiment — in the region where the model breaks down. This is sequential design.

Background: When Validation Fails — Refining Your Model

Validation failure is not the end — it is the beginning of scientific insight. When your model fails on new data, the pattern of failure tells you exactly what is wrong with your assumptions. The goal is to diagnose the mismatch and choose the simplest refinement that fixes it.

Diagnosis Checklist

  1. Where does it fail? Early times, late times, or everywhere? If late-time divergence, a growth-related assumption is likely wrong. If early-time, an initial-condition or transport assumption is wrong.
  2. How does it fail? Systematic bias (model consistently over- or under-predicts) vs. increased scatter (more noise, but no trend)? Bias means model structure is wrong; scatter means conditions are noisier.
  3. Which parameters are sensitive? Use your sensitivity analysis (Module 8–9) to identify which parameters the model prediction depends on most. Those are the candidates for refinement.

Three Refinement Strategies

Strategy A — Re-fit to new data: Keep the same model form, fit parameters to the validation data alone. If R² recovers, the model form is correct — only the parameter values differ between conditions.

Strategy B — Pool all data: Combine training + validation data and re-fit. This produces a compromise fit. Useful when the two datasets represent the same population and you want maximum statistical power.

Strategy C — Shared + condition-specific parameters: Keep mechanistic parameters (like \(D\)) shared across conditions, but allow biological parameters (like \(\mu\)) to vary per condition. This is the gold standard: it separates physics (which should be universal) from biology (which can vary).

Which Remedy to Choose? (depends on the failure mode)

  • Improper split (data is fine, fit is biased): try Strategy B (Pool) first — pooling restores full time-range coverage. Strategy A is a diagnostic; Strategy C confirms biology is consistent across splits.
  • Overfitting (model too flexible, e.g. high-degree polynomial): the remedy is fewer parameters. Switch back to the mechanistic model. Adding more data won't help — the problem is model form.
  • Underfitting (model too simple, missing physics): the remedy is more structure. Restore the missing term (here, the diffusion lag). Adding more data won't help — you'd just fit the wrong model better.
  • High noise (data too noisy): no model change helps. Remedies live in the experimental design: more replicates, better instruments, cleaner protocols.
  • If none of the model-side remedies work, consider that the model form may need new variables (carrying capacity, density dependence) or a fundamentally different mechanism.
