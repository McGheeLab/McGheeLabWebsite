1.1 · The vacuum is a window, not a floor

Everyone's first instinct about a vacuum chamber is that lower is better — pump harder, get a better result. For a plasma cleaner that instinct is wrong in both directions. Harrick's FAQ puts the rule in one sentence: the chamber must be below 1500 mTorr for plasma to be generated and sustained at all, and they recommend operating from 200–800 mTorr.

There is a floor as well as a ceiling, and the reason is the distance an electron travels between collisions. Sweep the pressure below and watch the discharge come and go.

  1. Start at atmosphere and drag pressure down. Find the pressure at which the discharge first lights. Compare it to Harrick's 1500 mTorr ceiling.
  2. Keep going down to 10 mTorr. The glow dies again. Read the mean free path at that point and compare it to the 15 cm chamber.
  3. Park in the middle of the green band. Read the mean free path there — it should be a small fraction of the chamber, so an electron collides many times on its way across.
  4. Now switch the gas from air to argon. The window moves. Say which way, and why a heavier, larger atom should shift it.

Controls

Where a discharge will and will not light

Why there are two edges

The average distance a molecule travels between collisions is

eq. 4.1.1 $$\lambda \;=\; \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi d^{2} p}$$

with \(k_B\) Boltzmann's constant, \(T\) the gas temperature, \(d\) the kinetic collision diameter of the molecule and \(p\) the pressure. The only thing that matters here is \(\lambda \propto 1/p\): halve the pressure and everything travels twice as far before hitting something.

Too much gas — the upper edgeAt atmosphere λ is about 65 nm. An electron is knocked sideways almost immediately, so it never accumulates enough energy from the RF field to ionise anything. Plenty of collisions, none of them energetic. Nothing lights.
Too little gas — the lower edgeBelow about 10 mTorr λ becomes comparable with the chamber itself. Electrons now cross the whole chamber and hit the wall before meeting a molecule, so each one ionises nothing on the way. Plenty of energy, nothing to spend it on.
In between — a chain reactionAn electron gains energy over a mean free path, ionises, and the two electrons that result do it again. Harrick's 200–800 mTorr sits comfortably inside that band, with margin at both ends.
The pressure knob is not a "cleanliness" knob. It sets whether the discharge exists at all, and — as tab 2 shows — you do not set it with the pump. You set it with the flowmeter, against a pump that is running flat out the whole time.
Where the published guidance lands Note from Dr. McGhee. Harrick publishes the window (1500 mTorr ceiling, 200–800 mTorr recommended) but not the physics behind it — their site has no Paschen curve and no mean-free-path discussion. Equation 4.1.1 is standard kinetic theory, and the position of the lower edge in the sim is illustrative rather than a measured property of this chamber. The upper edge is Harrick's own number and is the one to obey. If you want the electron avalanche itself worked through, it is in Surface Functionalization lesson 2.

Dose, and the two things it is made of

What a surface receives is power multiplied by time:

eq. 4.1.2 $$D \;=\; P\,t$$

with \(P\) the RF power in watts and \(t\) the exposure in seconds, so \(D\) is in joules. 1.3 · Five things a plasma does to a surface

Harrick's physics page lists five distinct mechanisms, and which one dominates is set almost entirely by which gas you put in. Two of them are what people mean by "plasma cleaning"; the other three are why the machine is also a surface-modification tool.

  1. Start on oxygen. Read which mechanism dominates and what it produces.
  2. Switch to argon. The dominant mechanism changes completely. Read Harrick's stated reason for using argon.
  3. Switch to room air — the gas you get for free. Where does it sit relative to the other two?
  4. Using the panel, say which gas you would choose to clean a metal surface without oxidising it, and check your answer against Harrick's guidance in the verdict.

Controls

Which mechanism this gas is doing

Harrick's five mechanisms, in their words

Ablation"Mechanical removal of surface contaminants by energetic electron and ion bombardment." Affects the contaminant layers and the outermost molecular layers only. Argon is used "for its high ablation efficiency and chemical inertness".
Chemical etching"Chemical reaction of surface organic contaminants or films with highly reactive free radicals in the plasma to form volatile byproducts that are released from the sample surface." Oxygen is the standard choice.
ActivationPlasma "breaks down weak surface bonds in the polymer and replaces these surface polymer groups with chemical groups from the plasma gas, such as carbonyl, carboxyl, and hydroxyl groups" — this is the one PDMS bonding depends on.
DepositionPlasma polymerisation of a vaporised monomer into a thin coating. Not something this lab does, and it needs a monomer feed.
Cross-linkingAn inert-gas plasma links polymer chains together, "produc[ing] a stronger and harder substrate microsurface" with better wear and chemical resistance.

And the plasma doing all this is cold, in the only sense that matters to a sample. Harrick: plasma electrons sit "around 104 K" while "the plasma as a whole is at near-ambient temperature", with an electron density "typically around 1010 cm−3". Formally:

eq. 4.1.3 $$T_e \;\gg\; T_{\mathrm{ion}} \;\gtrsim\; T_{\mathrm{gas}} \;\approx\; 300\ \mathrm{K}$$

with \(T_e\) the electron temperature and \(T_{\mathrm{gas}}\) the temperature of everything heavy. The electrons carry chemistry-breaking energies — 104 K is about 0.9 eV, and plenty of the distribution sits well above that — while the gas your sample is actually sitting in never leaves room temperature. Harrick's FAQ puts a number on the consequence: a conductive sample may see about 5 °C of surface heating during treatment, and that is all.

That disequilibrium is the whole product. A gas hot enough to break a C–H bond would ordinarily melt PDMS. Here only the electrons are hot, there are only 1010 of them per cubic centimetre, and they have almost no heat capacity between them — so the chemistry happens and the part stays cool enough to pick up.
  • Pressure is a window, not a floor: below 1500 mTorr to strike, 200–800 mTorr recommended.
  • Both edges are the mean free path — too short and electrons never gain energy, too long and they never spend it.
  • This machine's LOW (30 W) is the standard model's HIGH. Every borrowed recipe needs converting.
  • Gas chooses mechanism: O₂ etches chemically, Ar ablates physically, and both activate.
Next: the two things you actually touch — a pump that never stops, and a flowmeter that turns out to be the pressure control.

2.1 · Getting the air out, and how long it honestly takes

The sequence never changes. Sample in, hold the door, pump on. The vacuum itself seals the door — Harrick's manual says so plainly, "the vacuum will hold the Plasma Cleaner door in place" — and you wait until the gauge is where you want it.

How long is that wait? It is a calculable number, and the calculation is worth doing because it tells you when something is wrong.

  1. Start with the oxygen-service scroll pump. Read the time to reach 200 mTorr. It is shorter than most people expect.
  2. Switch to the minimum pump Harrick will accept, 23 L/min. Read the time again — the ratio is exactly the ratio of the pumping speeds.
  3. Drag outgassing up. Watch the curve stop falling and flatten out. That floor is the pressure the chamber will actually sit at, and no amount of waiting improves it.
  4. Set outgassing high enough that the floor rises above 800 mTorr. What has that done to your ability to run the machine at all?
Pump:

Controls

Chamber pressure while the pump runs

An exponential with a floor

A chamber of volume \(V\) evacuated by a pump of speed \(S\), with a steady gas load \(Q\) from outgassing and leaks, obeys

eq. 4.2.1 $$p(t) \;=\; p_\infty + \left(p_0 - p_\infty\right)e^{-St/V}, \qquad p_\infty = \frac{Q}{S}$$

with \(p_0\) the starting pressure, \(p_\infty\) the floor it will asymptote to, and \(V/S\) the time constant. For this chamber — 6″ diameter by 6.5″ long, so 3.0 L — against a 50 L/min scroll pump, \(V/S\) is 3.6 seconds, and getting from atmosphere to 200 mTorr takes \(\ln(3800) \approx 8.2\) time constants, or about 30 seconds.

Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. The manual says pump-down takes "a few minutes", and that is longer than 30 seconds for good reasons this model leaves out: the ½″ hose has a finite conductance that limits the effective speed at the chamber, and a real pump's speed falls off as it approaches its ultimate pressure. Treat the number here as the best case and the manual's few minutes as the reality. The useful part of the model is not the absolute time — it is the floor. If the gauge stops falling well above 200 mTorr, that is \(Q/S\) telling you about a leak or a wet sample, and tab 4 is about finding it.

2.2 · The flowmeter is the pressure control

Here is the part that surprises people. Once the plasma is running, the pump never stops and the pump is not how you set the pressure. It runs flat out the whole time. The pressure in the chamber is set by how fast you let gas in against it — which means the PlasmaFlo's flowmeter knobs, not the vacuum, are your pressure control.

Set a flow below and watch where the chamber settles.

  1. Start with both channels closed. Read the pressure — this is the pump's floor, and the plasma will not run here.
  2. Open channel 1 (O₂) slowly. Find the setting that lands you in the middle of Harrick's 200–800 mTorr band.
  3. Open it all the way to the flowmeter's 50 mL/min full scale. Where does the pressure land relative to the top of the band? That is not a coincidence — read the note underneath.
  4. Now add channel 2 (Ar) on top. Two gases, one pressure. Note that mixing changes the chemistry without giving you an independent pressure knob.

Where the chamber settles, against total flow

Throughput in, throughput out

At steady state the gas going in equals the gas coming out. The pump removes volume at a rate \(S\), so it removes gas at a rate \(pS\); the flowmeters admit gas at a throughput \(Q\). Setting them equal:

eq. 4.2.2 $$Q \;=\; p\,S \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p \;=\; \frac{Q}{S}$$

with \(Q\) a throughput in pressure×volume per time — a flowmeter reading of 50 mL/min of atmospheric air is \(50 \times 760 = 38{,}000\) Torr·mL/min of gas. Divide by a 50 L/min pump, which is 50,000 mL/min, and you get 0.76 Torr.

The flowmeter's full scale is the top of the operating window. 760 mTorr against Harrick's recommended ceiling of 800. That is not luck — the PlasmaFlo is specified to be a sensible controller for exactly these cleaners, and its whole range maps onto the band the machine wants to run in. Practically: nearly all of the knob is usable, the bottom of the range is where the plasma gets thin, and you should never need to force it.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. Two honesty notes. The 50 mL/min figure is Harrick's spec for atmospheric air at 0 psig; the datasheet is explicit that "maximum flowrate varies with process gas and regulated pressure" and that the real correlated tables — for Air, Ar, N₂ and O₂ metered at 10 psig — are supplied on request rather than published. So the mL/min axis here is the air scale, and an oxygen reading of "20" is not 20 mL/min of oxygen. Second, the pressure the gauge shows you is the truth and the arithmetic is only a model: the thermocouple gauge reads 1–1999 mTorr and it is what you should set by.

2.3 · The sequence, and the one fiddly bit

Harrick's manual gives the operating order, and it is worth following exactly because two of the steps exist to protect the hardware rather than the sample.

1 · Purge the lineIf the gas line may hold anything unwanted: regulator to 10 psi, needle valve open, door off, purge a few minutes, then close the valve.
2 · Load and pump downSample in, hold the door against the chamber, pump on. The vacuum takes over holding the door. Wait for the gauge.
3 · Bleed gas inOpen the needle valve slowly and let the gas flow for a few minutes, so the chamber is running on your process gas rather than on whatever was left.
4 · Strike the plasmaClose the needle valve, switch the cleaner on, select the RF level, and wait for the glow. Harrick: "wait until a purplish glow is observed" — for air, the videos say purple-pink after 5–10 seconds.
5 · Re-open the gas, carefullyThe fiddly bit: "carefully open the needle valve just enough to bleed process gas into the reaction chamber without extinguishing the plasma." If it goes out, close the valve, let the plasma re-form, then open it again very slightly.
6 · Treat, then stopRF level to OFF, then the cleaner off.
7 · VentVacuum off, hold the door, vent for a few minutes, and only remove the door once you are back at atmospheric pressure. Then close the needle valve and take the sample out.
Step 5 is the whole skill. Striking a discharge needs a different pressure from running one comfortably, so you light it in a quiet chamber and then feed it without blowing it out. Nobody gets this on the first try, and the failure is completely harmless — the glow just stops, and you close the valve and start again.
Where the published guidance lands Note from Dr. McGhee. Harrick names a colour for air only: purplish, or purple-pink. They publish no colour chart for oxygen, argon or nitrogen anywhere on their site, and the colours quoted around the internet disagree with each other. So use the glow as a presence check — is it lit, is it steady, does it fill the chamber — and not as a gas identification, unless you have watched your own machine on a known gas and written down what you saw.
  • Pump-down is an exponential with a floor at Q/S. The floor, not the time, is the diagnostic.
  • The pump runs continuously. You do not set pressure with it.
  • p = Q/S — the flowmeter sets the pressure, and its full scale lands at the top of Harrick's window.
  • Strike the plasma in a closed chamber, then open the gas a little at a time. Losing it is harmless.
Next: the two clocks that decide whether the treatment was any good — one that runs while the plasma is on, and one that starts the moment it goes off.

3.1 · More treatment is not more bonding

The intuition to unlearn: if 30 seconds activates the surface, two minutes must activate it more. Harrick's guidance is blunt about it — "Plasma treatment should not exceed 2 min, as prolonged plasma exposure causes cracking in PDMS and migration of low molecular mass molecules from bulk to surface, decreasing the number of hydrophilic SiOH groups and resulting in weak or incomplete bonding."

What makes this genuinely dangerous is that the measurement you would naturally reach for does not catch it. Run the dose up and watch both readouts.

  1. Start on Harrick's recipe, HIGH for 30 s. Read the contact angle and the oxide skin thickness.
  2. Drag time to 120 s — Harrick's stated ceiling. Watch the contact angle. It barely moves.
  3. Now watch the oxide skin over the same drag, and the crack verdict underneath. This is the damage, and the contact angle never told you about it.
  4. Go to 180 s. Then drop to LOW and find the time that gives the same dose. Is the skin the same? Say what that tells you about which knob to reach for.

Controls

What the dose buys, and what it costs

Two curves, and only one of them is visible

Wettability improves fast and then saturates. A simple exponential approach captures it:

eq. 4.3.1 $$\theta(D) \;=\; \theta_{\min} + \left(\theta_0 - \theta_{\min}\right)e^{-D/D_c}$$

with \(\theta_0 = 113°\) the native PDMS contact angle, \(\theta_{\min} \approx 7°\) the floor a good treatment reaches, \(D\) the dose from eq. 4.1.2 and \(D_c\) a characteristic dose of a few hundred joules. By 30 seconds at HIGH the exponential is essentially finished. Everything after that point is dose you are spending for nothing.

Underneath it, the oxidised silica-like layer keeps thickening the whole time, roughly as the square root of dose, because it is a diffusion-limited oxidation. That layer is stiff and the rubber underneath it is not, and the mismatch is what cracks.

The contact angle cannot tell you that you have over-treated. The literature makes the same point starkly: two minutes of air plasma takes PDMS to about 6.8°, and thirty minutes takes it to 13.7° — worse, but only slightly, and nowhere near enough to look like failure. Meanwhile the surface has become a cracked, brittle skin that bonds badly. If you are judging treatment by how well water spreads, you cannot see the failure mode that matters.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. The oxide-thickness curve here is calibrated so that the crack threshold lands on Harrick's own two-minute rule at HIGH on this machine — it is a way of turning their instruction into a number you can reason with, not an independent measurement. Published skin thicknesses for plasma-treated PDMS run from a few nanometres to over a hundred depending on dose and technique, so treat the absolute nanometres as indicative and the rule as the thing to obey. What is not in doubt is the shape: activation saturates, damage does not.

3.2 · The clock starts when the plasma stops

A plasma-treated surface is not a finished product, it is a temporary state. The silanol groups that make bonding possible begin disappearing the moment you vent the chamber, as low-molecular-weight chains migrate up from the bulk and polar groups rotate away from the surface. Harrick's instruction: perform bonding within 15 minutes to an hour, and the surface recovers its hydrophobic character in about an hour.

And here the trap from §3.1 repeats itself, backwards. Watch the two curves separate.

  1. Drag delay to zero. Both readouts are at their best, which is what Harrick means by bringing the surfaces together straight away.
  2. Drag delay to 60 minutes — the far end of Harrick's window. Read the contact angle. It has hardly changed.
  3. Now read bondability at the same 60 minutes. It has collapsed. The surface still looks perfectly wettable and will no longer weld.
  4. Switch to stored under water and drag out to several hours. Storage buys you time on one curve — check whether it buys the same time on the other.
After treatment:

Controls

Wettability and bondability do not decay together

Two processes, two timescales

Both recoveries are exponential and they have very different time constants:

eq. 4.3.2 $$\theta(t) = \theta_{\mathrm{rec}} - \left(\theta_{\mathrm{rec}} - \theta_0\right)e^{-t/\tau_\theta} \qquad\qquad B(t) = e^{-t/\tau_B}$$

with \(\theta_{\mathrm{rec}}\) the recovered contact angle, \(\tau_\theta\) of order tens of hours, and \(B\) the fraction of bonding capability remaining with \(\tau_B\) of order tens of minutes. The contact angle is a bulk-ish property that takes a day or more to come back; bondability lives in the outermost monolayer and is gone far sooner.

This is why "it looked fine" is not a defence. An hour after treatment your PDMS still wets beautifully and will not bond. The reverse mistake is rarer but real: a part stored under water can stay bondable much longer than the bench-top rule suggests, which is why cold, wet storage is the standard trick for buying time.
Where the published guidance lands Note from Dr. McGhee. Harrick gives one number — bond within 15 minutes to an hour, recovery in about an hour — and the literature gives a longer and more detailed picture: surfaces stay water-wet for the first several hours and take a couple of days to recover fully, with recovery slower at 4 °C than at 20 °C and slower again under water or saline. Both are right because they are measuring different things, and this sim shows them as two curves for exactly that reason. Harrick's window is the one to work to. Storage under water is a literature result, not a Harrick instruction — treat it as a way to buy margin, not as a licence to plan around it.

3.3 · The rules that are about the machine, not the sample

Everything above is about getting a good result. These are the ones where the cost of being wrong is a broken chamber, a ruined pump or an injury. They come straight out of Harrick's manual.

Never open the door under vacuumRepeated three times in the manual: "Do not open the door when the chamber is under vacuum since this will damage the glass chamber." Vent first, get back to atmosphere, then remove the door.
Vent immediately after you finish"If the Plasma Cleaner chamber is not vented immediately, oil may back stream from the vacuum pump and contaminate the system." A chamber left under vacuum on a stopped pump is a chamber being slowly coated.
Oxygen needs an oxygen-service pump"The hydrocarbon pump oil mist can react with the ozone formed producing a potentially explosive combination." This lab runs a Harrick oxygen-service pump — dry scroll, no hydrocarbon oil — so this requirement is met. It is met because somebody bought the right pump, and it stops being met the moment anyone substitutes a spare oil pump "just for today".
Nothing flammable nearby"Do not use the Plasma Cleaner near flammable materials." An RF discharge and solvent vapour are a bad pairing, and the chamber is not the only place a plasma can strike.
Toxic gases need an exhaust filterThe manual calls out CF₄ by name: gases that ionise to toxic products need "an efficient exhaust filter". If you are contemplating a gas that is not air, O₂, Ar or N₂, that is a conversation before it is an experiment.
7 amps into the pump outlet, no moreThe cleaner has an integral switched outlet for the pump, and it is fused for 7 A. A larger pump goes in the wall, not in the machine.
Two hours, then stopHarrick: "We recommend continuously operating for no longer than 2 hours at a time", then turn the main power off. The electronics are air-cooled and the chamber warms from them, not from the plasma.
McGhee Lab practice — this is a gap, not guidance Note from Dr. McGhee. Harrick publishes nothing on several things you might reasonably expect: room ventilation for the ozone an oxygen plasma makes, UV exposure from the glow, RF emissions, a list of materials or solvents that must not go in the chamber, or guidance on biological samples. Their silence is not permission. Until this lab writes its own rule, the defensible defaults are: run the machine in a well-ventilated space, do not stare into the chamber for extended periods, and put nothing in it that is wet, volatile, or that you have not thought about — a sample that outgasses is a sample that will not let the chamber reach pressure anyway, which tab 4 covers.
  • Activation saturates; damage does not. Past about 30 s at HIGH you are only accumulating oxide.
  • The contact angle cannot see over-treatment — 2 min gives 6.8°, 30 min gives 13.7°, and the second one is cracked.
  • Bondability decays in tens of minutes; wettability takes a day. "It still looks wet" is not evidence.
  • Never open the door under vacuum, and vent the moment you are done or the pump backstreams into your chamber.

4.1 · No glow: it is one of two things

Harrick's answer to the most common fault is a single sentence, and it is more useful than its length suggests: "these issues are usually caused by either a problem with the electrical circuit or vacuum pressure where the chamber is not within the proper pressure range."

Two branches, and a test that separates them in under a minute. Break the machine below and work out which branch you are on.

  1. Start on nothing wrong and confirm the glow is there. Note what both tests say when the machine is healthy.
  2. Switch to chamber never got below 1500 mTorr. Run both tests. Which one changed?
  3. Switch to a blown fuse. Run both tests again. Notice that the symptom in the chamber is identical and the tests are not.
  4. Switch to gas cylinder empty — a third cause with the same symptom. Say which test catches it and why the fluorescent bulb does not.

4.2 · When the gauge stops falling

The other half of §2.1's equation. If pump-down flattens out above where it should, something is adding gas as fast as the pump removes it — a leak, a wet sample, or a chamber that has been soaking up humidity. The floor tells you how big the problem is, and the pump's own ultimate pressure tells you how much of it is not your fault.

  1. Start on a healthy system. Read the floor and compare it to the pump's own ultimate pressure.
  2. Switch to o-ring with debris on it. Read the new floor. Can you still operate?
  3. Switch to a wet sample. Same floor problem, completely different fix — and note the floor drifts down as it dries.
  4. With a leak selected, drag pumping speed up. A bigger pump helps, but read how much. Say why buying your way out of a leak is a bad trade.

The floor, against how hard you pump

You cannot out-pump a leak

eq. 4.4.1 $$p_\infty \;=\; p_{\mathrm{pump}} + \frac{Q}{S}$$

with \(p_{\mathrm{pump}}\) the pump's own ultimate pressure, \(Q\) the total gas load from leaks and outgassing, and \(S\) the pumping speed. The leak term falls only as \(1/S\), so doubling the pump halves that contribution and no more — while fixing the leak removes it entirely. A machine that needs its big pump to reach operating pressure is a machine with a leak somebody has decided to live with.

Check the cheap things firstHarrick's own order: connections at the chamber outlet, the vacuum hose, and the pump inlet — with a nut driver on the hose clamps. Then the chamber for cracks and the o-ring for debris.

No vacuum greaseHarrick's installation video is explicit: "Do NOT use Vacuum Grease on any of the vacuum connections." It is the obvious thing to reach for and it is wrong here.
A wet sample is not a leakSame symptom, and it gets better on its own as the water pumps away. If the floor drifts down over a few minutes rather than sitting still, you are watching something dry — give it time rather than dismantling the plumbing.

5.1 · Bond a PDMS chip to glass

This is what the machine is for in this lab. A cured PDMS slab with channels in it, a glass coverslip, and one minute of plasma standing between them and a device that holds pressure.

Harrick's recipe is the only complete one they publish, and it is the answer key here: O₂ or room air, 200 mTorr – 1 Torr, RF typically HIGH, 15–60 seconds, then contact immediately, press and hold lightly for 30 seconds, do not pull apart and realign, and finish at 80–100 °C for 60 seconds. Set it up below and see what comes out.

  2. Switch to left it too long in the chamber. The treatment is more thorough and the bond is worse. Name the mechanism from §3.1.
  3. Switch to went to get coffee. Same treatment, mated an hour later. Read what survived.
  4. Go back to the good recipe and drag pressure outside Harrick's band in both directions. One end fails loudly and one end fails quietly — say which is which.
How it went:

The run

Where the bond strength went

Four things multiply

Bond strength is not the sum of your choices, it is the product of them — each one is a fraction of the best case, and any single one near zero takes the whole bond with it:

eq. 4.5.1 $$\sigma \;=\; \sigma_{\max}\; f(\theta)\; B(t_{\mathrm{delay}})\; g(\mathrm{skin})\; h(p)$$

with \(\sigma_{\max} \approx 510\) kPa the best PDMS–glass bond reported in the literature, \(f(\theta)\) how far the contact angle got (eq. 4.3.1), \(B\) the bondability left after the delay (eq. 4.3.2), \(g\) the penalty from an over-thick oxide skin, and \(h\) whether you were in the pressure window at all. Multiplying is the right model because these are not independent costs you can trade off — a surface that never activated cannot be rescued by mating it quickly.

The two failures that look like success are the expensive ones. Under-treatment announces itself: the parts fall apart in your hand. Over-treatment and delay both produce a chip that looks bonded, goes into the oven, comes out, and then delaminates under pressure a day later with cells in it. Everything in tabs 1 to 4 exists so that you can tell those two apart before you find out the expensive way.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. The 510 kPa figure and the contact-angle relationship come from Bhattacharya and colleagues' 2005 blister-test study, which found the strongest PDMS–glass bonds at contact angles below 5°. Worth knowing that they reached those angles at lower RF power over longer times than Harrick's "typically HIGH", and reported an optimum near 100 mTorr. Both routes arrive at the same low contact angle; Harrick's is the faster one, and on this machine — where HIGH is 45 W — the fast route reaches the two-minute ceiling sooner than it would on the unit most published recipes were written for. When in doubt on this machine, shorten the time before you raise the power.

Check yourself — the competency set

1. A paper you are copying says "60 s oxygen plasma at high power" and reports lovely bonds. You run exactly that on our machine and the chips delaminate. What is the most likely explanation, and what would you change first?

Answer

Their HIGH was almost certainly not your HIGH. If they used a PDC-001 or a comparable unit, HIGH is 30 W and 60 s delivers about 1800 J. On ours HIGH is 45 W, so the same instruction delivers 2700 J — half as much again, and well past the point where activation saturated. You have over-treated, grown a thicker oxide skin and produced a surface that looks fine and bonds badly. The first change is to drop to LOW (30 W) for 60 s, which reproduces their dose exactly. Shorten the time before you touch the power.

2. The chamber will not go below 900 mTorr, and it used to reach 80. Walk through the diagnosis in order.

Answer

The floor has risen, so from eq. 4.4.1 either \(p_{\mathrm{pump}}\) or \(Q\) has changed. Watch it for a couple of minutes first: if it drifts down, something is drying and you should wait rather than dismantle. If it sits still, work Harrick's order from cheapest: hose connections at the chamber outlet and the pump inlet, the door seating, the o-ring for debris, then the chamber for cracks. Note 900 mTorr is below the 1500 mTorr ceiling, so a plasma may well still strike — you would be running out of the recommended band without an obvious symptom, which is exactly the kind of slow drift that makes results stop reproducing.

3. Someone needs the machine for a solvent-cleaning job and the oxygen-service pump is away being serviced. There is a spare oil-sealed rotary vane pump on the shelf that meets the speed and ultimate-pressure spec. Can they use it?

Answer

For air, N₂ or argon — yes, an oil pump meeting ≥23 L/min and ≤200 mTorr is exactly what Harrick specifies for non-reactive gases. For oxygen — no. Harrick's warning is unambiguous: "The hydrocarbon pump oil mist can react with the ozone formed producing a potentially explosive combination." Meeting the speed spec is irrelevant to that hazard. Either run the job on air or argon, or wait for the oxygen-service pump. And whoever swaps the pump back needs to know which one is on the bench, because the machine will happily run either.
