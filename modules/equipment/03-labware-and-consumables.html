1.1 · Two flasks, identical plastic, and cells only grow in one

Take two polystyrene flasks off the shelf. Chemically they are the same polymer, moulded the same way. Put cells in both and one grows a monolayer while the other keeps them in sad floating clumps. Corning puts it plainly: "Most flasks are made of untreated polystyrene, which isn't always optimal for some cell lines", and the fix is a treatment that makes the surface "more hydrophilic to encourage cell attachment".

That word — hydrophilic — is the whole mechanism, and you can watch it. Drop a bead of medium on the surface below and change what the surface is.

  1. Start on untreated polystyrene. Read the contact angle and the fraction of cells that have attached after a day.
  2. Switch to tissue-culture treated. The polymer underneath has not changed. Read both numbers again.
  3. Switch to ultra-low attachment. Attachment collapses — and that is the point of that surface. Name an experiment you would want it for.
  4. Go back to untreated and drag serum from 0% to 20%. Serum partly rescues attachment on a bad surface. Using eq. 3.1.2, say what the serum is actually doing.

Wetting, in one equation

Where a drop meets a solid, three surface tensions pull on the contact line and balance:

eq. 3.1.1 $$\gamma_{sv} \;=\; \gamma_{sl} + \gamma_{lv}\cos\theta$$

with \(\gamma_{sv}\), \(\gamma_{sl}\) and \(\gamma_{lv}\) the solid–vapour, solid–liquid and liquid–vapour surface tensions and \(\theta\) the contact angle. A high-energy solid (large \(\gamma_{sv}\)) forces \(\cos\theta\) up, so \(\theta\) goes down and the drop spreads. Plasma treatment is exactly this: it oxidises the top few nanometres of polystyrene, planting carboxyl and hydroxyl groups that raise \(\gamma_{sv}\). Nothing else about the flask changes.

The reason a wettable surface grows cells is one step further on. How strongly a liquid — and the proteins in it — adheres to a solid is the work of adhesion:

eq. 3.1.2 $$W_a \;=\; \gamma_{lv}\left(1 + \cos\theta\right)$$

the energy per unit area you would have to supply to peel the liquid back off. On an untreated surface at \(\theta = 90°\), \(\cos\theta = 0\) and \(W_a = \gamma_{lv}\). Treat it to 40° and \(W_a\) nearly doubles. Cells do not attach to plastic directly — they attach to a layer of adsorbed proteins (fibronectin and vitronectin, mostly from the serum), and that layer forms and stays put in proportion to \(W_a\).

Serum is not just food. This is why adding serum partly rescues attachment on a poor surface: you are supplying more of the adhesion proteins that have to land before any cell can. It is also why serum-free work usually needs a coated surface — Corning's BioCoat range with collagen, gelatin or fibronectin does the same job by putting the protein there deliberately instead of hoping it adsorbs.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. Ultra-low attachment surfaces are the same physics run backwards: a covalently bound hydrophilic hydrogel that proteins will not adsorb to, so nothing gets a foothold. That is not a defect, it is the product — it is how you force cells into spheroids and organoids instead of a monolayer. If you order the wrong one of these two you will spend a week blaming your cells.

1.2 · Six plastics, and why the tube is that one

Labware is not made of "plastic". It is made of a specific polymer chosen for a specific compromise, and the compromises conflict: the ones you can see through mostly cannot be autoclaved, and the ones you can autoclave are mostly cloudy. Pick a material below and a condition to put it in.

  1. Start on polystyrene at 121 °C. Read what happens. Now ask why every flask you own is made of it anyway.
  2. Switch to polypropylene at the same temperature. This is why tubes and tips are PP and flasks are PS.
  3. Set the temperature to −80 °C and step through PS, PP and PET. Corning's own guidance on which survives freezing is in the verdict — check whether the sim agrees with it.
  4. Find the one material that survives everything, then look at what it costs you.

  • Cells attach to adsorbed protein, not to plastic — so wettability and serum both act on the same step.
  • Ultra-low attachment is the same physics inverted, and it is a product feature rather than a fault.
  • Clarity, temperature range, chemical resistance and protein binding conflict. Every piece of labware is a choice among them.
Next: two numbers people habitually get wrong — the one on the centrifuge dial, and the volume of medium in a well.

2.1 · "Spin at 3000 rpm" is not a protocol

Corning is blunt about this, and it is worth quoting in full: "Instructions for centrifuging a sample at a given RPM and time are incomplete unless the rotor or radius is specified. Protocols should always state the time and RCF value for centrifuging a sample." And, separately: "Do not confuse speed or revolutions per minute (RPM) with RCF."

The reason is one line of algebra. Set a speed below and then change the rotor without touching the dial.

  1. Set 3000 rpm on the small benchtop rotor. Read the RCF.
  2. Now switch to the large swing-out rotor without changing the speed. Read it again. Same dial, same protocol as written, and a different experiment.
  3. Go back to the small rotor and find the speed that reproduces the large rotor's RCF.
  4. Drag the speed up until you exceed the tube's rating. Note how much of the dial's range is actually usable — and that a rotor which can reach a speed does not mean your tube can.

The formula, and the trap inside it

eq. 3.2.1 $$\mathrm{RCF} \;=\; 1.118\times10^{-5}\;r\,N^{2}$$

with \(r\) the radius in centimetres and \(N\) the speed in rpm, exactly as Corning writes it. Two things are hiding in there. First, RCF goes as \(N^{2}\), so a 20% speed increase is a 44% force increase — the dial is far more sensitive than it looks. Second, and the one that bites: \(r\) is measured to the bottom of the tube, not to the middle of the rotor, not to the sample. Two rotors that both say "3000 rpm" deliver forces in the ratio of their radii, and that ratio is easily two to one.

Write the RCF in the notebook, never the rpm. An rpm is a property of one machine on one day. An RCF is a property of the experiment, and it is the only form in which a spin is reproducible by anyone else — or by you, on the other centrifuge, in six months.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. Corning's other centrifuge rules are short and all worth obeying: tubes are single use — hairline cracks and deformation happen even within acceptable conditions; balance opposing buckets and distribute them about the centre of rotation and the pivot axis; never autoclave a centrifuge tube, because it accentuates any deformation and the tube then leaks or fails on the next spin; and close the cap by pressing down while turning it backwards until the threads click, then tightening — which is what stops cross-threading.

2.2 · How deep is the medium, and can the cells breathe?

Choosing a vessel looks like choosing a growth area. It is really choosing three things at once: how many cells you will end up with, how much medium sits on top of them, and therefore how easily oxygen reaches them. The third one is invisible and it is the one that goes wrong when somebody "adds a bit more medium so it does not dry out".

  1. Start on the T-25 flask with its standard medium volume. Read the depth and the oxygen delivery.
  2. Double the medium volume. Growth area unchanged, cells unchanged. Watch the oxygen number.
  3. Switch to a 96-well plate and read the depth. Small vessels are shallow, which is good for gas and terrible for evaporation — tab 3 is about the second half of that.
  4. Using the confluence figure in the stats, work out how many T-25 flasks you would need to seed a 6-well plate at 20% confluency.
Vessel:

Controls

Growth to confluence, and the medium on top

Depth is volume over area, and oxygen has to cross it

eq. 3.2.2 $$h \;=\; \frac{V}{A} \qquad\qquad J_{\mathrm{O_2}} \;=\; D\,\frac{\Delta C}{h}$$

with \(h\) the medium depth, \(V\) the volume, \(A\) the growth area, \(D\) the diffusion coefficient of oxygen in medium and \(\Delta C\) the concentration difference between the surface and the cell layer. In a static flask nothing stirs, so oxygen arrives by diffusion alone and the supply is inversely proportional to depth. Double the medium and you halve the oxygen delivery to the cells at the bottom.

More medium is not more generous. It is more buffer and more nutrient, and it is less oxygen — and for a dense culture oxygen is usually the thing that runs out first. The standard volumes on a vessel's datasheet are chosen to land near 2–3 mm of depth for exactly this reason. If a culture needs feeding more often, feed it more often; do not make it deeper.

Corning's rule for when to passage is 85–95% confluency — "aim to passage cells at moderate to less than 85-95 percent confluency when there's still space to grow", because beyond that "cells may be changed irreparably". The sim above tells you when you get there, and it is worth noticing how short the window is: a culture at 40% on Monday morning can be past 95% by Tuesday afternoon.

  • RCF, not rpm. An rpm without a radius is not a protocol, and RCF goes as the square of speed.
  • Radius is measured to the bottom of the tube. Two rotors at the same dial setting can differ two-fold.
  • Medium depth is V/A, and oxygen delivery goes as 1/h. Extra medium starves cells of oxygen.
  • Passage at 85–95% confluency, not after — past that, cells change irreversibly.
Next: two ways your sample disappears without anybody spilling anything — out through the top of the plate, and into the wall of the tube.

3.1 · The outer wells are a different experiment

A 96-well plate looks like ninety-six identical experiments. It is not. The wells around the outside lose water faster than the ones in the middle, because they have more exposed perimeter and sit against the plate's cooler walls. Over a few days the outer ring concentrates — the medium in them is not the medium you made.

This is the edge effect, and it is the single most common reason a plate shows a beautiful, meaningless gradient from left to right.

  1. Start at day 3 in a normal incubator. Read the volume lost from a corner well and from a centre well.
  2. Drag days out to 7. Watch the map. At what point does the outer ring stop being usable?
  3. Drop incubator humidity from 95% to 70% — a door left ajar, or a dry pan. Read the corner well again.
  4. Tick sacrifice the outer ring. You lose 36 of 96 wells and gain a plate you can believe. Decide whether that trade is worth it for a 7-day experiment.

Volume lost per well, seen from above

Evaporation, and why the corner is worst

Water leaves a well by diffusing through the air above it into an incubator that is not quite saturated:

eq. 3.3.1 $$\frac{dV}{dt} \;=\; -\,k\,A_{\mathrm{well}}\left(1 - \mathrm{RH}\right)\,f_{\mathrm{pos}}$$

with \(A_{\mathrm{well}}\) the open area of the well, RH the incubator's relative humidity as a fraction, \(k\) a mass-transfer coefficient, and \(f_{\mathrm{pos}}\) a position factor greater than one for wells on the perimeter, which have extra exposed plate around them. The \((1-\mathrm{RH})\) is the whole reason the incubator has a water pan — and why a pan allowed to run dry turns into a plate-wide artefact three days later.

The damage is concentration, not volume. Losing 30% of the volume from a corner well raises the concentration of everything dissolved in it by 43%, including your drug, the salt and the buffer. The cells in that well are in a different medium from the cells in the middle, and the plate reader cannot tell you that.
McGhee Lab practice — confirm before you rely on it Note from Dr. McGhee. For anything running longer than about three days, fill the outer ring with sterile PBS and do not use it. You lose 36 wells of a 96 and you get 60 you can compare with each other, which is the better trade almost every time. The alternative — a plate sealed with a gas-permeable membrane — works too, and is what to reach for when you cannot spare the wells. This is house practice; the underlying physics is standard, but the three-day threshold is a rule of thumb rather than anything measured here.

3.2 · Your protein is on the wall of the tube

Put one microgram of a purified protein into a 15 mL conical, and a measurable fraction of it never comes out again. It is not degraded and it did not leak — it adsorbed onto the polypropylene, in the same monolayer-of-protein process that §1.1 relied on to make cells stick.

Whether that matters depends entirely on the ratio of wall area to sample amount, and that ratio is something you choose when you pick the tube.

  1. Start with 1 µg in a 15 mL conical. Read the fraction lost. Most people guess far too low.
  2. Switch to 1 µg in a 0.5 mL tube — same sample, smaller container. Read it again.
  3. Tick low-bind surface. Compare the two fixes: a smaller tube, and a better tube.
  4. Drag the sample amount up to 1 mg in the big conical. Where does the problem go, and why?

Controls

Fraction lost to the wall, against sample amount

A monolayer is a fixed amount, so what matters is the ratio

Once the wall is saturated it holds a fixed mass per unit area, so the fraction of your sample that vanishes is

eq. 3.3.2 $$f_{\mathrm{lost}} \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\;\frac{\Gamma\,A_{\mathrm{wet}}}{m}\right)$$

with \(\Gamma\) the saturated surface coverage — a couple of milligrams per square metre for a typical protein on a hydrophobic surface — \(A_{\mathrm{wet}}\) the wetted wall area, and \(m\) the total mass of protein present. The numerator is set by the tube and the denominator by the sample. Nothing about this depends on concentration once you are above saturation, which is why dilute samples suffer and concentrated ones do not.

A big tube is not a safe default. The wetted area of a 15 mL conical holding 1 mL is several times that of a 1.5 mL microtube holding the same millilitre, and the protein does not know it is supposed to stay in the liquid. For anything under about ten micrograms, use the smallest tube that holds the volume — and for anything precious, a low-bind tube, which is the same trick as §1.1's ultra-low attachment surface applied to protein.
  • Outer wells evaporate faster. Over days this becomes a concentration gradient across the plate that looks like a result.
  • Losing 30% of the volume raises every concentration in that well by 43%.
  • Wall adsorption is a fixed mass per area, so it hurts small samples and ignores large ones.
  • Two fixes, and they stack: a smaller tube (less area) and a low-bind surface (less Γ).
Next: the autoclave — what it kills, and the thing it does almost nothing to.

4.1 · Sterile is not the same as clean

An autoclave kills things. It is extremely good at it — a standard 121 °C, 15-minute cycle reduces a bacterial population by more than twelve orders of magnitude. What it does not do is remove what the bacteria left behind.

Endotoxin is lipopolysaccharide from the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria, shed while they grow and dumped wholesale when they die. Corning is unambiguous: "Autoclaving glassware using standard procedures has little effect on endotoxin levels due to their high heat stability", and destroying it needs "250 °C for more than 30 minutes or 180 °C for three hours". Run both cycles below and compare the two bars.

  1. Start on the standard autoclave cycle. Read the microbial log reduction, then read the endotoxin reduction underneath it.
  2. Switch to depyrogenation, 250 °C. Both bars fill. Note how long it takes.
  3. Switch to gamma irradiation — the process most of your disposable plastic actually goes through. Same story, different mechanism.
  4. Set a custom cycle and find the shortest time at 180 °C that completes depyrogenation. Check it against Corning's three hours.

Controls

What this cycle actually achieves

Two different kinds of killing

Moist-heat sterilisation is measured in \(F_0\) — minutes of equivalent lethality at 121 °C:

eq. 3.4.1 $$F_0 \;=\; \int 10^{\,(T-121)/z}\,dt \qquad z \approx 10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$$

with \(T\) in °C and \(z\) the temperature change that alters the kill rate ten-fold. A \(z\) of 10 °C is why 134 °C for three minutes matches 121 °C for a quarter of an hour: thirteen degrees buys you a factor of twenty in time.

Note that \(F_0\) describes moist heat. Above about 140 °C there is no steam left and you are doing dry-heat sterilisation, which is a different mechanism with its own kinetics — the sim switches to it and stops quoting an \(F_0\), because extrapolating the moist-heat curve to 250 °C would claim dry heat is a trillion times more lethal than an autoclave, which is the opposite of the truth.

Endotoxin destruction is a different chemistry with a much larger \(z\). Take Corning's two published equivalences — 250 °C for 30 minutes, and 180 °C for 180 minutes — as the same treatment and solve for \(z\):

eq. 3.4.2 $$10^{\,(250-180)/z} \;=\; \frac{180}{30} \;=\; 6 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z \;=\; \frac{70}{\log_{10} 6} \;\approx\; 90\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$$

That \(z\) of about 90 °C, nine times the microbial one, is the whole problem. Because the exponent falls off so slowly with temperature, going down from 250 °C to 121 °C costs you a factor of \(10^{129/90} \approx 27\) in rate — so a 15-minute autoclave cycle delivers around 2% of a depyrogenation treatment. "Little effect", quantified.

Gamma and e-beam are worse, not better. Corning again: radiation sterilisation "destroys microbial contaminants and will leave the endotoxins largely intact". Your disposable plastic is sterile because it was irradiated — which tells you nothing at all about whether it is pyrogen-free. That is a separate certification, and if it matters to your assay you have to look for it on the box.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. The \(z \approx 90\) °C above is derived here from Corning's own two data points rather than quoted from a standard, and two points define a straight line whether or not the underlying kinetics are one. Treat it as what it is: an interpolation that reproduces both published cycles exactly and gives the right order of magnitude in between. For a real depyrogenation validation you use the published cycle, not this curve. And note it applies to glassware — you cannot put plastic anywhere near 250 °C, which is why plasticware has to be bought certified low-endotoxin rather than treated.

4.2 · Freezing cells is a rate, not a temperature

Getting cells to −130 °C is easy. Getting them back alive is a question of how fast you went. Too slow and they sit in a concentrating brine and dehydrate to death; too fast and water freezes inside them and the crystals tear them apart. Corning's number is 1 °C to 3 °C per minute, and there is a real optimum in between the two failure modes.

  1. Start at a controlled-rate container. Read the cooling rate and the survival.
  2. Switch to straight into the −80. Faster is not better — read which failure mode took over.
  3. Switch to straight into liquid nitrogen. Then read the aside below on why this is also a safety problem, not only a viability one.
  4. Set the rate back to 1 °C/min and drag DMSO from 0% to 15%. Cryoprotectant moves the whole curve; find where Corning's 5–15% band sits on it.

The rest of the cryo rules, and why each one exists

1–3 °C per minuteSlow enough to let water leave the cell, fast enough that it does not sit dehydrating. Corning notes that "cells that are larger or have less permeable membranes may require a slower rate" — big cells take longer to dehydrate.
DMSO 5–15%, added at 4 °CIt is toxic at room temperature with prolonged contact, so it goes in cold and comes out immediately on thawing. Glycerol, 5–20%, is gentler but causes osmotic problems on thaw and must be removed slowly by dilution.
Fill to 90%, never moreCorning's rule for any tube that will be frozen. Water expands; the space is where it goes.
Never a Styrofoam rackCorning's exact reasoning: contents hidden under the foam freeze later, so the liquid freezes from the top down, "the force escape area is lost and the tube is much more likely to break when the lower liquid freezes".
−80 °C overnight, then vapour phaseLong-term storage below −130 °C. Never submerge vials directly in liquid nitrogen — it is a contamination route, and the trapped nitrogen makes vials explode on warming.
Thaw fast: 60–90 s at 37 °CThe mirror image of freezing. Rapid warming gives ice no time to re-form into large crystals. Then get the DMSO out immediately.
Freezing and thawing are asymmetric on purpose. Freeze slowly so water has time to leave the cell; thaw quickly so it has no time to crystallise on the way back. Doing either one at the other's speed is a standard way to lose a stock, and it looks exactly like "the cells were unhealthy".
Check yourself

1. A colleague autoclaves glassware, rinses it with ultrapure water, and reports that their primary cells still respond badly. What have they not ruled out?

Answer

Endotoxin. Autoclaving delivers about 2% of a depyrogenation cycle (eq. 3.4.2), and endotoxin adheres strongly to glass — Corning notes it is "difficult to completely remove the strongly adhered endotoxins during washing". The glassware is sterile and may still be pyrogenic. The fixes are 250 °C for over 30 minutes or 180 °C for three hours, or certified low-endotoxin disposables. Water is also worth checking: Corning calls it "possibly the highest risk material for endotoxin contamination", and an LAL assay settles it.

2. Vials frozen in a foam rack in the −80 keep cracking. Why does the rack matter, and what should be used instead?

Answer

The foam insulates the lower part of each vial, so the exposed top freezes first. Once a plug of ice caps the tube, the liquid below has nowhere to expand into when it freezes in turn, and the tube splits. Use a controlled-rate cooling container or an uninsulated metal rack, and fill only to 90% of nominal volume — both are Corning's own instructions, and the second one is the reason the fill line matters.

  • Autoclaving sterilises and does almost nothing to endotoxin — about 2% of a depyrogenation cycle.
  • Gamma and e-beam are the same story: sterile plastic tells you nothing about pyrogen level.
  • Freeze at 1–3 °C/min — slower dehydrates, faster forms intracellular ice.
  • Freeze slowly, thaw fast. The asymmetry is deliberate, and reversing it kills stocks.

What a chain of containers costs you

Real work is never one container. A sample goes from a stock tube to a dilution tube to a plate, and each step takes its cut — some to the pipette from Lesson 2, some to the wall from §3.2. Those losses multiply:

eq. 3.5.1 $$\text{recovery} \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1 - f_i\right)$$

with \(f_i\) the fraction lost at step \(i\). Five transfers each losing 8% to the wall leaves you \(0.92^5 = 66\%\) of what you started with — a third of the sample gone, with nothing spilled and nothing to see. Set against Lesson 2's eq. 2.5.1, the pair covers both halves of the same problem: eq. 2.5.1 counts what the pipette failed to move, eq. 3.5.1 counts what the plastic kept.

Fewer containers is a real experimental design. Every transfer is a pipetting error and an adsorption loss. If you can do a dilution series in one plate instead of five tubes, or aliquot once into the vessel you will actually use, you have removed the errors rather than corrected them — which is the only thing that ever works reliably.

Check yourself — the competency set

1. A protocol says "spin at 4000 rpm for 5 minutes". You are running it on a different centrifuge from the one it was written on. What do you need, and what do you write down?

Answer

You need the radius of the rotor the protocol was written for, so you can convert to an RCF with eq. 3.2.1 and then back to an rpm on yours. Without it the instruction is genuinely incomplete — Corning says so explicitly. What you write in the notebook is the RCF and the time, plus the rotor you used, so the next person does not face the same problem. A 4000 rpm spin at r = 8.5 cm is 1520 × g; at r = 19 cm it is 3400 × g, more than twice as hard.

2. You are aliquoting a 50 µg/mL protein stock into 20 µL single-use aliquots. What is the container decision, and what is the failure mode if you get it wrong?

Answer

Each aliquot holds 1 µg in 20 µL. Use the smallest tube that holds 20 µL, in a low-bind surface. In a 0.5 mL tube the wetted area for 20 µL is tiny; in a 1.5 mL tube it is several times larger for no benefit, and by eq. 3.3.2 the fraction adsorbed scales with it. The failure mode is silent: every aliquot is short by the same amount, so the aliquots agree with each other perfectly and all of them are wrong — which is the systematic-error signature from Lesson 2 all over again.

3. Cells thawed from a vial attach loosely or not at all, 24 hours later. Name three things from this lesson that could cause it.

Answer

Cooling rate: frozen too fast or too slow, so the cells were damaged before they ever got cold — Corning notes that cells like this "will either recover slowly or not at all". DMSO exposure: left in contact at room temperature, or not removed promptly on thawing. The surface: an untreated or ULA vessel, where §1.1 says attachment was never going to happen — and Corning notes that some vessels have surfaces designed specifically to improve attachment after cryogenic storage. A fourth, outside this lesson: the thaw itself, which should be 60–90 seconds at 37 °C.

  • Every consumable is a compromise among clarity, temperature, chemistry and binding. Know which one your job cares about.
  • Losses multiply down a chain of containers: five 8% steps leave you two-thirds.
  • The best fix for a transfer loss is one fewer transfer, not a correction factor.
  • When it matters, run a trial with the real tube, the real solution and the real conditions.
