1.1 · The piston never touches your liquid

Press the plunger on a pipette and something moves. It is not the liquid. Inside the barrel a piston slides in a sealed bore, and between that piston and the top of your sample sits a column of air. The piston pushes the air, the air pushes the liquid. The manual calls this an air displacement principle (i.e. an air interface), and that interface is the source of almost every systematic error a pipette has.

Air is compressible. Water is not. So the piston does not sweep the volume you get — it sweeps the volume you get plus whatever the air cushion quietly absorbed. Below, watch how much that is.

  1. Start on water, straight from the box at 1000 µL. Read the shortfall. Compare it to the ±8.0 µL the manual permits for this model at this volume.
  2. Tick tip pre-wetted. Read the shortfall again. One habit, described in the manual in a single sentence with no explanation, and this is what it was for.
  3. Untick it and switch to acetone. This is what a volatile solvent does to an un-wetted tip.
  4. Leave it on acetone, tick pre-wetted, and drag dead air volume from 50 to 800 µL. Which matters more for a volatile — the pre-wet, or the size of the air cushion?
In the tip:

Controls

Piston, air cushion and liquid

Why the cushion loses you volume

The air between piston and liquid obeys Boyle's law — at constant temperature its pressure and volume trade off:

eq. 2.1.1 $$p_1 V_1 \;=\; p_2 V_2$$

with \(p\) the absolute pressure and \(V\) the volume of the trapped air. Pull the piston back and the cushion expands, so its pressure drops and liquid is drawn up until the pressures balance. But two things push back. The liquid column you have just lifted weighs something, \(\rho g h\). And the liquid evaporates into the dry cushion, adding its saturated vapour pressure \(p_v\). Both reduce how far the liquid climbs:

eq. 2.1.2 $$\Delta V \;=\; V_d\,\frac{\rho g h + p_v}{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}$$

where \(\Delta V\) is the volume you are short by, \(V_d\) the dead air volume between piston and liquid, \(h\) the height of the liquid column in the tip, \(\rho\) its density, and \(p_{\mathrm{atm}}\) the ambient pressure — 92.9 kPa in Tucson, not the 101.3 kPa at sea level, which makes every one of these errors about 9% larger here.

Pre-wetting deletes the \(p_v\) term. Fill and empty the tip two or three times and the air cushion is already saturated with your liquid's vapour, so no further evaporation happens during the real aspiration. For water it takes a 1.05% error down to 0.07%. The manual says "fill and empty the tip 2–3 times" and never says why. That is why.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. This model treats the cushion as going from bone dry to fully saturated. Reality is in between — some evaporation happens during the aspiration itself, and the tip's shape sets how fast. So read the size and the ordering here, not the third decimal: water is a fraction of a percent, ethanol is a percent or two, acetone is catastrophic, and DMSO does not care. That ordering is what decides how many times you pre-wet.

1.2 · There are two stops, and they do different jobs

Press the plunger slowly and you will feel it stop twice. The first stop is the calibrated stroke — the volume in the display window. The second stop is past it, and it exists only to push out what surface tension left behind.

Which stop you aspirate from, and which you dispense to, is the difference between the two pipetting techniques in the manual. Work the plunger below and watch where the liquid goes.

  1. Drag the plunger slowly from the top to the first stop and back, in forward mode. Watch the liquid rise. Read the delivered volume.
  2. Now push past the first stop to the second. Watch what leaves the tip — and notice what follows it out.
  3. Switch to reverse and repeat. Where does the extra liquid come from, and where does it end up?
  4. Set surface tension to its maximum in forward mode. Which mode leaves less behind?
Technique:

Controls

The stroke, from ready to second stop

The manual's two techniques, in one sentence each

ForwardAspirate from the first stop. Dispense to the first stop, wait about a second, then go to the second stop to empty the tip. This is what the pipette was factory-calibrated with, so it is the default.
ReverseAspirate from the second stop, so the tip takes up more than the set volume. Dispense only to the first stop and keep it there. The excess stays in the tip and is discarded. For viscous liquids, anything that foams, and very small volumes.
Reverse works because the tip never has to empty completely. In forward mode the last of the liquid is blown out against surface tension, and whatever clings to the wall is volume you were supposed to deliver and did not. In reverse mode the delivered volume is bounded by two piston positions with liquid on both sides of the transition, so wall retention never enters the number at all — it just stays behind in the discarded excess.
Where the published guidance lands Note from Dr. McGhee. The manual is explicit that the pipette is "factory calibrated and adjusted … using the forward pipetting technique" and that "the use of other pipetting techniques may affect the calibration results". So reverse pipetting is not free: it is more repeatable on hard liquids, and it is differently accurate. If a protocol needs both accuracy and reverse technique, the pipette has to be re-checked in that mode — which is exactly what tab 4 shows you how to do.
  • The piston moves air, not liquid. Every systematic error lives in that cushion.
  • The cushion loses you volume through the liquid's vapour pressure and the weight of the column it lifted.
  • Pre-wetting saturates the cushion and deletes the vapour term — worth about 1% on water and about 10% on acetone.
  • First stop = the calibrated volume. Second stop = blow-out. Forward and reverse differ only in which stop you start from.

2.1 · The same volume, two pipettes, ten times the error

The manual's Table 1 is the most useful page in it, and the thing to notice is what stays constant down each column. For the 100–1000 µL model the permitted inaccuracy is ±8.0 µL at 1000 µL — and ±8.0 µL at 100 µL. The same number of microlitres. Which means 0.8% at the top of the range and 8.0% at the bottom.

Set a volume below and compare what each pipette in the rack is allowed to do with it.

  1. Set volume wanted to 100 µL. Read the permitted error for the 100–1000 µL pipette and for the 10–100 µL pipette. They differ by a factor of ten.
  2. Now set 20 µL and find the best pipette for it. Note how the chart's lower envelope is made of each pipette's top end.
  3. Set 1000 µL. Only one pipette can do it — is it also the one with the smallest relative error?
  4. Find a volume where two pipettes are almost equally good, and say which you would pick and why.
Jump to:

Controls

Permitted error against volume, per model

The two numbers the table is quoting

Every row gives two independent things. Inaccuracy is how far the mean of your deliveries sits from the volume you dialled — a systematic error, the same every time:

eq. 2.2.1 $$A \;=\; \bar{V} - V_0 \qquad\qquad A\% \;=\; 100\,\frac{A}{V_0}$$

with \(\bar{V}\) the mean delivered volume and \(V_0\) the nominal (set) volume. Imprecision is the scatter around that mean — a random error, different every time:

eq. 2.2.2 $$\mathrm{CV} \;=\; 100\,\frac{s}{\bar{V}}$$

where \(s\) is the ordinary sample standard deviation — the same statistic the balance's repeatability was built from in Lesson 1, applied to volumes instead of masses, and carrying its original number:

eq. 1.1.4 again — earned in Lesson 1, applied here to volumes $$s \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2}$$

Read either way, A pipette can be badly inaccurate and beautifully precise, which is the failure mode you will never notice, because every replicate agrees with every other one.

Use the smallest pipette that reaches your volume. Not the one already in your hand, and not the one whose range merely contains the number. The tolerance is fixed in microlitres per model, so the relative error is worst at the bottom of every range — and a volume near the top of a smaller pipette's range is always the better instrument for it.
Worked example — what reaching for the wrong pipette costs a 6-well plate
  1. Write what you know. You need 100 µL of a treatment into each of six wells. Two pipettes are within reach: the 100–1000 µL and the 10–100 µL. From the manual's Table 1, at 100 µL each is permitted:
    $$\text{100–1000 µL model: } A = \pm 8.0\ \text{µL},\quad s = 3.0\ \text{µL}$$
    $$\text{10–100 µL model: } A = \pm 0.80\ \text{µL},\quad s = 0.30\ \text{µL}$$
  2. Turn each into a relative error. Symbolically, from eq. 2.2.1:
    $$A\% = 100\,\frac{A}{V_0}$$
    giving 8.0% for the large pipette and 0.8% for the small one.
  3. Ask what that does to the experiment. Inaccuracy is systematic, so it shifts every well the same way — a dose you believe is 1× may be 0.92× or 1.08×, in all six wells, invisibly.
  4. Now the well-to-well scatter. That is imprecision, and it is what makes replicates disagree:
    $$\mathrm{CV} = 100\,\frac{s}{\bar{V}} = 100\times\frac{3.0}{100} = 3.0\%\quad\text{vs}\quad 100\times\frac{0.30}{100} = 0.30\%$$
  5. Read the answer. Same volume, same operator, same minute.
The wrong pipette gives you a dose that may be 8% off with 3% well-to-well scatter; the right one gives 0.8% and 0.3%. A tenfold penalty on both, for a choice that costs nothing. And note which error is more dangerous: the 8% systematic shift is identical in every well, so no amount of replication reveals it, and no statistical test will flag it.

2.2 · Liquids that do not behave like water

The pipette was calibrated with distilled water, in forward mode. Every liquid that is not water departs from that, and in three different ways: it may be more viscous, so it flows into and out of the tip slowly; it may be denser, so the column weighs more; or it may be volatile, so it attacks the air cushion. Pick a liquid below and see which of the three is biting.

  1. Start on water in forward mode with a normal speed. This is the calibrated condition — the error should be near zero.
  2. Switch to glycerol. Keep forward mode. Read the error, then slow the plunger speed right down and read it again.
  3. Now switch that same glycerol to reverse mode. Compare.
  4. Switch to ethanol and set pre-wets to 0, then to 5. Which technique change helps a volatile, and which helps a viscous liquid? They are not the same fix.

Check yourself

1. A labmate pipettes ethanol very slowly and carefully, and gets consistently low volumes. What is happening, and what should they change?

Answer

Slowness is the problem, not the cure. Ethanol's vapour pressure is about 6.5 kPa, so the dry air cushion is losing volume to evaporation the whole time the liquid sits in the tip — and going slowly gives it longer. Their care is making it worse. The fix is to pre-wet the tip several times so the cushion is already saturated, then work at a normal pace. Slow strokes are for viscous liquids, where the problem is flow, not evaporation.

2. You need 15 µL. The rack holds a 2–20 µL, a 10–100 µL and a 20–200 µL pipette. Which, and what would the 20–200 give you?

Answer

The 2–20 µL. Fifteen microlitres is near the top of its range, where its ±0.20 µL tolerance is about 1.3% — the best of the three. The 10–100 µL is permitted ±0.80 µL, about 5.3%. The 20–200 µL cannot legitimately be used at all: 15 µL is below its stated range, and the manual warns that turning the button outside the range "may jam the mechanism and eventually damage the pipette". Even if it physically displayed 15, its tolerance is ±1.60 µL, better than 10%.

  • Tolerance is fixed in microlitres per model, so relative error is always worst at the bottom of a range.
  • Use the smallest pipette that reaches the volume — it is a free tenfold improvement.
  • Inaccuracy shifts every replicate the same way and no amount of replication reveals it.
  • Viscous wants a slow stroke and reverse technique; volatile wants more pre-wets. Applying the wrong fix makes it worse.
Next: what goes wrong that is not technique — a seal you cannot see, a hand that is warmer than the liquid, and a tip that was never on properly.

3.1 · The seal you cannot see, and the tip that was never on

An air displacement pipette works only if the air cushion is sealed. Two things seal it: the O-ring around the piston, inside the handle where you will never look, and the friction fit between the tip and the tip cone, which you press on by hand and mostly do not check.

When either leaks, the cushion bleeds pressure and the liquid slides back down the tip. It is slow, so a fast operator may never see it — and it is worse for large volumes, because a taller column pushes harder. Aspirate below, then wait.

  1. Start on everything sound at 1000 µL. Aspirate and let the clock run to 10 s. The meniscus should not move measurably.
  2. Switch to tip not seated. Watch the meniscus over the same ten seconds and read the volume lost.
  3. Switch to O-ring worn, never serviced. Compare the two — one of them you can fix in a second, and one needs the strip-down in tab 4.
  4. With the worn O-ring still selected, drop the volume to 50 µL. Does the leak get better or worse, and why?

Controls

Volume held, against time in the air

Why a leak looks like carelessness

Liquid drains back through a leaking seal at a rate set by how hard it is being pushed — the weight of the column it is holding up — and by how big the leak is:

eq. 2.3.1 $$\frac{dV}{dt} \;=\; -\,k_\ell\,\rho\,g\,h(V)$$

with \(k_\ell\) a leak conductance set by the gap, \(h(V)\) the height of the liquid column for the aspirated volume \(V\), and the minus sign because you are losing it. Since \(h\) grows with \(V\), a big volume leaks faster than a small one, and the loss also grows with how long you hold it. A leaking pipette therefore behaves exactly like an inattentive operator: fine on quick small transfers, mysteriously low whenever you pause or work at the top of the range.

Test it in one second, without any equipment. Aspirate a full volume of water, hold the pipette vertically, and watch the meniscus for ten seconds. If it moves at all, either the tip is not seated or the seal is gone. Do this at the start of any session where the number matters. It is the fastest diagnostic in this entire series.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. The manual says "Make sure that the tip is firmly attached to the tip cone. Check for foreign particles in the tip." Both halves matter. A tip pressed on with a light touch, a tip cone with dried medium on it, and a cracked tip all produce the same symptom. Check the cheap causes before you conclude the pipette needs servicing — and note that hammering tips on is not the fix either, since it damages the cone and creates the problem permanently.

3.2 · Your hand is warmer than your reagent

The manual asks you to "make sure that the tips, pipette and solution are at the same temperature", which sounds like fussiness. It is not. Temperature reaches the delivered volume by two routes at once: the liquid's density changes, and — much larger — the sealed air cushion expands when it warms, pushing liquid straight back out of the tip.

Take a pipette out of a 4 °C cold room, or hold one in your fist for five minutes, and see what it costs.

  1. Start on everything equilibrated. Both errors should be near zero.
  2. Switch to reagent from the fridge: liquid at 4 °C, pipette and room at 22 °C. Read the total error at 1000 µL.
  3. Switch to held in your hand. This is the one people do not believe. Read it.
  4. Drop to 50 µL with the hand-warmed case still selected. Is the relative error better or worse at small volume?
Situation:

Controls

Delivered volume against temperature mismatch

Two temperature terms, one much bigger than the other

The air cushion is a fixed quantity of gas in a nearly fixed space, so warming it makes it push:

eq. 2.3.2 $$\Delta V \;=\; V_d\,\frac{\Delta T}{T} \;+\; V\,\beta\,\Delta T_\ell$$

The first term is the air cushion, from the ideal gas law at constant pressure: \(V_d\) the dead air volume, \(\Delta T\) how much warmer the cushion is than when the volume was set, and \(T\) the absolute temperature. The second is the liquid's own thermal expansion, with \(\beta \approx 2.1\times10^{-4}\)/K for water near room temperature and \(\Delta T_\ell\) its temperature offset. The first term is roughly ten times the second, and it does not care what volume you dialled — which is why this error is proportionally devastating at small volumes.

Warm hands are worth more than cold reagents. Holding the pipette until its body is 3 °C above the room moves a 350 µL cushion by about 3.6 µL — regardless of whether you set 1000 µL or 50 µL. At 1000 µL that is 0.4% and inside spec. At 50 µL it is 7% and far outside it. Put the pipette down between transfers.
  • A leak drains faster at large volumes and worsens the longer you hold — so it mimics carelessness.
  • The ten-second meniscus watch is a complete, free leak test. Do it whenever the number matters.
  • Thermal error lives mostly in the air cushion, not the liquid, so it is a fixed number of microlitres.
  • A fixed microlitre error is a catastrophe at small volumes. Everything in this tab hurts the bottom of the range most.

4.1 · Find out what it really delivers: weigh the water

A pipette has no display of truth. The only way to know what it delivers is to catch the delivery on a balance and convert. That is what the manual's calibration section does, and it is a twenty-minute job you can run yourself.

The procedure, verbatim in outline: pre-wet a new tip 3–5 times, do ten pipettings at the minimum volume and ten at the maximum, compute inaccuracy and imprecision for both series, and compare against Table 1. Run it below.

  1. Start on a healthy pipette at 1000 µL. Read the inaccuracy and CV, and check both against the ISO 8655 limits shown on the chart.
  2. Drop to the minimum volume of the same model, 100 µL. The limits move; does the pipette still pass?
  3. Switch to needs adjusting. Which of the two numbers went bad — the mean, or the scatter? Which service does that call for?
  4. Switch to worn seal and answer the same question. The two faults look completely different on this chart, and that is the diagnostic.
The pipette:

Controls

Ten deliveries, against the permitted band

Mass to volume — the manual's own formula

eq. 2.4.1 $$V \;=\; (w + e)\,Z$$

with \(V\) the delivered volume in µL, \(w\) the weight in mg, \(e\) the evaporation loss in mg, and \(Z\) the conversion factor for water at the test temperature and pressure — 1.0032 µL/mg at 22 °C and 95 kPa. \(Z\) is not 1.000 because water is not exactly 1 mg/µL and because the water on the pan is buoyed by air, which is eq. 1.3.3 from Lesson 1 folded into a single lookup number.

The \(e\) term is real work, not a formality. The manual tells you how to measure it: dispense water into the vessel, note the reading, start a stopwatch, and see how much the reading falls in 30 seconds. If it drops 6 mg in 30 s that is 0.2 mg/s, and a 10-second pipetting cycle costs you 2 mg. This is also why the calibration conditions demand relative humidity above 50% — a requirement Tucson fails for most of the year, and one reason the manual recommends an evaporation trap below 50 µL.

Which balance you need is then set by the volume, and Lesson 1 already told you why. Its minimum-weight rule comes back unchanged, with the pipette's tolerance standing in for the required accuracy:

eq. 1.3.4 again — earned in Lesson 1, used here to choose the balance $$m_{\min} \;=\; \frac{2s}{\mathrm{RE}}$$
A 1 mg-readability precision balance can check a 1000 µL pipette and cannot check a 10 µL one. The manual asks for a graduation of 0.1 mg above 100 µL, 0.01 mg from 10 to 100 µL, and 0.001 mg below 10 µL. Put that through eq. 1.3.4: to resolve a P10's ±0.12 µL tolerance you must see about 0.12 mg, and a balance with s = 0.5 mg cannot — \(2s\) alone is 1 mg. That is not a technique problem; it is arithmetic.
Worked example — can we calibrate our P1000 on the lab's XSR balance?
  1. Write what you know. The pipette is the 100–1000 µL model. At 1000 µL the manual permits \(A = \pm 8.0\) µL and \(s = 3.0\) µL. Lesson 1's XSR303S has a typical repeatability of \(s_{\mathrm{bal}} = 0.5\) mg. One microlitre of water is about one milligram.
  2. State what "can calibrate" means. The balance must resolve the tolerance you are testing against, so from eq. 1.3.4 with the tolerance as the required relative accuracy:
    $$m_{\min} = \frac{2s_{\mathrm{bal}}}{\mathrm{RE}}, \qquad \mathrm{RE} = \frac{8.0\ \text{µL}}{1000\ \text{µL}} = 0.008$$
  3. Plug in.
    $$m_{\min} = \frac{2(0.5\ \mathrm{mg})}{0.008} = 125\ \mathrm{mg}$$
  4. Compare with what you are weighing. A 1000 µL delivery weighs about 1000 mg, comfortably above 125 mg.
  5. Now repeat for a 10 µL pipette. Tolerance ±0.12 µL on 10 µL, so \(\mathrm{RE} = 0.012\):
    $$m_{\min} = \frac{2(0.5\ \mathrm{mg})}{0.012} = 83\ \mathrm{mg}\quad\text{against a delivery of only }10\ \mathrm{mg}$$
Yes for the P1000, no for the P10 — and the manual says the same thing from the other direction, asking for a 0.001 mg graduation below 10 µL. So a check at the top of a large pipette's range is something this lab can do this afternoon; anything at the small end needs a semi-micro or micro balance. Notice this is the mirror image of tab 2's lesson: the volume you can trust most is the one at the top of a range, and it is also the only one you can independently verify.

Which fault does which service fix?

Mean is off, scatter is fineThe stroke is delivering the wrong volume consistently. That is an adjustment, at the calibration nut. It is quick, and it does not touch the seals.
Scatter is largeSomething is inconsistent: a worn O-ring, dry grease, a damaged tip cone, or dirt. That is the strip-down. Adjusting it would only re-centre a distribution that is too wide.
Both are badStrip down first, then re-check, then adjust if the mean is still off. Adjusting before servicing means adjusting to a moving target.
Neither is bad but it feels wrongCheck the tip cone and the tip. A gritty or sticky plunger is a service item even when the numbers still pass — it is the beginning of a fault, not the absence of one.

Check yourself

1. Ten deliveries at 1000 µL give a mean of 1006 µL and a standard deviation of 1.1 µL. Pass or fail, and what do you do?

Answer

Pass on both counts. Inaccuracy is +6 µL against a permitted ±8.0 µL, and \(s = 1.1\) µL against a permitted 3.0 µL. It is a good pipette with a small positive bias. You could adjust the 6 µL out at the calibration nut, and if you do you must re-run the check afterwards — but nothing obliges you to, and adjusting a pipette that already passes is a way to introduce a mistake rather than remove one.

2. The same pipette gives a mean of 999 µL with a standard deviation of 9 µL. Now what?

Answer

Fail — on imprecision, not accuracy. The mean is nearly perfect but \(s = 9\) µL is three times the permitted 3.0 µL. Adjusting is exactly the wrong response, because the mean is not the problem. This is the strip-down: O-rings, piston, grease, tip cone. Check the obvious first — a poorly seated tip produces this signature too, and costs nothing to rule out.

  • V = (w + e)Z. The evaporation term is measurable and the manual tells you how; below 50 µL it is not optional.
  • Calibration wants water, a draft-free room held to ±0.5 °C, and RH above 50% — which Tucson fails for most of the year.
  • A 1 mg balance can check a 1000 µL pipette and cannot check a 10 µL one. That is eq. 1.3.4, not an opinion.
  • Bad mean → adjust. Bad scatter → strip down. Doing them in the wrong order wastes both.
Next: six transfers in a row, and what happens to an error when you multiply it by itself.

5.1 · Make a 1:1000 dilution. How many steps?

Here is the question this whole lesson has been circling. You need to dilute something a thousand-fold. You can do it in one step — 1 µL into 999 µL — or in three steps of one-in-ten, or in six steps of about one in three. More steps means more transfers, and every transfer adds its error to the pile. So fewer steps must be better.

It is not, and the reason is tab 2. Work it out below.

  1. Start on one step. Read the transfer volume the arithmetic demands, which pipette that forces you into, and the final error.
  2. Step to three steps of 1:10. Three times as many transfers. Read the final error again — it should be markedly better, not worse.
  3. Go to six steps. The trend turns around hard between four and five. Find the exact step where it breaks, look at the transfer volume there, and say what has gone wrong.
  4. Drag volume per step from 200 µL up to 2000 µL with three steps selected. What does a bigger working volume buy, and what does it cost?

The job

Final error against number of steps

Why the two errors grow at different rates

Each step multiplies the concentration by a factor \(r\), so after \(n\) steps

eq. 2.5.1 $$C_n \;=\; C_0\,r^{\,n} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \left(\frac{\sigma_C}{C}\right)_{\!\mathrm{sys}} \!=\; n\,A\% \qquad \left(\frac{\sigma_C}{C}\right)_{\!\mathrm{rand}} \!=\; \sqrt{n}\;\mathrm{CV}$$

with \(A\%\) the per-step relative inaccuracy from eq. 2.2.1 and \(\mathrm{CV}\) the per-step imprecision from eq. 2.2.2. The difference in how they grow is the whole point. Inaccuracy is the same bias every time, so it adds: \(n\) steps, \(n\) times the error. Imprecision is a fresh random draw every time, so the draws partly cancel and the total grows only as \(\sqrt{n}\).

Set against that is the effect you found in step 2: adding steps makes each individual transfer larger, which moves each pipette toward the top of its range where its relative error is smallest. That effect is strong — going from one step to three takes the transfer from 1 µL to 100 µL and the per-step transfer error from 8% to 0.8%. The two effects fight, so there is a genuine optimum: for a thousand-fold dilution at 1 mL per step it sits at two or three steps, and going to five is far worse than going to one, because a 251 µL transfer is above every small pipette's range and strands you on the 1000 µL model at a quarter of its scale.

The one-step dilution is the worst option, every time. Not because compounding is kind, but because a thousand-fold dilution in one step forces a microlitre-scale transfer, and a microlitre-scale transfer is where every error in this lesson is at its largest — vapour pressure, thermal expansion of the cushion, a fixed-microlitre tolerance, and a volume too small to verify on any balance this lab owns.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. Two honesty notes on this model. First, it adds the two pipettes' systematic errors as magnitudes, which is a worst case — in reality they may partly cancel, and if you use the same pipette for every transfer the bias is genuinely identical each time, which is what makes the \(n\) scaling exact. Second, it assumes you mix properly between steps. Incomplete mixing is a bigger error than anything on this page and it is not modelled at all, because it is not a property of the pipette.

Check yourself — the competency set

1. Six wells dosed from the same pipette all come out about 8% high. A colleague says "run more replicates". Will that help?

Answer

No. An 8% offset that is the same in every well is inaccuracy — a systematic error, and eq. 2.5.1's \(n\) term rather than its \(\sqrt{n}\) one. Replication reduces the random component and does nothing at all to a bias. What would help: checking that the volume is not near the bottom of the pipette's range (tab 2), pre-wetting (tab 1), a ten-second leak test (tab 3), and if it survives all three, a gravimetric check (tab 4) followed by an adjustment.

2. You are asked to pipette 300 µL of glycerol accurately. Walk through the choices.

Answer

Pipette: the 100–1000 µL model — 300 µL is above the 20–200's range, and ±8.0 µL on 300 µL is 2.7%. Technique: reverse, because glycerol is viscous and will not blow out cleanly. Speed: slow, and never let the plunger snap back. Pre-wet: yes, though glycerol's vapour pressure is essentially zero so this is about wetting the wall, not saturating the cushion. Caveat: the pipette was calibrated in forward mode with water, so if 2.7% is not good enough you must re-check it in reverse with glycerol, or weigh the delivery directly.

3. Why can this lab verify a 1000 µL pipette but not a 10 µL one, and what would it take to change that?

Answer

Because of eq. 1.3.4. Verifying against a ±0.12 µL tolerance means resolving about 0.12 mg, and a balance with \(s = 0.5\) mg has \(2s = 1\) mg of expanded uncertainty on a single weighing — eight times the tolerance you are trying to measure. The manual asks for a 0.001 mg graduation below 10 µL. Changing it needs a semi-micro or micro balance, plus the conditions that make one usable: a draft-free enclosure, ±0.5 °C, and above 50% humidity — which in Tucson means an evaporation trap and probably a humidified enclosure.

  • Systematic error accumulates as n; random error as √n. They are not interchangeable and replication only touches one.
  • Splitting a big dilution into steps makes each transfer larger, which beats the compounding — up to a point.
  • For a 1000-fold dilution at 1 mL per step the optimum is two or three — and five is worse than one.
  • Everything in this lesson hurts small volumes most. When you have a choice, work bigger.
