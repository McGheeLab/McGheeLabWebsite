1.1 · Put something on the pan and watch what doesn't move

Here is the mental model almost everyone arrives with: you put an object on the pan, the pan sinks a little, a spring stretches, and something measures how far it stretched. That is a kitchen scale. It is not this balance.

On a Mettler Toledo XSR the pan is not allowed to move. It hangs off a lever inside a magnetic field, and the moment your sample pushes it down by a fraction of a micrometre, a position detector notices and a control loop pushes a current through a coil until the pan is back exactly where it started. The balance never measures a displacement. It measures how hard it had to push back.

Run the loop below and watch three things at once: where the pan is, how much current the coil is drawing, and what the display says. They do not settle at the same time, and that gap is the first practical thing this lesson will cost you if you ignore it.

  1. Leave everything at its default and press through the set it down scenario. Watch the pan position trace dive and come back to zero. Read how far it actually moved — the number is in micrometres, and the drawing is exaggerated 2000×.
  2. Now watch the coil current trace. Double the mass and read the current again. Write down both pairs.
  3. Switch to knock the bench. The pan is disturbed but no mass was added. Does the settled current change? Should it?
  4. Drag the display filter down to 0.03 s. The number now twitches. Drag it to 0.5 s: the number is calm but late. Find the setting that settles in about 1.5 s — the manual's quoted settling time for this balance — and notice that the two traces are on completely different time axes. The mechanism is finished in milliseconds; the number is not.

Now the equation that explains the current you just watched

A wire of length L carrying current I through a magnetic field B feels a force. That is all the compensation coil is:

eq. 1.1.1 $$F \;=\; B\,I\,L$$

and when the loop has finished, that force is holding your sample up against gravity, so \(mg = BIL\), and therefore

eq. 1.1.2 $$m \;=\; \frac{B\,I\,L}{g}$$

Here B is the field of the permanent magnet in tesla, L the effective conductor length in the gap in metres, I the coil current in amperes, g the local gravitational acceleration in m/s², and m the mass in kilograms. B and L are fixed by the mechanism; the balance knows their product from its last adjustment.

The reading is a current, converted. Everything that can corrupt a weighing is something that either adds a force the coil has to fight, or changes the conversion factor between current and mass. The rest of this lesson is a tour of those two categories — and nothing else.
Where the published guidance lands Note from Dr. McGhee. The XSR manual does not describe its own transducer; it tells you how to operate and maintain the instrument, not how it was designed. Electromagnetic force restoration is the standard mechanism for precision balances at this readability, and the equations above are general physics rather than a quotation from Mettler Toledo. What is quoted, throughout this lesson, is every performance number — those all come from the manual's §9.3 tables and are listed in the constants bar.

1.2 · There is a g in the denominator, and it is not the same everywhere

Look at eq. 1.1.2 again. The balance measures a force and divides by g to report a mass. It does not know g. It was told, once, at a place that is not this room.

Gravity is weaker at the equator than at the poles, because the Earth is not a sphere and because you are moving in a circle. It is weaker high up than low down. The standard formula:

eq. 1.1.3 $$g(\varphi,h) \;=\; 9.780327\left(1 + 0.0053024\sin^{2}\!\varphi - 0.0000058\sin^{2}\!2\varphi\right) \;-\; 3.086\times10^{-6}\,h$$

with \(\varphi\) the latitude and \(h\) the elevation in metres. Tucson sits at 32.22° N and 728 m. Greifensee, where these balances are built, sits at 47.37° N and about 440 m. Those are not exotic numbers, and the difference between them is not small.

Worked example — what a balance shipped from Switzerland reads in Tucson, before you adjust it
  1. Write what you know. Greifensee: \(\varphi = 47.37°\), \(h = 440\) m. Tucson: \(\varphi = 32.22°\), \(h = 728\) m. Sample mass 100 g.
  2. Evaluate eq. 1.1.3 at the factory. \(\sin^2(47.37°) = 0.5413\), \(\sin^2(94.74°) = 0.9933\):
    $$g_{\mathrm{GF}} = 9.780327\left(1 + 0.0053024(0.5413) - 0.0000058(0.9933)\right) - 3.086\times10^{-6}(440)$$
    $$g_{\mathrm{GF}} = 9.780327(1.0028645) - 0.00136 = 9.8070\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$
  3. Evaluate it again here. \(\sin^2(32.22°) = 0.2843\), \(\sin^2(64.44°) = 0.8140\):
    $$g_{\mathrm{TUS}} = 9.780327(1.0015028) - 3.086\times10^{-6}(728) = 9.7928\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$
  4. Assemble the error, symbolically first. The coil current for a given mass is fixed by the real local gravity, but the balance divides by the g it was adjusted against. So the reading is
    $$m_{\mathrm{read}} \;=\; m\,\frac{g_{\mathrm{TUS}}}{g_{\mathrm{GF}}}$$
  5. Plug in.
    $$\frac{g_{\mathrm{TUS}}}{g_{\mathrm{GF}}} = \frac{9.7928}{9.8070} = 0.998551 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \Delta m = 100\ \mathrm{g} \times (1 - 0.998551)$$
  6. Read the answer. \(\Delta m = 0.145\) g.
145 mg low on a 100 g sample — 145 times the balance's readability, and 73 times its linearity spec. This is not a subtle effect, and it is exactly why the balance carries a motorised internal weight and why the manual's maintenance table says to run an internal adjustment after changing the location. The adjustment does not calibrate the mass of the internal weight; it re-measures BL/g in the room the balance is standing in.
An internal adjustment is a local-gravity measurement. Every time you run one, the balance lifts a known mass with its own motor, sees what current that takes here, and rewrites its conversion factor. Move the balance across the bench and nothing changes. Move it to another building, another floor, or another city, and you must re-adjust before the readings mean anything.

1.3 · Weigh the same object ten times

The XSR303S displays milligrams. It is natural to read that as "this balance is accurate to a milligram". Those are different claims, and the gap between them is where most bad numbers in a lab notebook come from.

Readability (d) is the size of the last digit — a display property, chosen by the manufacturer. Repeatability (s) is the scatter you get putting the same object on the same pan ten times in a row — a physics property, set by the mechanism and by the room. The manual quotes both, separately. For the XSR303S readability is 1 mg, and repeatability is given twice: 0.9 mg as a limit value and 0.5 mg as a typical one.

Below, weigh one object repeatedly. The tall bars are what the mechanism actually produced; the comb underneath is what the display was willing to show you.

  1. Start on the typical value. Read the number of distinct values the display showed. With scatter below the readability, most repeats land on the same digit.
  2. Step through limit value and then a draughty bench. Same object, same balance, nothing about the sample changed. Count the distinct displayed values each time.
  3. Drag readability to 0.1 mg with the scatter left where it is. The display gets more digits. Did the measurement get better?
  4. Find the scatter at which the displayed values first spread over three digits. Compare it to the 1 mg readability.

The number the manual calls repeatability

It is the ordinary sample standard deviation of those repeats:

eq. 1.1.4 $$s \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2}$$

with \(x_i\) the individual readings, \(\bar{x}\) their mean and \(n\) the number of repeats. Mettler Toledo publishes it twice. The typical value, 0.5 mg, is what a healthy XSR303S delivers on a decent bench. The limit value, 0.9 mg, is the number the instrument is guaranteed not to exceed — the one to quote when you are promising an accuracy to someone else. Neither is a measurement of your balance in your room, and you can make that measurement in about two minutes: tab 4 has the routine test that does exactly that.

Readability is what the balance shows you. Repeatability is what it knows. A display with more digits than the mechanism can support is not more accurate; it just spreads the same uncertainty across more of them. Everything in tab 3 that decides whether a weighing is trustworthy is built on s, never on d.
  • The pan does not move. The reading is the coil current needed to hold it still, converted through BL/g.
  • Local gravity is part of that conversion. An internal adjustment is how the balance measures it here.
  • Readability ≠ repeatability. 1 mg on the display; 0.5 mg of real scatter typically, 0.9 mg guaranteed.
  • Anything that adds a force to the pan, or changes BL/g, changes your answer. There is no third category.

2.1 · Tilt the balance and watch the number move

The bubble in the corner of the balance is not a decoration and it is not a suggestion. The compensation coil pushes along one axis. If that axis is not vertical, the coil is not fighting your sample's whole weight — only the part of it that points along the axis. The rest is carried by the mechanism, and the balance never sees it.

Tilt the balance below and watch what a level you would call "close enough" is actually worth.

  1. Leave the load at 100 g and tilt to 1.0° in one axis. Read the error in milligrams. Compare it to the balance's 1 mg readability.
  2. Double the tilt to 2.0°. Did the error double? Look at the equation underneath and explain why not.
  3. Put the tilt back to 1.0° and drag the load from 10 g to 300 g. Watch the error in milligrams and the error in percent. Only one of them moves.
  4. Find the tilt at which a 300 g weighing is wrong by one full readability step.

Why doubling the tilt more than doubles the error

Only the vertical component of the sample's weight reaches the coil, so the balance reads

eq. 1.2.1 $$m_{\mathrm{read}} \;=\; m\cos\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\Delta m}{m} \;=\; 1-\cos\theta \;\approx\; \frac{\theta^{2}}{2}$$

with \(\theta\) the total tilt from vertical in radians and \(\Delta m = m - m_{\mathrm{read}}\) the shortfall. The error goes as \(\theta^{2}\), which is why a small tilt looks harmless and a moderate one does not: going from 1° to 2° does not double the error, it quadruples it.

Notice what the sim showed you in step 3: the error in milligrams grows with the load, but the error in percent does not move at all. Tilt is a proportional error — it scales your whole answer. That will matter in tab 3, because the other big errors are not proportional, and the two kinds have to be handled differently.

A tilt error is invisible. Nothing about the reading looks wrong. It is stable, it repeats beautifully, and it is wrong by the same fraction every time. The bubble is the only instrument you have that can see it.

2.2 · Put the sample in the middle of the pan

Mettler Toledo measures this and publishes it twice, as it does every performance figure: for the XSR303S, 3 mg at a 100 g test load as a limit value and 1 mg as a typical one, with the weight placed off-centre in the standard test position. Eccentricity, and the one number the manual gives you

To first order the error grows with how far off-centre the load sits and with how heavy it is:

eq. 1.2.2 $$\Delta m_{\mathrm{ecc}} \;=\; k_e\,r\,m$$

with \(r\) the radial distance from the centre of the pan in millimetres, \(m\) the load, and \(k_e\) a per-balance coefficient in mm−1. You never look \(k_e\) up — you back it out of the one specification you are given. Standard eccentricity testing puts the load at the centre of a pan quadrant, which on a 127 mm square pan is \(r = 44.9\) mm; the manual's 3 mg limit value at 100 g then fixes \(k_e = 6.7\times10^{-7}\) mm−1 for this model. Repeat the arithmetic with the 1 mg typical value and you get a third of that — the honest range your own balance lives in until you measure it.

Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. Real eccentricity is not exactly linear in r and it is not the same in every direction — a mechanism can be well-behaved front-to-back and poor left-to-right. The straight line above is a cartoon calibrated to hit the one published point. Its job is to get the size and the scaling right, which it does; do not read a specific number off it and put it in a notebook. If you need to know your own balance's behaviour, run the eccentricity routine test in tab 4 — that measures it instead of modelling it.

2.3 · The order of operations, and why it is that order

The manual gives a start-up sequence and it is worth taking literally, because every step invalidates the ones before it:

1 · Acclimatise, ≥ 4 hAfter the balance arrives at the bench it stays there, powered off, for at least four hours. A mechanism at a different temperature from its housing is still moving.
2 · Warm up, ≥ 30 minFrom cold power-on. From standby it is ready immediately — which is the real argument for never switching the balance off at the wall.
3 · Check the levelLevel first, because levelling moves the mechanism and invalidates any adjustment made before it.
4 · Internal adjustmentNow, and only now, does re-measuring BL/g mean anything. The manual asks for this daily, and after cleaning, levelling or moving.
5 · Routine testPer your SOP. Adjustment sets the balance; a test tells you whether it worked. They are not the same operation.
6 · Zero, then weighZero with the pan as it will be — bare, or with whatever stays on it all day.

Steps 3 and 4 are the pair people invert, and inverting them wastes both. Levelling after adjusting means you adjusted a balance that then moved.

Tare, or weigh by difference?

You almost never want the mass of the weighing boat. Two ways to get rid of it: put the empty boat on and press tare, or weigh the boat, weigh boat-plus-sample, and subtract. People argue about which is more accurate. The arithmetic says they are identical:

eq. 1.2.3 $$\sigma_{\mathrm{net}} \;=\; \sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{tare}}^{2} + \sigma_{\mathrm{gross}}^{2}} \;=\; s\sqrt{2} \;\approx\; 1.41\,s$$

Both routes are a difference of two weighings, each carrying the balance's repeatability \(s\), so both cost you a factor \(\sqrt{2}\). Taring does not make the boat's uncertainty disappear; it just stores it out of sight. What weigh-by-difference buys you is a written record of both numbers, so a drift between them is something you can notice afterwards. What taring buys you is one less transcription error. Pick per job, not per dogma.

Worked example — you bumped the bench mid-session and carried on. What did it cost?
  1. Write what you know. The knock left the balance 0.8° out of level. You weighed out 250 mg of a reagent, tared with the boat on the pan, and the boat sat about 35 mm off-centre. XSR303S: \(k_e = 6.7\times10^{-7}\) mm−1.
  2. Take the tilt term first, symbolically. Tilt is proportional, and it acts on the net mass because the tare was taken under the same tilt:
    $$\Delta m_{\theta} = m\left(1-\cos\theta\right), \qquad \theta = 0.8° = 0.01396\ \mathrm{rad}$$
    $$\Delta m_{\theta} = 250\ \mathrm{mg}\times\left(1-\cos 0.8°\right) = 250\times 9.75\times10^{-5} = 0.024\ \mathrm{mg}$$
  3. Now eccentricity. Same argument — the boat did not move between tare and reading, so only the added 250 mg is weighed off-centre:
    $$\Delta m_{\mathrm{ecc}} = k_e\,r\,m = 6.7\times10^{-7}\times 35 \times 250\ \mathrm{mg} = 0.0059\ \mathrm{mg}$$
  4. Compare against the noise you cannot avoid.
    $$\sigma_{\mathrm{net}} = s\sqrt{2} = 0.5\ \mathrm{mg}\times 1.41 = 0.71\ \mathrm{mg}$$
  5. Read the answer. Tilt and eccentricity together contribute 0.03 mg. Repeatability contributes 0.71 mg — more than twenty times as much.
The bump cost you essentially nothing, and that is the lesson. At 250 mg these two errors are far below the noise floor. They become the dominant term only on large loads: repeat this at 250 grams and the tilt term alone is 24 mg, against the same 0.71 mg of scatter. Proportional errors matter for big samples; the noise floor matters for small ones — and almost everything the McGhee Lab weighs is small. Tab 3 is about the small end.
Check yourself

1. You level the balance, then run an internal adjustment, then discover the bench wobbles and move the whole thing to a different bench. What must you repeat, and in what order?

Answer

Everything from step 3. The new bench is a new level and a new location, so: re-level, then re-adjust, then re-test. Adjusting before levelling on the new bench would set BL/g against a mechanism that then moves. And because it is a genuinely new place, the gravity argument from §1.2 applies too — although across one room the change is far too small to see, the levelling change is not.

2. A colleague says taring is sloppy and insists on weighing by difference for everything because "it is more accurate". Are they right?

Answer

No — eq. 1.2.3 is the same for both. Both are a difference of two weighings and both cost \(s\sqrt{2}\). Weighing by difference is better documented, not more accurate: you keep both raw numbers, so if the balance drifted between them you can see it. If your SOP requires a record of the vessel mass, weigh by difference. If it does not, taring is one fewer number to write down wrong.

  • Tilt error goes as θ² and is proportional — the same percentage on every sample, invisible in the reading.
  • Eccentricity error grows with radius and with load: 3 mg (limit) or 1 mg (typical) at 100 g in the test position.
  • Level, then adjust, then test, then zero. Levelling after adjusting throws the adjustment away.
  • Tare and weigh-by-difference cost the same s√2. Choose on documentation, not on accuracy.
Next: the errors that do not scale with your sample — the ones that stay the same size while the sample gets smaller, until they are the whole answer.

3.1 · The reading that will not settle

You put a plastic weighing boat on the pan and the number will not stop moving. It drifts one way for thirty seconds, slows, and eventually parks somewhere. You take the boat off and the zero has moved too. Nothing is broken. You are watching electrostatic charge leak off a piece of plastic.

A charged object near a grounded metal surface — the pan, the draft shield, the balance housing — is pulled toward it. That pull is a force the coil has to fight, and the coil cannot tell it apart from weight. The pull dies as the charge drains away, and how fast it drains depends almost entirely on how humid the room is. In Tucson, the room is not humid.

  1. Start on plastic boat, Tucson in June. Watch the apparent mass drift. Read the initial error and compare it to the 1 mg readability.
  2. Drag humidity up to 60% and watch the same charge disappear. Read the time constant at 25% and at 60% — it is not a small factor.
  3. Halve the charge slider. Does the error halve? Look at the equation and say why not.
  4. Switch to metal spatula and boat. Explain, in one sentence, why the error is gone.

Controls

Apparent mass while the charge drains away

Why halving the charge quarters the error

A charge q a distance d above a grounded conducting plane is attracted to it exactly as if an equal and opposite charge sat the same distance below the plane — the image charge. The two are \(2d\) apart, so

eq. 1.3.1 $$F \;=\; \frac{q^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}(2d)^{2}} \;=\; \frac{q^{2}}{16\pi\varepsilon_{0}d^{2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta m \;=\; \frac{F}{g}$$

with \(q\) the charge in coulombs, \(d\) the gap in metres, \(\varepsilon_0\) the permittivity of free space and \(\Delta m\) the spurious mass the balance reports. The \(q^{2}\) is why halving the charge quarters the error, and the \(d^{-2}\) is why the draft shield being close is not helping you.

Static is not a small effect. Five nanocoulombs — a boat rubbed once against a nitrile glove — sitting 10 mm from grounded metal produces about 57 mg of apparent mass on this balance. That is fifty-seven times the readability, and it decays while you watch, so it does not even look like a constant offset you might catch.
McGhee Lab practice Note from Dr. McGhee. Tucson bench humidity sits in the teens for months at a time, which is squarely in the regime where charge takes tens of minutes to drain. In practice: weigh powders in a metal or antistatic vessel wherever the protocol allows it; do not peel a boat off a stack and go straight to the pan; and if a reading is drifting monotonically in one direction with nothing on the pan but a plastic vessel, stop and treat it as charge, not as a balance fault. If it becomes a recurring problem for a specific protocol, an ioniser is the standard fix and worth asking for.

3.2 · Everything on the pan is floating a little

Air is a fluid and your sample displaces it. Archimedes does not care that the fluid is thin: an object in air weighs less than the same object in vacuum, by the weight of the air it pushed aside. The balance does not correct for this, because it cannot — it has no idea what your sample is made of.

What the balance was given is a reference density. Adjustment weights are conventionally treated as 8000 kg/m³, so the balance implicitly reports "the mass of a piece of 8000 kg/m³ steel that would read the same". If your sample is not steel, that is not your mass.

  1. Start on water. Read the correction in percent and in milligrams on a 100 g sample. Is it bigger or smaller than the balance's readability?
  2. Switch to steel weights. The correction nearly vanishes. Say why, using the equation below.
  3. Switch to polystyrene foam. Read the correction. This is the case people find unbelievable, and it is real.
  4. Put it back on water and drag elevation from 0 m to 728 m — Tucson. Which direction does the correction move, and by how much of itself?

Buoyancy correction against sample density

The correction, and where the air density comes from

Air is close enough to an ideal gas for this:

eq. 1.3.2 $$\rho_{\mathrm{air}} \;=\; \frac{p\,M_{\mathrm{air}}}{R\,T}$$

with \(p\) the ambient pressure in pascals, \(M_{\mathrm{air}} = 0.028964\) kg/mol, \(R = 8.314\) J/(mol·K) and \(T\) the absolute temperature. At sea level and 22 °C that is 1.20 kg/m³; in Tucson, where the pressure is about 92.9 kPa, it is 1.10 — nine percent less air to be buoyed by, which is nine percent less correction. Then

eq. 1.3.3 $$m_{\mathrm{true}} \;=\; m_{\mathrm{read}}\left[1 + \rho_{\mathrm{air}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{s}} - \frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)\right]$$

with \(\rho_s\) the sample's density and \(\rho_{\mathrm{ref}} = 8000\) kg/m³ the conventional reference-weight density. The bracket is the whole story: when \(\rho_s = \rho_{\mathrm{ref}}\) the two terms cancel and there is no correction at all, which is exactly why steel weights show nothing. As the sample gets lighter than steel, \(1/\rho_s\) grows and the correction grows with it.

Water on this balance reads about 0.1% low. On 100 g that is 96 mg — ninety-six readability steps, and a hundred and ninety times the repeatability. Buoyancy is not a fussy metrologist's correction; it is the largest systematic error in this lesson for any low-density sample weighed in quantity.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. Before you start correcting everything: buoyancy is a proportional error, so it cancels wherever you only care about a ratio. Weighing agarose to make a percentage solution, you weigh the powder and measure the volume — the buoyancy error on 0.1 g of powder is about 0.06 mg, which is nothing. Buoyancy earns its keep when you are transferring an absolute mass between labs, calibrating something, or working with foams and low-density solids. Powders are also awkward for a second reason: the volume that matters is the volume the powder actually displaces, air gaps included, not the density of the solid material.

3.3 · How small a sample is this balance allowed to weigh?

Every error so far has been proportional: tilt, eccentricity and buoyancy all scale with the sample, so they are a fixed percentage whether you weigh 300 g or 300 mg. Repeatability does not. It is a fixed number of milligrams — and as the sample shrinks, that fixed number becomes a bigger and bigger fraction of it.

There is a mass below which the balance simply cannot deliver the accuracy you need, and it is calculable. Find it below, then check your answer against the manual.

  1. Leave repeatability at 0.5 mg and set required accuracy to 0.10%. Read the minimum weight off the chart. Now open the constants bar and compare it to the manual's published number for this balance.
  2. Change required accuracy to 1%. Read the minimum weight again and check it against the manual a second time.
  3. Switch to the agarose job: 0.1 g of powder. Where does it sit relative to the two thresholds?
  4. Now drag repeatability up to 0.9 mg — the manual's typical figure rather than its reference-conditions one. What happens to the agarose job?

Relative uncertainty against sample mass

The minimum weight, in one line

The uncertainty of a single weighing, expanded to about 95% confidence, is \(2s\). Divide it by the sample mass to get a relative uncertainty, demand that this be no worse than your required accuracy \(\mathrm{RE}\), and solve:

eq. 1.3.4 $$\frac{2s}{m} \;\le\; \mathrm{RE} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m_{\min} \;=\; \frac{2s}{\mathrm{RE}}$$

with \(s\) the repeatability from eq. 1.1.4, \(\mathrm{RE}\) the required relative accuracy as a fraction, and \(m_{\min}\) the smallest sample the balance may be used for. The factor 2 is a coverage factor — the \(k = 2\) the manual names in its own footnote to the minimum-weight row — and it is not the \(\sqrt{2}\) from eq. 1.2.3. The two arrive from different arguments and must never be multiplied together by accident.

Worked example — derive the manual's two published minimum weights from one measured number
  1. Write what you know. XSR303S typical repeatability, from the manual's §9.3 table: \(s = 0.5\) mg. Two requirements to test: the USP pharmacopoeial tolerance of 0.10%, and an ordinary prep tolerance of 1%.
  2. Assemble the equation. Symbolically, from eq. 1.3.4:
    $$m_{\min} = \frac{2s}{\mathrm{RE}}$$
  3. Plug in the pharmacopoeial case. \(\mathrm{RE} = 0.0010\):
    $$m_{\min} = \frac{2(0.5\ \mathrm{mg})}{0.0010} = 1000\ \mathrm{mg} = 1\ \mathrm{g}$$
  4. Plug in the prep case. \(\mathrm{RE} = 0.010\):
    $$m_{\min} = \frac{2(0.5\ \mathrm{mg})}{0.010} = 100\ \mathrm{mg}$$
  5. Check against the manual. Its §9.3 table lists, for the XSR303S: minimum weight 1 g at 0.10% tolerance, and 100 mg at 1% tolerance.
Both numbers, exactly — and the manual's own footnote on that row (determined at 5% load, k = 2) confirms it used the same coverage factor 2 that eq. 1.3.4 does. You did not look the answers up; one repeatability figure and one line of algebra reproduced the manufacturer's published specification. Which means the reverse is available to you: measure your balance's repeatability with the routine test in tab 4, and you can compute its real minimum weight on your bench, on a Tuesday, with the air handling doing whatever it does.
0.1 g of agarose sits exactly on the 1% line. The protocol's weigh-out is precisely at this balance's minimum weight for 1% work — not below it, but with no margin either. Use the manual's limit repeatability of 0.9 mg instead of its typical 0.5 mg and the minimum weight becomes 180 mg — the same weigh-out is now under it. That is the single most useful number in this lesson, and tab 5 spends its capstone on it.
Check yourself

1. You need 20 mg of a peptide to 2% accuracy. Your balance has a measured repeatability of 0.5 mg. Can you do it?

Answer

No. \(m_{\min} = 2(0.5)/0.02 = 50\) mg, and you have 20 mg. At 20 mg the achievable relative uncertainty is \(2(0.5)/20 = 5\%\), not 2%. Your options are a balance with better repeatability (a semi-micro or micro balance, where s is tens of micrograms), or weighing a larger mass and diluting down — which trades the balance's uncertainty for a pipette's, and lesson 2 is about whether that is a good trade.

2. Someone corrects a 250 g water weighing for buoyancy and quotes the answer to the nearest 0.1 mg. What is wrong?

Answer

The correction is right — about 240 mg on 250 g — but the precision claimed is not supportable. A single weighing carries \(2s = 1\) mg of expanded uncertainty at best, so the last digit is meaningless. Correcting a systematic error does not shrink the random one; buoyancy and repeatability are independent, and the answer can only be as precise as the worse of the two.

  • Static charge on plastic can be worth tens of milligrams, goes as q²/d², and drains slowly in dry air. Tucson is dry air.
  • Buoyancy makes water read about 0.1% low, and vanishes only for samples as dense as the 8000 kg/m³ reference.
  • Proportional errors (tilt, eccentricity, buoyancy) are a fixed percentage. Repeatability is a fixed number of milligrams.
  • mmin = 2s/RE. One measured number reproduces the manufacturer's published minimum weights exactly.

4.1 · Let the room warm up and watch the span go

The manual's maintenance table opens with a single word in the interval column for internal adjustment: Daily. That looks like ritual. It is not — it is a number, and you can compute it.

An internal adjustment re-measures the conversion factor BL/g from §1.1 by lifting a known mass with the balance's own motor. Between adjustments that factor drifts, mostly with temperature: the magnet gets weaker as it warms, so the same current holds up slightly less mass. The manual quantifies exactly this and calls it the sensitivity temperature drift. Below, let the bench warm up and watch what it costs.

  1. Start on same morning, load at 300 g — this balance's nominal load. Confirm the span error is essentially zero.
  2. Step to that afternoon. Read the span error. Compare it to the residual after adjustment stat, which is the manual's own sensitivity-offset spec.
  3. Drag the temperature change down to 1.0 °C — a room you would call stable. Is the span error still bigger than the residual?
  4. Tick run internal adjustment. Watch the error collapse. Untick it and drag the load down to 10 g: does a drifting span matter for small samples?

Controls

Where "Daily" comes from

Sensitivity drift is proportional to both the load and the temperature change:

eq. 1.4.1 $$\Delta m \;=\; m\,\mathrm{TC}\,\Delta T$$

with \(m\) the load, \(\Delta T\) the temperature change since the last adjustment in kelvin, and \(\mathrm{TC}\) the sensitivity temperature drift — for the XSR303S, 0.0005 %/°C, which is \(5\times10^{-6}\) per °C. Now put the two published numbers side by side. At nominal load, 300 g:

The best the balance can doSensitivity offset after adjustment with the internal weight: 1.2 mg typical at nominal load (6 mg limit value). That is the residual error you cannot adjust away.
What one degree costs300 g × 5 × 10−6 × 1 °C = 1.5 mg. One single degree of drift already exceeds the residual the adjustment leaves behind.
That is the whole argument for "Daily". A bench that moves one degree between morning and afternoon has already given back more than an adjustment can buy you. A real lab bench moves three to five. The manual is not being cautious — at nominal load, an un-adjusted balance is out of its own specification by lunchtime.
Worth knowing Note from Dr. McGhee. Notice who this hurts and who it does not. Span drift is a proportional error, so at 300 g it is milligrams and at 100 mg it is \(100\ \mathrm{mg}\times5\times10^{-6}\times5 = 0.0025\) mg — utterly invisible next to the 0.5 mg of repeatability. If everything you weigh is a hundred milligrams of powder, a missed adjustment is not what will ruin your day; tab 3's minimum weight is. Run the adjustment anyway, because it costs ninety seconds and because the next person at this balance may be weighing something big — but know which error you are actually fighting.

4.2 · Adjusting is not testing — and one test is not the others

An adjustment sets the balance. A test tells you whether it is right. They are different operations and the manual lists them on separate rows, with different triggers.

There are three routine tests, and Mettler Toledo's recommendation is careful: "METTLER TOLEDO recommends to at least perform a sensitivity test." A floor, not a complete answer. Break the balance in each of the three ways below and see which tests notice.

  1. Drag severity down to zero. That is a healthy balance — all three tests pass. Note how much margin each one has.
  2. Put severity back to 1.0. The fault is span drift. Which tests go red? Is the minimum recommendation enough here?
  3. Switch to corner load out of spec — a knock, or a worn mechanism. Now which tests go red, and what does the sensitivity test alone tell you?
  4. Switch to the bench is shaking and answer the same question. Then raise the test load to 300 g and watch two of the three measured values move while the third does not — and say why that is the correct behaviour, not a bug.
What is wrong:

Controls

Three routine tests, run against one fault

What each test actually does

Sensitivity testOne known weight, near nominal load, in the centre of the pan. Compares the reading to the weight's certified value. Catches span drift. Blind to where you put things and blind to noise.
Eccentricity testOne weight, moved to the standard off-centre positions. Compares the readings to the centred one. Catches corner load — a knock, a worn pivot, a pan seated wrong. Blind to span.
Repeatability testOne weight, on and off ten times. Reports the scatter. Catches a bad environment — draughts, vibration, static — and is the only test that gives you the s that tab 3's minimum weight is built from. Blind to span and to corner load.
Each test is blind to the other two faults. "At least a sensitivity test" protects you from the error that grows quietly on every weighing at every load. It does not tell you your balance is healthy. If a number matters, the repeatability test is the one that tells you whether it can matter — it is the only one that measures the thing your minimum weight depends on.

Cleaning, without wrecking it

The manual's NOTICE on cleaning is short enough to obey exactly:

Do not spray or pourLiquid entering the housing damages the instrument. Nothing is ever sprayed at a balance.
Lightly moistened, lint-freeA damp cloth or tissue. Not a wet one, not an abrasive one.
Only the agents in the tableThe manual carries a per-component compatibility table — the same agent can be fine on the weighing pan and damaging on the housing.
Wipe spills immediatelyEspecially anything acidic or alkaline; the table's "partially recommended" grade is explicitly about exposure time.

That compatibility table is per component and per agent, with four grades — best recommendation, R recommended without limitation, PR partially recommended (individual resistance to acid and alkali must be evaluated, including dependence on exposure time), and — not recommended, high risk of damage. It is short, it is in §7.2.1, and it is not reproduced here on purpose: look up your component and your agent in the manual rather than trusting a copy. The one pairing worth memorising is that 70% ethanol and 70% isopropanol are recommended without limitation on every part of the balance — so the reflex you already have from the cell-culture bench is the right one here.

Check yourself

1. You spill a little powder, wipe the pan down with 70% ethanol, and go straight back to weighing. What did you skip, and does it matter?

Answer

An internal adjustment. The manual triggers one after cleaning, because handling the pan and draft shield disturbs the mechanism. Whether it matters depends on your sample: at 300 g it is worth milligrams, at 100 mg it is worth nothing measurable. Run it anyway — it takes ninety seconds and you are not the only person who uses this balance.

2. Your SOP says "run a sensitivity test weekly" and nothing else. You are about to weigh 5 mg of a peptide. Is the SOP sufficient?

Answer

No. A sensitivity test catches span drift, which at 5 mg is worth about \(5\ \mathrm{mg}\times5\times10^{-6}\times5\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} = 1.3\times10^{-4}\) mg — nothing. What decides whether 5 mg is weighable at all is s, and only the repeatability test measures it. With \(s = 0.5\) mg, eq. 1.3.4 says even 10% accuracy needs 10 mg. The SOP is checking the error that does not matter here and ignoring the one that makes the weighing impossible.

  • One degree of temperature change costs more than the residual an internal adjustment leaves behind. That is why the interval is "Daily".
  • Adjusting sets the balance; testing tells you whether it worked. Cleaning, levelling and moving all force a re-adjustment.
  • The three routine tests catch three different faults and each is blind to the other two.
  • The repeatability test is the only one that measures s — and therefore the only one that tells you your real minimum weight.

The concentration, and where its uncertainty comes from

The gel you actually made is the mass you actually weighed divided by the volume you actually added, and each of those carries its own uncertainty:

eq. 1.5.1 $$c \;=\; \frac{m}{V} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\sigma_c}{c} \;=\; \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_m}{m}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{\sigma_V}{V}\right)^{2}}$$

with \(c\) the concentration in % w/v, \(m\) the weighed mass, \(V\) the added volume, and \(\sigma\) the standard uncertainty of each. Errors add in quadrature, which is the single most useful thing to know about combining them: the biggest term dominates, and halving a small term buys you almost nothing.

At 5 mL and 2%, the balance and the pipette are about evenly matched — and below that the balance loses. Weighing 0.1 g sits exactly on this balance's 1% minimum weight, so the mass term is around 1%. A serological pipette delivering 5 mL is around 1% too. Halve the batch and the mass term doubles while the volume term barely moves: small batches are limited by the balance, large ones by the pipette.

Check yourself — the competency set

1. The balance reads a stable 0.1043 g for a sample you believe is about 0.100 g. Name three mechanisms from this lesson that could produce a stable, repeatable, wrong reading, and one that could not.

Answer

Stable and wrong: a tilt error (proportional, perfectly repeatable), a span error from a missed adjustment (proportional, repeatable), and an eccentric load that you keep putting in the same wrong place. Buoyancy also qualifies, though at 0.1 g it is only 0.05 mg. What could not do it is repeatability — random scatter does not produce a stable offset — and a settled static charge is unlikely to be stable, because it drains. Note that 4.3 mg on 0.1 g is 4.3%, far larger than tilt or span can plausibly manage: the honest first suspicion is that the sample really is 0.104 g.

2. You need to weigh 30 mg of a dye to 2% accuracy. What do you check first, and what are your options if the answer is no?

Answer

Minimum weight, eq. 1.3.4: with \(s = 0.5\) mg, \(m_{\min} = 2(0.5)/0.02 = 50\) mg. Thirty milligrams is below it — at 30 mg you can only reach \(2(0.5)/30 = 3.3\%\). Options: use a balance with a smaller s; weigh a larger mass and dilute; or measure your own s with the repeatability test, because 0.5 mg is the manual's typical figure and a very good bench may beat it. What is not an option is weighing more carefully — no technique reduces s.

3. A labmate weighs 250 g of buffer salts, gets a number they distrust, and re-runs the internal adjustment. It changes the reading by 4 mg. What did they just learn, and what have they still not checked?

Answer

They learned the span had drifted — 4 mg on 250 g is \(1.6\times10^{-5}\), which by eq. 1.4.1 is about 3 °C of temperature change since the last adjustment. Perfectly ordinary, and now fixed. Still unchecked: the level (an adjustment does not detect tilt, and at 250 g a 1° tilt is worth 38 mg), and where on the pan the vessel sat (at 250 g in the corner, eccentricity is worth about 10 mg). Both are larger than the error they just corrected.

  • Errors combine in quadrature. The biggest term dominates; shrinking a small one buys almost nothing.
  • The lab's 0.1 g agarose weigh-out sits exactly on this balance's 1% minimum weight — right at the edge, with no margin.
  • Halve the batch and the mass error doubles. Make the biggest batch the protocol allows.
  • At 5 mL the balance and the pipette contribute about equally. Below that the balance limits you; above it, the pipette does.
