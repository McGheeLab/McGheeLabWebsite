Specific Heat — Holding the Jiggle

Heat Transfer Foundations · Lesson 2 of 6 — On a summer afternoon the beach sand burns your bare feet while the ocean one step away stays cool. Same sun, same hours of heating. The difference is specific heat: how much jiggle a kilogram of stuff can swallow before its temperature climbs.

Background: same burner, different block — who heats up first?

Lesson 1's punchline: temperature is jiggle — the random vibration of atoms. A burner is just a machine that pours jiggle into whatever sits on it, at a steady rate we call power, measured in watts (1 W = 1 joule of energy per second). Put two identical blocks on two identical burners and every second each block swallows exactly the same number of joules.

So they heat up together, right? No — and that's the whole lesson. Materials differ wildly in how many joules it takes to push 1 kilogram up by 1 degree. That number is the specific heat. Real values, in joules per kilogram per degree: water 4186, air ≈1005, aluminum 897, dry sand ≈830, copper 385. Water needs almost eleven times more energy per degree than copper — it is a heat sponge.

How fast the temperature climbs = power in ÷ (mass × specific heat). A bigger burner → a faster climb. A bigger block, or a spongier material → a slower climb.
$$ \text{climb rate} \;=\; \frac{P}{m\,c} $$
  • climb rate — how many degrees the temperature rises each second (K/s). The stats show it per minute.
  • P — the burner's power, in watts (joules per second).
  • m — the block's mass, in kilograms.
  • c — the specific heat, in J/kg·K: the price, in joules, of one degree for one kilogram. (A step of 1 K is the same size as 1 °C.)
  • The product mc acts as one number — the block's "thermal mass", the joules it takes to warm the whole block by one degree.

That's the beach: the sun delivers roughly the same power to every square meter, but dry sand (c ≈ 830) climbs about five times faster per kilogram than sea water (c ≈ 4186) — and only a thin top layer of sand takes the hit, while the ocean stirs its heat downward. Burnt feet, cool waves.

In calculus notation the climb rate is written dT/dt — "the change in T per change in t". Tab 3 uses it to draw the whole heating curve.

Put a 1000 W burner under 1 kg of copper, then under 1 kg of water.

  1. Copper: climb rate = 1000 ÷ (1 × 385) = 1000 ÷ 385 ≈ 2.6 degrees per second. A 40-degree rise takes 40 ÷ 2.6 ≈ 15 seconds.
  2. Water: climb rate = 1000 ÷ (1 × 4186) ≈ 0.24 degrees per second. The same 40-degree rise takes 40 ÷ 0.24 ≈ 167 seconds.
  3. The ratio is exactly the ratio of the specific heats: 4186 ÷ 385 ≈ 10.9. Same energy in — copper just has nowhere spongy to put it, so its temperature shoots up.
  • Specific heat c = joules needed to raise 1 kg of a material by 1 degree.
  • Under steady power the temperature climbs at P ÷ (mc): the thermal mass mc decides the pace.
  • Water (4186 J/kg·K) is the everyday champion sponge; metals like copper (385) heat up ~10× faster per kilogram.
  • The burning beach is specific heat in action: dry sand ≈ 830 vs water ≈ 4186.
  1. Run Water vs copper. Copper's thermometer sprints to the 60 °C finish line while water crawls — check the Speed ratio stat: ≈ 10.9×.
  2. Switch to the beach scenario, Water vs dry sand. The ratio drops to ≈ 5× — that gap is your burnt feet versus the cool ocean.
  3. Crank Burner power from 200 to 3000 W. Both blocks speed up, but the ratio never budges. Power sets the pace; \(c\) picks the winner.
  4. Double Block mass. The on-screen race looks the same, but watch the finish times in the stats double — the clock, not the picture, carries the physics: \(m\) and \(c\) enter the equation the same way.

Background: the energy bill for a temperature change

Tab 1 was about rates. Now ask the total-cost question: how many joules does it take to warm this much stuff by this many degrees? Multiply the three knobs together.

Energy needed = mass × specific heat × temperature change. Twice the mass, twice the energy. Twice the temperature rise, twice the energy. And the specific heat is the price per kilogram per degree — water charges 4186 joules, copper only 385.
$$ Q \;=\; m\,c\,\Delta T $$
  • Q — the energy, in joules (the sim shows kilojoules, kJ: thousands of joules).
  • m — mass in kilograms; c — specific heat in J/kg·K, as in Tab 1.
  • ΔT — "delta T", the temperature change, in kelvin. Only the change matters, not where you started; a rise of 1 K is the same as a rise of 1 °C.

On the chart below, Q plotted against ΔT is a straight line through zero, and its slope is mc: the steeper the line, the spongier your block. Slide c around and you're sliding between real materials — the verdict box will tell you which one you're closest to.

Pasta water: bring 1 kg of water (about a litre) from 20 °C to 100 °C.

  1. The change: ΔT = 100 − 20 = 80 K.
  2. The bill: Q = 1 × 4186 × 80 = 334,880 J ≈ 335 kJ.
  3. How long on a 2 kW burner? 2 kW means 2000 joules every second, so 335,000 ÷ 2000 ≈ 167 s ≈ 2.8 minutes — if every joule went into the pot.
  4. Reality check: your stove takes longer, because plenty of those joules heat the kitchen instead of the water.
  • Q = mcΔT: energy = mass × specific heat × temperature change.
  • On a Q-vs-ΔT plot the slope is mc — steeper line = more energy per degree.
  • Only the change ΔT matters here, not the starting temperature.
  • Boiling a litre of pasta water costs ≈ 335 kJ — about 2.8 minutes of a 2 kW burner at 100% efficiency.
  1. Take the Water scenario with \(m = 1\) kg and \(\Delta T = 80\) K and confirm the pasta number: \(Q \approx 335\) kJ in the stats.
  2. Switch to Copper with the same \(m\) and \(\Delta T\): \(Q\) collapses to ≈ 31 kJ. Compare the two lines' slopes on the chart — that gap is 10.9×.
  3. Double Mass and halve ΔT. Did \(Q\) change? Why not? (It's a product — the knobs trade off exactly.)
  4. Slide Specific heat away from the presets and watch the verdict hunt for the nearest material. Park it at 1005: that's a kilogram of air — cheap to heat, but it fills a bedroom.

Background: heating takes time — power is the slope

Put Tabs 1 and 2 together and you can predict the whole heating curve. Start at some temperature (tap water is about 20 °C), pour in power at a steady rate, and the temperature climbs at a steady rate too.

Temperature now = starting temperature + (climb rate) × (time so far). The climb rate is Tab 1's P ÷ (mc). Plotted against time, that is a straight line — and its slope is the climb rate.
$$ T(t) \;=\; T_0 \;+\; \frac{P}{m\,c}\,t $$
  • T(t) — "T at time t": the temperature after t seconds of heating.
  • T0 — "T-zero", the temperature at time zero, before the burner is on.
  • P/(mc) — the climb rate from Tab 1: power on top, thermal mass on the bottom.
  • t — time since the burner went on, in seconds.

Same burner under a kettle, a big soup pot, and a copper pan gives three very different waits — not because the burner changed, but because mc did. Triple the water and the line goes three times shallower; swap water for copper and it rockets.

One honest warning: this straight line assumes every joule stays put. Real objects leak heat to the room while you heat them — the hotter they get, the faster they leak — so the real curve sags below the line and eventually flattens. We'll build that leak properly when we meet moving air in Lesson 5. The line is a good friend early in the heating and an optimist late in it.

A kettle and a copper pan on the same 2000 W burner, both going from 20 to 100 °C.

  1. Kettle, 1 kg of water. Climb rate = 2000 ÷ (1 × 4186) ≈ 0.48 degrees per second — about 29 degrees per minute.
  2. Time to boil: 80 degrees ÷ 0.48 ≈ 167 s ≈ 2.8 min.
  3. Copper pan, 1.5 kg. Climb rate = 2000 ÷ (1.5 × 385) ≈ 3.5 degrees per second.
  4. Its time: 80 ÷ 3.5 ≈ 23 seconds. That's why an empty pan scorches before you've found the oil.
  • Under constant power, temperature rises along a straight line with slope P/(mc).
  • Same power, bigger mc → shallower slope → longer wait. The kettle-vs-soup-pot difference is pure thermal mass.
  • Low-c metals respond in seconds — great for pans, dangerous for fingers.
  • Real objects also lose heat while heating, so this no-loss line is an upper bound (Lesson 5 fixes it).
  1. Load the Kettle scenario. Read the slope (≈ 29 °C/min) and the time to 100 °C (≈ 2.8 min) from the stats, and find the dot where the line crosses the boiling mark.
  2. Switch to Soup pot: triple the water on a weaker burner. The slope flattens and the wait stretches past 11 minutes.
  3. Switch to Copper pan: same 2 kW as the kettle but \(mc\) is tiny — 80 °C in ≈ 23 s. Watch the verdict flag it.
  4. Build the worst case: Power at 200 W and Mass at 5 kg of water. Over two hours to boil — in real life the pot would leak heat almost as fast as that little burner supplies it, and you'd wait forever. That leak is Lesson 5.

Level up: calorimetry, and why (per mole) all metals hold the same

Everything in Tabs 1–3 stands on its own. This tab is the real machinery, one box at a time.

Box 1 — mixing. Drop a hot block into a bath of water inside an insulated cup, so no energy escapes. Whatever the block loses, the water gains, and they meet at one final temperature. That final temperature is a weighted average of the two starting temperatures — and the weights are the thermal masses mc. Whoever holds more jiggle per degree drags the average toward itself.
$$ T_f \;=\; \frac{m_1 c_1 T_1 + m_2 c_2 T_2}{m_1 c_1 + m_2 c_2} $$
  • Tf — the final, shared temperature.
  • m1, c1, T1 — the block's mass, specific heat and starting temperature; m2, c2, T2 the same for the water (c2 = 4186).
  • Where it comes from: energy lost by the block = energy gained by the water, m1c1(T1 − Tf) = m2c2(Tf − T2); solve for Tf.

This is calorimetry: run it backwards and a measured Tf hands you an unknown c1 — which is exactly how the specific heats in the tables were measured.

Box 2 — the atomic punchline. Lesson 1 said each atom stores about 3kBT of jiggle, so each atom contributes about 3kB of heat capacity — regardless of what atom it is. Count one mole of atoms and every simple solid stores the same amount per mole: the Dulong–Petit law.
$$ C_{\text{molar}} \;\approx\; 3R \;\approx\; 24.9\ \text{J/mol·K} \qquad\qquad c \;=\; \frac{3R}{M} $$
  • R — the gas constant, 8.314 J/mol·K (it is kB × Avogadro's number).
  • Cmolar — heat capacity per mole (per 6.022 × 10²³ atoms).
  • M — molar mass in kg/mol: the mass of one mole of the substance. Light atoms → many atoms per kilogram → big c per kilogram.

The wild per-kilogram differences in the tables are mostly just atom-counting. Water breaks the pattern from the other side: its molecules also tumble and flex, storing extra energy — about 75 J/mol·K, triple the metal value — which is why it's the champion sponge. (One caveat you'll meet in statistical mechanics: at low temperatures vibrations "freeze out" quantum-mechanically and Dulong–Petit fails — see Kittel, Ch. 5.)

Drop 100 g of copper at 100 °C into 200 g of water at 20 °C.

  1. Thermal masses: block 0.1 × 385 = 38.5 J/K; water 0.2 × 4186 = 837.2 J/K. The water outweighs the block 22 to 1.
  2. Top of the fraction: 38.5 × 100 + 837.2 × 20 = 3850 + 16,744 = 20,594.
  3. Bottom: 38.5 + 837.2 = 875.7.
  4. Divide: Tf = 20,594 ÷ 875.7 ≈ 23.5 °C. The water barely notices a boiling-hot lump of metal.
  5. Dulong–Petit check: copper's molar mass is 63.5 g/mol = 0.0635 kg/mol, so c = 24.9 ÷ 0.0635 ≈ 392 J/kg·K. Measured: 385 — within 2%, from nothing but 3kB per atom.
  • In an insulated mix, Tf is the thermal-mass-weighted average of the starting temperatures.
  • The weights mici (J/K) decide everything: water baths dominate metal blocks.
  • Dulong–Petit: per mole, simple solids all store ≈ 3R ≈ 24.9 J/mol·K — each atom carries ≈ 3kB, straight from Lesson 1.
  • Per kilogram c = 3R/M: light atoms → many atoms per kg → big c. Water's extra molecular motions make it the outlier.
  1. Load Copper into water and verify the worked example: \(T_f \approx 23.5\) °C in the highlighted stat, and both curves converging to it.
  2. Switch to Aluminum into water — same 100 g, same 100 °C, but \(T_f\) jumps to ≈ 27.7 °C. Why? Lighter atoms: a kilogram of aluminum simply contains more atoms, so it brought more jiggle (\(c = 897\) vs 385).
  3. Try the Big hot splash: 1 kg of copper at 300 °C into 200 g of water. \(T_f\) computes above 100 °C — and the verdict flags that our equation quietly broke, because real water would boil away first (phase changes are extra energy bookkeeping we haven't done).
  4. Read the bar chart: five metals cluster at \(3R\) while water towers at ≈ 75 J/mol·K. Predict gold's specific heat from \(3R/M\) with \(M = 197\) g/mol (\(\approx 126\) J/kg·K), then compare with the measured 129.
