Radiation & Color — Heat That Travels as Light

Heat Transfer Foundations · Lesson 6 of 6 — Two cars park side by side in July. By 2 pm the black seat can blister the backs of your legs while the white one is merely warm. Same sun, same air — the only difference is color. This lesson is about heat that arrives as light, and why color decides how much of it you keep.

Background: light is energy making a delivery

Press your palm onto a black car seat in July and you get a genuine yelp. Nobody lit a burner under that seat, and the air touching it is the same air touching the white car next to it. The seat has been receiving deliveries all morning: sunlight. Light is not just something to see by — every beam is energy in flight, and when it lands on something, that energy has to go somewhere.

This is the third — and strangest — way heat travels. In Lessons 1–5 heat either got handed atom-to-atom through a solid (conduction) or was physically carried by moving air or water (convection). Both need stuff. Radiation needs nothing at all: the energy rides an electromagnetic wave — in particle language, a stream of photons — and crosses even a perfect vacuum. It's how the sun's heat reaches us through 150 million kilometers of empty space.

What happens on arrival? Call the arriving power per square meter \(G\) (the irradiance, in W/m²). Each surface has an absorptivity \(\alpha\) ("alpha"): the fraction of the arriving light it swallows. The rest, \(1-\alpha\), bounces off as reflected light and carries its energy elsewhere. Fresh black paint has \(\alpha\approx0.95\); a good mirror is down near 0.05. That single number is what "color" means to heat.

Put on the atomic lens one last time. When a photon is absorbed, its energy has exactly one place to go: into the jiggle of the atoms that caught it (Lesson 1's definition of heat). And the photon was born the same way — somewhere in the sun, a jiggling charged particle shook an electromagnetic wave loose. So radiation is nothing new: it is jiggle that left a hot object as a wave, flew in a straight line, and became jiggle again on landing. Watch the sim: every absorbed photon makes the surface atoms visibly shake harder.

$$ \underbrace{G}_{\text{arriving light, W/m}^2} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\alpha\,G}_{\text{absorbed — becomes jiggle}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1-\alpha)\,G}_{\text{reflected — bounces away}} $$

Stand a panel in strong summer sun: \(G = 1000\ \text{W/m}^2\) is a good midsummer-noon number. Painted black (\(\alpha = 0.95\)) it absorbs \(\alpha G = 0.95 \times 1000 = 950\ \text{W}\) on every square meter — very nearly a full space heater per square meter, all of it becoming atomic vibration. Painted white (\(\alpha = 0.25\)) it keeps only \(250\ \text{W/m}^2\) — almost 4× less — and the other 750 W bounce away as light. And "light" is a torrent: one photon of green light carries about \(3.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\), so 1000 W/m² means roughly \(1000 / 3.6\times10^{-19} \approx 3\times10^{21}\) photons landing per square meter, per second.

  • Radiation is energy traveling as electromagnetic waves (photons). It needs no material in between — it crosses vacuum.
  • A surface absorbs the fraction \(\alpha\) of arriving light and reflects the rest; absorbed power per area = \(\alpha G\).
  • "Color" is heat-transfer hardware: black \(\alpha\approx0.95\), white paint ≈ 0.25, mirror ≈ 0.05.
  • Absorbed light becomes atomic jiggle — radiation is jiggle → wave → jiggle.
  1. On the Black asphalt scenario, watch single photons: nearly every one dives into the surface and vanishes in a flash — and the atoms below jiggle hard. Read Absorbed αG in the stats.
  2. Slide Absorptivity α down to 0.05 (or click Polished mirror). Same sun, but now the photons ricochet away and the atoms calm right down.
  3. Click Cloudy day: intensity drops to 300 W/m². Even a dark surface stays tame when the deliveries slow — it's the product αG that matters.
  4. Set α ≈ 0.5 and follow ten photons with your eyes: about half sink in, half bounce. Compare with the "of 100 photons" stat.
