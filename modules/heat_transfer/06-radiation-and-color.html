Radiation & Color — Heat That Travels as Light

Heat Transfer Foundations · Lesson 6 of 6 — Two cars park side by side in July. By 2 pm the black seat can blister the backs of your legs while the white one is merely warm. Same sun, same air — the only difference is color. This lesson is about heat that arrives as light, and why color decides how much of it you keep.

Background: light is energy making a delivery

Press your palm onto a black car seat in July and you get a genuine yelp. Nobody lit a burner under that seat, and the air touching it is the same air touching the white car next to it. The seat has been receiving deliveries all morning: sunlight. Light is not just something to see by — every beam is energy in flight, and when it lands on something, that energy has to go somewhere.

This is the third — and strangest — way heat travels. In Lessons 1–5 heat either got handed atom-to-atom through a solid (conduction) or was physically carried by moving air or water (convection). Both need stuff. Radiation needs nothing at all: the energy rides an electromagnetic wave — in particle language, a stream of photons — and crosses even a perfect vacuum. It's how the sun's heat reaches us through 150 million kilometers of empty space.

What happens on arrival? Every surface keeps a certain fraction of the light that lands on it — that fraction is what "colour" means to heat. Fresh black paint keeps about 95%; white paint about 25%; a good mirror only 5%. The rest bounces off and carries its energy elsewhere.
$$ \underbrace{G}_{\text{arriving light, W/m}^2} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\alpha\,G}_{\text{absorbed — becomes jiggle}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1-\alpha)\,G}_{\text{reflected — bounces away}} $$
  • G — the arriving power per square metre (the irradiance), W/m². Strong summer sun is about 1000.
  • α — "alpha", the absorptivity: the fraction kept, from 0 to 1. Black ≈ 0.95, white paint ≈ 0.25, mirror ≈ 0.05.
  • αG — the watts absorbed per square metre; (1 − α)G — the watts reflected.

Put on the atomic lens one last time. When a photon is absorbed, its energy has exactly one place to go: into the jiggle of the atoms that caught it (Lesson 1's definition of heat). And the photon was born the same way — somewhere in the sun, a jiggling charged particle shook an electromagnetic wave loose. So radiation is nothing new: it is jiggle that left a hot object as a wave, flew in a straight line, and became jiggle again on landing. Watch the sim: every absorbed photon makes the surface atoms visibly shake harder.

Stand a 1 m² panel in strong summer sun, G = 1000 W/m².

  1. Painted black (α = 0.95): absorbed = 0.95 × 1000 = 950 W — very nearly a full space heater per square metre, all of it becoming atomic vibration.
  2. Painted white (α = 0.25): absorbed = 0.25 × 1000 = 250 W — almost 4× less. The other 750 W bounce away as light.
  3. How many photons is that? One photon of green light carries about 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so 1000 W/m² means roughly 1000 ÷ (3.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 3 × 10²¹ photons landing on each square metre, every second.
  • Radiation is energy traveling as electromagnetic waves (photons). It needs no material in between — it crosses vacuum.
  • A surface absorbs the fraction α of arriving light and reflects the rest; absorbed power per area = αG.
  • "Colour" is heat-transfer hardware: black α ≈ 0.95, white paint ≈ 0.25, mirror ≈ 0.05.
  • Absorbed light becomes atomic jiggle — radiation is jiggle → wave → jiggle.
  1. On the Black asphalt scenario, watch single photons: nearly every one dives into the surface and vanishes in a flash — and the atoms below jiggle hard. Read Absorbed αG in the stats.
  2. Slide Absorptivity α down to 0.05 (or click Polished mirror). Same sun, but now the photons ricochet away and the atoms calm right down.
  3. Click Cloudy day: intensity drops to 300 W/m². Even a dark surface stays tame when the deliveries slow — it's the product αG that matters.
  4. Set α ≈ 0.5 and follow ten photons with your eyes: about half sink in, half bounce. Compare with the "of 100 photons" grid in the lower panel.

Background: why the black car is an oven — the balance point

Tab 1 counted the watts going in. But a car roof does not heat up forever: as it warms, it starts losing heat faster, and it settles at the temperature where the losses exactly match the delivery. Two loss routes are already in your toolkit. The warm roof heats the air touching it — convection, Lesson 5's h × (surface minus air temperature). And the warm roof glows: every surface above absolute zero sends out its own invisible infrared light, at a rate that climbs steeply with temperature (Tab 3 shows the glow; Tab 4 derives the law).

At the balance point, for each square metre of roof: sunlight kept = heat lost to the air + heat lost as infrared glow. The roof climbs until that is true, then stops climbing.
$$ \underbrace{\alpha\,G}_{\text{sunlight absorbed}} \;=\; \underbrace{h\,(T_s - T_{\text{air}})}_{\text{lost to the air}} \;+\; \underbrace{\varepsilon\,\sigma\,(T_s^{4} - T_{\text{air}}^{4})}_{\text{lost as infrared glow}} $$
  • αG — Tab 1's absorbed sunlight, W/m².
  • Ts — the roof temperature; Tair — the air (and surroundings) temperature. Both in kelvin: the glow term only makes sense on the absolute scale, so add 273 to every °C.
  • h — Lesson 5's convection coefficient, W/m²K: about 5–10 in still air, higher in wind.
  • ε — "epsilon", the emissivity: how good the surface is at glowing, 0 to 1, like α. σ — the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴. The glow term is the only new piece; its fourth powers are what make it climb so steeply.

The sim solves this balance for Ts and also shows the roof climbing toward it, because a real panel has to soak up energy (Lesson 2's thermal mass) before it reaches the balance point.

Now the twist that makes the whole car story work. Ordinary paint — black, white, red, any colour — glows infrared about equally well: ε ≈ 0.9 for all of them. Colour changes only what a surface absorbs of visible sunlight. So the black roof and the white roof lose heat by the same two routes at the same efficiency; the black one simply has almost four times more coming in, and must climb far hotter before its losses catch up. (Bare polished metal is the odd one out: it neither absorbs much nor glows much, ε ≈ 0.05.)

Black roof at noon: G = 1000, α = 0.95, ε = 0.90, a light breeze h = 10, air at 35 °C (308 K).

  1. Coming in: 0.95 × 1000 = 950 W/m².
  2. Guess 60 °C (333 K). To the air: 10 × (60 − 35) = 250. Glow: 0.9 × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (333⁴ − 308⁴) ≈ 170. Out = 420, in = 950 — the roof keeps climbing.
  3. The balance closes near 88 °C (361 K): to the air ≈ 530, glow ≈ 420, total 950 ✓. (The sim does this hunt for you.)
  4. White paint (α = 0.25): only 250 W/m² in, and it balances at about 50 °C. Same sun, same air, same wind — 38 degrees apart, all from α.
  • A sunlit surface settles where losses = delivery: sunlight kept = lost to the air + lost as glow.
  • Two loss routes: convection (∝ Ts − Tair) and infrared glow (∝ Ts⁴ − Tair⁴, steep!).
  • Paint colour sets α for sunlight but barely changes ε (≈ 0.9 for any paint) — so black roofs run far hotter than white ones.
  • Wind (bigger h) and clouds (smaller G) both pull the balance point down.
  1. Load Black paint and watch the roof climb. Read the balance temperature in the stats — about 88 °C — and check that convection + glow ≈ 950 W/m² once it has settled.
  2. Click White paint. Only α changed. How far does the balance point drop?
  3. Back on black, raise Wind h from 10 to 40 W/m²K. Wind is the cheapest fix there is — find the wind that brings a black roof below 60 °C.
  4. Click Polished aluminium: it absorbs little (α = 0.10) and glows badly (ε = 0.05). Which loss route is carrying almost all the load now? Read the two loss stats.

Background: everything glows — you just can't see most of it

Tab 2 leaned on a claim: warm things glow. Here is the whole picture. A jiggling atom is a shaking electric charge, and shaking charges broadcast electromagnetic waves. So every object above absolute zero glows, all the time — you, this screen, an ice cube. What changes with temperature is how much it glows and what colour. Cold things glow feebly and only in long-wavelength infrared light your eyes cannot see. Heat an object up and two things happen at once: the glow gets enormously brighter, and its peak slides to shorter wavelengths — out of the infrared, into deep red, then orange, yellow, white. That is the whole story of a stove element, a candle, a light-bulb filament and the sun: the same glow, at different temperatures.

Rule 1 — colour (Wien's law): the wavelength where the glow is brightest is a constant divided by the temperature. Hotter → shorter wavelength. Your eyes see only 0.38–0.75 micrometres.
$$ \lambda_{\text{peak}} \;=\; \frac{2898\ \mu\text{m·K}}{T} $$
  • λpeak — "lambda peak", the peak wavelength, in micrometres (µm — a millionth of a metre).
  • T — temperature in kelvin. The constant 2898 µm·K is Wien's constant.
Rule 2 — brightness (the Stefan–Boltzmann law): the total glow per square metre rises as the fourth power of temperature. Double the temperature, sixteen times the glow.
$$ E \;=\; \sigma\,T^{4} $$
  • E — total emitted power per square metre, W/m² (for a perfect "black" glower; a real surface gives ε times this).
  • σ — the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴.

Those two laws are all an infrared camera needs: it "sees" the invisible glow and paints hot things bright — which is why firefighters can find people in smoke and why night-vision works with no light at all.

Four glowing objects.

  1. Your skin, 310 K: λpeak = 2898 ÷ 310 ≈ 9.3 µm — a dozen times longer than red light, deep in the infrared. Glow: 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 310⁴ ≈ 520 W/m²; with about 1.8 m² of skin that is ≈ 900 W leaving you as light (the 20 °C room glows back about 750 W, so the net is ~150 W — Tab 4 does that subtraction).
  2. Stove element, 900 K: λpeak ≈ 3.2 µm, still infrared, but the short-wavelength tail just reaches red — dull red heat.
  3. Light-bulb filament, 2700 K: peaks at 1.07 µm; only about 7% of its glow is visible, which is why incandescent bulbs are mostly heaters.
  4. The sun, 5800 K: peaks at 0.50 µm — the middle of the visible band, which is no coincidence: eyes evolved under that lamp.
  • Everything above 0 K glows. Below ~800 K the glow is entirely infrared — invisible to eyes, obvious to an IR camera.
  • Wien: λpeak = 2898 µm·K / T. Hotter → shorter wavelength → red, then white.
  • Stefan–Boltzmann: glow ∝ T⁴ per m² — double the temperature, 16× the light.
  • Even at 2700 K a filament is only ~7% visible light; the sun (5800 K) peaks right in the visible band.
  1. Load Your skin (310 K). The eye view is black — but the infrared camera sees you plainly. Read λpeak ≈ 9.3 µm and the total glow ≈ 520 W/m² in the stats.
  2. Drag Temperature up slowly. Watch the spectrum's peak slide left toward the shaded visible band, and note the temperature where the eye view first shows a dull red (around 800–900 K).
  3. Click Bulb filament (2700 K): white-hot, yet the "visible share" stat says most of the glow is still infrared. Now click The Sun (5800 K) — where is the peak?
  4. Compare total glow at 310 K and 620 K in the stats. Doubling T multiplied the glow by how much? (It should be 16 — that is the fourth power.)

Level up: Stefan–Boltzmann exchange, Kirchhoff's law, and selective surfaces

Everything in Tabs 1–3 stands on its own. This tab is the real machinery, one box at a time.

Box 1 — exchange. A surface glows, but the walls around it glow back. For a small object surrounded by large surroundings, the net radiation leaving each square metre is the difference of two fourth powers. Engineers often want that in the same shape as Newton's law of cooling, so they factor it — exactly, no approximation — into a "radiation coefficient" hrad multiplying a plain temperature difference.
$$ q''_{\text{rad}} \;=\; \varepsilon\,\sigma\left(T_s^{4} - T_{\text{surr}}^{4}\right) \;=\; h_{\text{rad}}\,(T_s - T_{\text{surr}}), \qquad h_{\text{rad}} \;=\; \varepsilon\,\sigma\,(T_s + T_{\text{surr}})\left(T_s^{2} + T_{\text{surr}}^{2}\right) \;\approx\; 4\,\varepsilon\,\sigma\,\bar{T}^{3} $$
  • q″rad — net radiation per square metre, W/m². Tsurr — the temperature of the surroundings (walls), kelvin.
  • hrad — the radiation coefficient, W/m²K, defined so that q″rad = hrad × (Ts − Tsurr) exactly (it uses the factorisation a⁴ − b⁴ = (a − b)(a + b)(a² + b²)). T̄ — the mean of the two temperatures; near 300 K, hrad ≈ 4εσT̄³.

The surprise is the size of hrad at ordinary temperatures: with ε = 0.95 around 300 K it comes out near 6 W/m²K — the same size as natural convection. So a person, a radiator, a mug of coffee in a room loses heat about half by glow and half by air, and the glow half depends on the wall temperature, not the air temperature. That is why you feel cold beside a big window on a winter night even in a warm room: your skin is trading infrared with a 5 °C sheet of glass. And because hrad grows as T̄³, radiation runs away from convection as things get hot — a 400 °C stove element loses several times more by glow than by air.

Box 2 — Kirchhoff's law (1860). At any given wavelength, a surface's ability to absorb equals its ability to emit: a good absorber is a good emitter — at the same wavelength. The subtlety that makes engineering possible is that sunlight and room-temperature glow live at very different wavelengths (0.5 µm vs 10 µm, by Wien), so a surface can be black to one and white to the other.
$$ \alpha_\lambda \;=\; \varepsilon_\lambda $$
  • αλ, ελ — the absorptivity and emissivity at wavelength λ. Tab 1's α is the average over sunlight's wavelengths; Tab 2's ε is the average over the infrared a warm surface glows at.

You in a room, then two variations.

  1. Set up: clothed-skin surface 33 °C (306 K), walls 20 °C (293 K), ε = 0.95.
  2. Radiation: 0.95 × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (306⁴ − 293⁴) ≈ 75 W/m²; hrad = 0.95σ × 599 × (306² + 293²) ≈ 5.8 W/m²K.
  3. Convection at h = 5: 5 × 13 = 65 W/m². Radiation and convection are equals.
  4. Beside a cold window (walls 5 °C = 278 K, air still 20 °C): 0.95σ(306⁴ − 278⁴) ≈ 150 W/m² — double, though the air never changed.
  5. Thermos flask: inner wall 80 °C facing an outer wall at 20 °C across a vacuum. Bare glass ε = 0.95: ≈ 440 W/m². Silvered, ε = 0.05: ≈ 23 W/m² — nineteen times less, and with no air in the gap there is no convection to pick up the slack. That is the Lesson 1 thermos, now with numbers.
  • Net exchange with large surroundings: q″ = εσ(Ts⁴ − Tsurr⁴) = hrad(Ts − Tsurr), with hrad ≈ 4εσT̄³.
  • Near room temperature hrad ≈ 5–6 W/m²K: radiation ≈ natural convection, and it answers to the wall temperature, not the air.
  • Kirchhoff: αλ = ελ — good absorbers are good emitters at the same wavelength. Sunlight (0.5 µm) and room glow (10 µm) are different wavelengths, so selective surfaces can be black to one and white to the other.
  • Low-ε metal is the radiation insulator (thermos, space blanket); high-ε white paint is the cool roof.
  1. Load You in a room and read both losses: radiation ≈ 75 W/m², convection ≈ 65 W/m². Check hrad ≈ 5.8 W/m²K in the stats — same league as h.
  2. Click Beside a cold window. Only the wall temperature changed. Which bar grew, and by how much? (This is why the window seat feels cold.)
  3. Load Stove element and read the ratio stat. Then drag Surface temperature from 100 °C to 500 °C and watch the orange T⁴ curve pull away from the straight convection line.
  4. Load Thermos, silvered: only 23 W/m² escapes. Now slide Emissivity ε up to 0.95 — bare glass — and watch the radiation loss multiply by ~19.
