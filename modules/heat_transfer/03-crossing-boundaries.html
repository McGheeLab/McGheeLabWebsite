Crossing the Boundary — How Heat Gets Through Walls

Heat Transfer Foundations · Lesson 3 of 6 — Press your palm against a window on a cold day. Heat is leaving your house through that glass right now, and exactly three knobs set how fast: what the wall is made of, how thick it is, and how big it is. Turn all three yourself.

Background: a wall between hot and cold

In Lesson 1 you watched heat spread as atoms handed their jiggle to their neighbors. Now put that idea to work on the most common situation in the real world: a hot region and a cold region with a wall in between. Coffee inside a mug, you inside a jacket, a warm house inside a winter. In every case, jiggle enters one face of the wall, gets handed atom-to-atom across it, and leaks out the other face.

Give the wall a little time and it settles into steady state: every layer of the wall passes along exactly as much energy as it receives, so no layer heats up or cools down anymore — the temperatures stop changing even though heat keeps flowing through. And in steady state, a uniform wall does something beautifully simple: the temperature inside it falls in a perfectly straight ramp from the hot-face temperature \(T_{hot}\) to the cold-face temperature \(T_{cold}\). If \(x\) is how far you are into the wall and \(L\) is its total thickness:

$$ T(x) \;=\; T_{hot} \;-\; \left(T_{hot} - T_{cold}\right)\frac{x}{L} \qquad \text{(steady state, uniform wall)} $$

Think of the ramp as a hill and heat as water running down it. The height of the hill is the temperature difference \(\Delta T = T_{hot} - T_{cold}\) (measured in kelvin, K — a difference of 1 K is the same size as 1 °C). A taller hill means a steeper ramp, and a steeper ramp drives a bigger flow. Equal temperatures? Flat ramp, no flow — heat only ever runs downhill in temperature. The next two tabs put numbers on how much flow.

A winter wall, 10 cm thick. The inside face sits at \(T_{hot} = 21\ \text{°C}\) and the outside face at \(T_{cold} = -9\ \text{°C}\), so \(\Delta T = 21-(-9) = 30\ \text{K}\). The steady-state ramp drops those 30 K evenly across 0.1 m: the gradient is \(30/0.1 = 300\ \text{K per meter}\), i.e. 3 K per centimeter. Halfway through the wall (\(x = L/2\)) the temperature is \(21 - 30\times0.5 = 6\ \text{°C}\) — exactly the average of the two faces.

  • Heat flows through a wall only when the two sides differ: downhill in temperature, always.
  • In steady state, temperatures stop changing but heat keeps flowing — in one face and out the other at the same rate.
  • The steady-state profile inside a uniform wall is a straight ramp from \(T_{hot}\) to \(T_{cold}\); the midpoint sits at the average.
  • \(\Delta T\) is the driver: double the difference, double the steepness, double the flow.
  1. Drag Hot-side temperature up slowly. Watch the ramp steepen and the flow arrows grow — same wall, bigger driver.
  2. Click the No difference scenario (or slide \(T_{cold}\) up to meet \(T_{hot}\)). The ramp goes flat and the arrows vanish. No hill, no flow.
  3. Now push Cold-side temperature above the hot side. Which way do the arrows point? Heat doesn't care which side you labeled "hot".
  4. Try the Oven door scenario and read the midpoint temperature in the stats. Confirm it is the average of the two faces.

Background: material matters — thermal conductivity k

Same wall, same \(\Delta T\) — but swap the material and everything changes. Remember the handoff-speed slider from Lesson 1's bar of atoms? Some atoms and bonds pass their jiggle to the neighbor almost instantly (metals, where free electrons carry it too); others hold onto it stubbornly (wood, foam, still air). Zoom out to human scale and that microscopic handoff speed becomes a single measurable number for each material: the thermal conductivity \(k\), in watts per meter per kelvin (W/m·K). Big \(k\) = eager handoffs = conductor. Tiny \(k\) = reluctant handoffs = insulator.

Put \(k\) together with Tab 1's ramp and you get the workhorse formula of this whole lesson — the rate of heat flow \(q\) (in watts, W, i.e. joules per second) through a wall of face area \(A\) (m²) and thickness \(L\) (m):

$$ q \;=\; \frac{k\,A\,\Delta T}{L} $$

The astonishing part is the range of \(k\) in ordinary life: copper 401, aluminum 237, glass 1.0, brick 0.72, wood 0.15, fiberglass batt 0.04, still air 0.026 W/m·K. That's a factor of about 15,000 between copper and still air — which is why the \(k\) slider below is logarithmic: each step multiplies rather than adds. Nothing else in this lesson varies by anywhere near that much.

Take a wall with \(A = 1\ \text{m}^2\), \(L = 10\ \text{cm} = 0.1\ \text{m}\), and \(\Delta T = 20\ \text{K}\) (cozy inside, freezing outside). Glass (\(k = 1.0\)): \(q = 1.0 \times 1 \times 20 / 0.1 = 200\ \text{W}\). Swap in wood (\(k = 0.15\)): \(q = 30\ \text{W}\). Still air (\(k = 0.026\)): just \(5.2\ \text{W}\). And copper (\(k = 401\)): \(q = 80{,}200\ \text{W}\) — eighty kilowatts, more than fifty space heaters. Same geometry, same \(\Delta T\); the material alone spans four orders of magnitude.

  • \(k\) is Lesson 1's atomic handoff speed measured at human scale: W of heat per m² of wall, per K of drive, per m of thickness.
  • \(q = kA\Delta T/L\): flow is proportional to conductivity — double \(k\), double \(q\).
  • Everyday materials span ~15,000× in \(k\); that range, not \(\Delta T\) or geometry, is the biggest lever you own.
  • Metals conduct so well partly because free electrons carry jiggle in addition to the vibrating lattice.
  1. Start on the Glass scenario and read \(q\) in the stats: 200 W at \(\Delta T = 20\) K.
  2. Click Copper. Same wall, same \(\Delta T\) — how many times more heat is getting through? Check the "vs. glass" stat.
  3. Drag Conductivity k slowly all the way left to Still air, and watch the live dot ride the line down past the wood and glass markers on the log–log chart.
  4. Double Temperature difference ΔT. Does \(q\) double? Compare that gentle lever with what one decade of the \(k\) slider does.

Background: thickness and area — the geometry knobs

Material chosen, \(\Delta T\) set by the weather. Two knobs remain, and you can see both in the formula: area \(A\) sits on top, thickness \(L\) sits on the bottom.

$$ q \;=\; \frac{k\,A\,\Delta T}{L} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad q \propto A \quad\text{and}\quad q \propto \frac{1}{L} $$

Area is the doorway. Twice the wall area means twice as many atomic bucket-brigades working side by side, so twice the flow. This is the parallel-chains idea from Solid Mechanics Lesson 1, wearing a thermal hat. Thickness is the journey. Twice the thickness means every parcel of jiggle must survive twice as many neighbor-to-neighbor handoffs to get across, so the flow halves. Plot \(q\) against \(L\) and you get a hyperbola: brutal at small \(L\) (thin walls leak ferociously), flattening at large \(L\) (past a point, extra thickness buys you less and less).

To isolate geometry, this tab freezes the material at glass-like \(k = 1.0\ \text{W/m·K}\) and the drive at \(\Delta T = 20\ \text{K}\). Only your two sliders move. Keep an eye on the Window pane scenario — it produces a number so large it should bother you. Tab 4 is where we deal with it.

Glass-like wall, \(k = 1.0\ \text{W/m·K}\), \(A = 1\ \text{m}^2\), \(\Delta T = 20\ \text{K}\). At \(L = 10\ \text{cm}\): \(q = 1.0 \times 1 \times 20 / 0.1 = 200\ \text{W}\). Halve the thickness to 5 cm: \(q = 20/0.05 = 400\ \text{W}\) — exactly double. Now shrink it to a window-pane 4 mm with \(A = 2\ \text{m}^2\): \(q = 1.0 \times 2 \times 20 / 0.004 = 10{,}000\ \text{W}\). Ten kilowatts through one window?! Hold that thought.

  • Flow scales up with area: \(q \propto A\) — a bigger doorway for the same bucket-brigade.
  • Flow scales down with thickness: \(q \propto 1/L\) — halve the wall, double the leak.
  • \(q\) vs. \(L\) is a hyperbola: thin walls are catastrophically leaky, and extra thickness has diminishing returns.
  • Thin + wide (a window) is the worst combination a heated house can have — the formula even seems to over-predict it, which Tab 4 resolves.
  1. On the Window pane scenario, read \(q\) in the stats. Write the number down — you will need it in Tab 4.
  2. Drag Thickness L to double its value and confirm \(q\) halves. The orange dot on the chart always previews the "halve L → double q" move.
  3. Compare House wall and Pot bottom: which passes more total watts, and which passes more watts per square meter (the flux stat)?
  4. Challenge: starting from the house wall, find two different single-slider moves that each cut \(q\) in half.

Level up: Fourier's law, thermal resistance, and an honest window

Time for the real machinery. Everything in Tabs 1–3 is the integrated form of Fourier's law of conduction (Fourier, 1822), which says the local heat flux \(q''\) — watts crossing each square meter — is proportional to the local temperature gradient \(dT/dx\), with the minus sign recording that heat runs down the gradient. For a uniform slab in steady state, integrating Fourier's law across the thickness gives back Tab 2's formula, which engineers immediately rearrange into resistance form:

$$ q'' \;=\; -\,k\,\frac{dT}{dx} \qquad\qquad q \;=\; \frac{\Delta T}{R}, \quad R \;=\; \frac{L}{k\,A}\ \ (\text{K/W}) $$

Why bother? Because \(q = \Delta T / R\) is Ohm's law with the labels changed, and every trick you know from circuits transfers wholesale — series resistances add, the biggest resistor hogs the voltage drop, and so on (that becomes Lesson 4):

Thermal Electrical
temperature difference \(\Delta T\) (K) — the driver voltage \(V\) (V)
heat flow \(q\) (W) — the current of energy current \(I\) (A)
thermal resistance \(R = L/(kA)\) (K/W) resistance \(R = V/I\) (Ω)

Now the honesty check the last two tabs have been building toward. A bare slab of glass predicts absurd heat flows — because no real wall touches the hot and cold air directly. A thin layer of nearly still air clings to each face (a boundary layer — Lesson 5's subject), and Tab 2 told you still air is a spectacular insulator. Each clinging film contributes an area-specific resistance \(R'' = R \cdot A\) (units m²·K/W); typical values are ≈0.12 m²·K/W for the calm indoor face and ≈0.05 m²·K/W for the breezier outdoor face — about 0.17 m²·K/W of air, in series with the wall, whether you asked for it or not.

The single-pane window paradox. Glass \(k = 1.0\ \text{W/m·K}\), \(A = 1\ \text{m}^2\), \(L = 4\ \text{mm}\), \(\Delta T = 20\ \text{K}\). Bare glass: \(R_{glass} = L/(kA) = 0.004/(1.0 \times 1) = 0.004\ \text{K/W}\), so \(q = 20/0.004 = 5000\ \text{W}\) — three space heaters pouring out through one pane, which is not what anyone measures. Add the air films: \(R_{films} = 0.17/A = 0.17\ \text{K/W}\), giving \(R_{tot} = 0.004 + 0.17 = 0.174\ \text{K/W}\) and \(q = 20/0.174 \approx 115\ \text{W}\) — the measured ballpark. The glass carries only \(q \cdot R_{glass} \approx 0.46\ \text{K}\) of the 20 K drop (about 2%); the invisible air films carry the rest. The air, not the glass, is the insulation. Lesson 4 stacks layers and traps air on purpose; Lesson 5 studies the films themselves.

  • Fourier's law \(q'' = -k\,dT/dx\) is the local rule; \(q = kA\Delta T/L\) is its steady-state slab solution.
  • Resistance form: \(R = L/(kA)\), \(q = \Delta T/R\) — a perfect Ohm's-law analogy (\(\Delta T \leftrightarrow V\), \(q \leftrightarrow I\), \(R \leftrightarrow R\)).
  • Resistances in series add, and the biggest resistance takes the biggest share of \(\Delta T\).
  • Real surfaces always carry still-air film resistances (≈0.17 m²·K/W total for a window) — for thin conductive walls, the films dominate everything.
  1. On the Bare glass scenario, read \(R_{total}\) and \(q\) in the stats: 0.004 K/W and 5000 W. Look at the circuit — one lonely resistor.
  2. Slide Surface air films up to 0.17 m²·K/W and watch \(q\) collapse along the curve to about 115 W. Two new resistors just appeared in series.
  3. With films on, read the "wall's share of ΔT" stat. The glass is nearly isothermal — check its two face temperatures in the picture.
  4. Click Insulated wall (9 cm of fiberglass). Now drag the film slider from 0 to 0.34: why does \(q\) barely move this time?
