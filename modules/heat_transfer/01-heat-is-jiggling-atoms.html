Heat Is Jiggling Atoms — Why Everything Warms and Cools

Heat Transfer Foundations · Lesson 1 of 6 — Pull a metal spoon and a wooden spoon out of the same drawer. The metal one feels colder — yet a thermometer swears they are exactly the same temperature. The answer is a trillion-atom dance, and you can watch it below.

Background: What temperature actually is

Wrap your hands around a hot mug. What is the "heat" you feel? It is not a fluid soaked into the ceramic, and not a glow trapped inside. Zoom past the glaze, past the molecules, and you find the same picture as in Solid Mechanics Lesson 1 — Atoms as Springs: a huge lattice of atoms connected by bond-springs. The only difference between a hot mug and a cold one is how violently those atoms are rattling around their home positions.

Temperature is a scoreboard for the jiggle. Hot = big, fast rattling. Cold = gentle trembling. Absolute zero (0 K, −273.15 °C) is the temperature where the classical jiggle would stop entirely — the atoms just sit at their lattice sites. That is why physicists count temperature in kelvin: it starts at the true zero of jiggle, and a room at 20 °C is 293 K.

Hotter → each atom wobbles farther. Stiffer bonds → each atom wobbles less far, but faster. And the wobble grows more slowly than the temperature: four times the temperature only doubles the wobble (it grows with the square root).
$$ \text{typical wobble } x \;\propto\; \sqrt{\frac{T}{k_{\text{bond}}}} $$
  • x — how far an atom typically strays from its home spot, in picometers (pm; 1 pm is a trillionth of a meter).
  • T — temperature in kelvin. Bigger T, bigger wobble.
  • kbond — the stiffness of the bond-springs, in newtons per meter (the same k from Solid Mechanics). Stiffer, smaller wobble.
  • ∝ — "is proportional to": the shape of the rule, without the constant in front.

Coming in Tab 4: the exact version, x = √(3kBT/kbond), where kB is the constant that converts kelvins into joules of jiggle. It is what the plot below uses — you do not need it yet.

How big is the jiggle in a metal at room temperature?

  1. Read the sim. At 300 K with k = 20 N/m the stats say the wobble is about 25 pm.
  2. Compare with the spacing. Atoms sit about 250 pm apart, so 25 ÷ 250 = 0.10 — the atom wanders about 10% of the way to its neighbour. A big, restless wobble.
  3. Now triple the temperature. √3 ≈ 1.7, so at 900 K the wobble is about 1.7 × 25 ≈ 43 pm (17% of the spacing). Check it on the slider.
  4. How fast? The stats also give the rattle rate: about 2 trillion round trips per second (2 THz).
  • Heat is not a substance. Temperature measures how violently the atoms jiggle.
  • Hotter → bigger wobble, but only as the square root: 4× the temperature, 2× the wobble.
  • Stiffer bonds → smaller, faster wobble.
  • At room temperature a metal atom wobbles ~10% of the way to its neighbour, trillions of times per second.
  1. Drag Temperature slowly from 10 K to 1000 K. Watch the lattice grow restless and the dot ride up the plot — is the curve a straight line or a √ curve?
  2. Click Near absolute zero. This is what "cold" really means: the atoms all but freeze at their lattice sites (the gray dots).
  3. Raise Bond stiffness k at fixed temperature. The jiggle gets smaller but faster — same energy budget, stiffer cage. Check the amplitude stat.

Background: Heat travels by pass-the-parcel — and it takes time

Leave a metal spoon in hot soup. The handle end — far from the soup — eventually gets hot too. How did the jiggle get up there? Neighbour to neighbour. An atom at the hot end rattles hard, shakes the bond-springs connecting it to its neighbours, and hands some of its energy over. Those neighbours shake their neighbours. The jiggle works its way along the spoon like a bucket brigade with very clumsy hands.

The sim below plays that game with a bar of 40 atom columns whose left end is held hot. Every step, each column looks at its two neighbours and moves part of the way toward their average.

Each step: new energy = old energy + (handoff speed) × (neighbours' average − old energy). The handoff speed D is the fraction of the gap a column closes per step — 45% for a copper-like bar, 4% for a wood-like one.
$$ e_i \;\leftarrow\; e_i \;+\; D\left(\frac{e_{i-1}+e_{i+1}}{2} \;-\; e_i\right) $$
  • ei — the energy (shown as temperature) of column number i.
  • ei−1, ei+1 — its left and right neighbours; the fraction is their average.
  • D — the handoff speed, between 0 and 0.5. It is the whole personality of the material: copper hands off eagerly (big D), wood hoards (tiny D).
  • ← — "becomes": the new value replaces the old one, then the next step begins.

Because each handoff is aimless — energy dribbles both directions — the warm front creeps forward like a random walk. It advances only as the square root of time (distance ∝ √t): four times the time buys only twice the distance. Heat is not a wave racing down the bar; it is a drunken shuffle.

Run one step by hand, then use the √t rule.

  1. Set up. A column sits at 300 K between neighbours at 800 K and 300 K, with D = 0.4.
  2. Neighbours' average: (800 + 300) ÷ 2 = 550 K. The gap to close is 550 − 300 = 250 K.
  3. Close 40% of it: 0.4 × 250 = 100 K, so the column becomes 300 + 100 = 400 K.
  4. The √t rule: if the warm front needs 4 s to reach column 10, reaching column 20 (twice as far) needs 2 × 2 = 4 times as long: 16 s. Double the distance costs quadruple the time.
  • Conduction is jiggle passed neighbour-to-neighbour through the bonds — nothing physically flows.
  • The handoff speed D is conductivity at atom scale: copper hands off eagerly, wood hoards.
  • The warm front creeps as √t — a random walk, not a wave. Doubling the distance quadruples the wait.
  • That √t creep is why the near end of the spoon burns you long before the handle warms.
  1. Click Restart pulse and watch the "Half-way front" stat: time how long it takes to reach column 10, then column 20. Is it roughly 4× longer? That's √t.
  2. Set Handoff speed D to the minimum and restart. This is wood: the left columns glow red while the rest of the bar stays stubbornly blue.
  3. Compare the Copper-like and Wood-like scenarios over the same 10 seconds of sim time. How much farther does the copper front get?
  4. Raise Hot-end temperature. The profile gets taller — but does the front position move any faster? (The shape of the creep belongs to \(D\), not to how hot the source is.)

Background: Three delivery services for the jiggle

So far the jiggle has travelled one way: passed along fixed atoms. But put a hot wall and a cold wall face to face and heat has three delivery services to choose from. Conduction is the relay race from Tab 2 — atoms stay put and hand the jiggle down the line. Convection is a courier service: in a liquid or gas, the atoms are free to move, so a parcel of air can pick up jiggle at the hot wall, physically fly across the gap, and dump it on the cold wall. Radiation is a broadcast: a rattling atom is a shaking electric charge, and shaking charges emit light (mostly invisible infrared). That light crosses the gap at light speed — with no atoms needed at all — and becomes jiggle again wherever it is absorbed.

All three follow the same shape: heat flow = (how good the route is) × (wall area) × (temperature difference). Bigger wall, more flow. Bigger difference, more flow. The three services differ only in "how good the route is" — and radiation is the odd one out, because its route gets dramatically better as things get hotter.
$$ q \;=\; (\text{route quality}) \times A \times \Delta T $$
  • q — heat flow, in watts (joules per second). The sim reports it per square meter of wall, W/m².
  • A — the wall area, m².
  • ΔT — "delta T", the temperature difference, hot side minus cold side, in kelvin.
  • route quality — for conduction it is the material's conductivity divided by the gap width; for convection it is a single number engineers call h; for radiation it grows with the fourth power of temperature.

The three exact recipes (Fourier's, Newton's and the Stefan–Boltzmann law) are written out in Tab 4, and each gets its own lesson later: conduction in Lesson 3, convection in Lesson 5, radiation in Lesson 6.

Now you can explain a thermos bottle. It is two nested flasks with the air pumped out of the gap. No atoms in the gap → no conduction relay, no convection courier. Only radiation survives — and the walls are silvered to choke that too. Three assassins, three separate alibis: that is why coffee stays hot all morning.

A 600 K hot wall faces a 300 K cold wall across a 1 cm gap (ΔT = 300 K, per square meter of wall).

  1. Solid glass in the gap. Route quality = conductivity ÷ gap = 1.0 ÷ 0.01 = 100. Flow = 100 × 300 = 30,000 W/m².
  2. Air in the gap. Conduction: 0.026 ÷ 0.01 × 300 = 780. Convection with h = 5: 5 × 300 = 1500. Radiation from the fourth-power rule (Tab 4 shows the arithmetic): about 6200. Total ≈ 8500 W/m² — less than a third of the solid.
  3. Vacuum in the gap. Only radiation survives: ≈ 6200 W/m². Silver the walls and even that drops about 18×, to ≈ 340 — nearly 100× less than the solid. That is a thermos.
  • Conduction = atoms stay put and relay the jiggle; convection = moving fluid carries it; radiation = the jiggle travels as light.
  • Every service follows heat flow = route quality × area × temperature difference.
  • Conduction and convection need atoms in the gap; radiation crosses a perfect vacuum.
  • A thermos kills conduction and convection with vacuum and starves radiation with silvered walls.
  1. Start on Air between: all three lanes are alive. Read the bar chart — which mechanism delivers the most at the default 600 K?
  2. Click Solid between. Convection and radiation go dark — the atoms can't flow and the solid is opaque — yet the total heat flow jumps. Why?
  3. Click Vacuum between. Only the photon lane survives. Compare the total against the other two scenarios in the stats — this is the thermos trick.
  4. On Vacuum, sweep Hot-wall temperature from 350 K to 1200 K and watch \(q_{\text{rad}}\). Does doubling the temperature merely double the radiation? (It's a \(T^4\) law.)

Level up: the exact rules — equipartition, the three recipes, and the diffusion clock

Everything in Tabs 1–3 stands on its own. This tab is where the real machinery lives, written the way a first-year physics or engineering course writes it. Take it one box at a time.

Box 1 — the jiggle budget. Nature hands each atom a fixed amount of jiggle energy per kelvin. The exchange rate is Boltzmann's constant, and a bound atom gets three of them.
$$ \bar{E}_{\text{atom}} \;=\; 3\,k_B T \qquad\qquad x_{\text{typ}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{3\,k_B\,T}{k_{\text{bond}}}} $$
  • kB — Boltzmann's constant, 1.38 × 10⁻²³ joules per kelvin: the conversion factor from temperature to energy per atom.
  • Ēatom — the average jiggle energy of one atom. The equipartition theorem gives ½kBT to each of six "directions" (moving in x, y, z and stretching springs in x, y, z), so 3kBT in all.
  • xtyp — Tab 1's wobble, now exact: a spring holding energy ½kbondx² with the budget 3kBT stretches to x = √(3kBT/kbond). Multiply 3kB by Avogadro's number and you get the Dulong–Petit heat capacity, 3R — Lesson 2's punchline.
Box 2 — the three delivery recipes. Tab 3's "route quality", spelled out for each service.
$$ q_{\text{cond}} = \frac{k\,A\,\Delta T}{L} \qquad\quad q_{\text{conv}} = h\,A\,\Delta T \qquad\quad q_{\text{rad}} = \varepsilon\,\sigma\,A\left(T_{\text{hot}}^{4} - T_{\text{cold}}^{4}\right) $$
  • k — thermal conductivity (W/m·K), L — gap width (m): conduction's route quality is k/L (Lesson 3).
  • h — the convection coefficient (W/m²K): how good the moving fluid is at hauling heat (Lesson 5).
  • ε — emissivity, 0 to 1, how well the surface glows; σ — the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴. Radiation uses the absolute temperatures to the fourth power (Lesson 6).
Box 3 — the diffusion clock. How fast heat soaks through a material is a competition: conductivity moves energy, but the material's heat capacity must be filled along the way. Their ratio is the diffusivity α, an "area per second" — exactly what a random walk needs. Soak time = thickness squared ÷ α.
$$ \alpha \;=\; \frac{k}{\rho\,c} \qquad\qquad \tau \;\approx\; \frac{L^2}{\alpha} $$
  • ρc — density × specific heat (J/m³·K): the joules it takes to warm a cubic meter by one kelvin (Lesson 2 meets c).
  • α — thermal diffusivity, m²/s. Copper 1.16 × 10⁻⁴, still air 2.2 × 10⁻⁵, glass 3.4 × 10⁻⁷, wood 1.9 × 10⁻⁷.
  • τ — "tau", the soak time through a thickness L. Distance is squared because heat wanders instead of marching: a walker taking N steps of size a gets only a√N away, so covering L needs (L/a)² steps.

This resolves a real paradox: each atomic handoff takes about a picosecond, yet your coffee takes an hour to cool. Atoms are fast; the path is a squared-up random shuffle. On the log-log plot below, τ = L²/α is a straight line of slope 2 — every 10× in size costs 100× in time.

How long does heat take to soak through 1 cm?

  1. Copper (α = 1.16 × 10⁻⁴ m²/s): τ = (0.01)² ÷ 1.16 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.0001 ÷ 0.000116 ≈ 0.9 s.
  2. Wood (α = 1.9 × 10⁻⁷): τ = 0.0001 ÷ 0.00000019 ≈ 530 s ≈ 9 minutes.
  3. Compare. Same slab, same ΔT — copper is about 600× faster (1.16 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 1.9 × 10⁻⁷ ≈ 610). The spoon riddle from the header is solved twice over: metal both conducts your hand's heat away faster and feels it deeper, sooner.
  • Equipartition: ½kBT per quadratic degree of freedom → 3kBT per atom in a solid.
  • The three recipes: q = kAΔT/L, q = hAΔT, q = εσA(Thot⁴ − Tcold⁴).
  • Thermal diffusivity α = k/ρc (m²/s) is the material's random-walk "area per second".
  • The diffusion clock τ ≈ L²/α: distance is squared because heat wanders, it doesn't march.
  1. On the Copper scenario, set Length scale L to 1 cm and read τ (~0.9 s). Switch to Wood: same slab, ~9 minutes.
  2. Sweep Length scale L from 1 mm to 1 m. The dot rides a straight line of slope 2 on the log-log axes — check that every 10× in L costs 100× in τ.
  3. Find the L where the Wood line crosses the "1 day" reference line (~13 cm). That's why thick log walls smooth out day–night temperature swings.
  4. Click Air (still) and use the Diffusivity α slider to compare: still air diffuses heat faster than glass or wood — yet air is a famous insulator. What's missing from this picture? (Hint: Tab 3's second lane; the full answer is Lesson 4.)
