Fins & Flowing Air — Engineering Heat Away

Heat Transfer Foundations · Lesson 5 of 6 — You blow on hot soup and it actually works. A CPU cooler is a spiky forest of metal. Both exist because the hardest part of heat's escape is the last few millimetres of air clinging to a hot surface — and engineers have exactly two tricks to beat it: move the air, or grow more surface.

Background: air steals heat — but only through a thin, lazy blanket

Hold your hand a few centimetres above a hot pan (don't touch!). You feel warm air rising. Air that touches the hot surface warms up, gets lighter, and floats away; cooler room air slides in to take its place, warms up, and floats away too. The air is running a conveyor belt that hauls heat off the surface. That conveyor is convection — you met it briefly in Lesson 1, and now it gets the full treatment.

But zoom in on the surface itself and there's a catch. The air right against the plate is essentially stuck to it — fluids cling to surfaces — and the air just above that moves only sluggishly. This slow-moving film hugging the surface is called the boundary layer, and it is the bottleneck of the whole operation: heat must first conduct across this nearly-still air blanket (and Lesson 3 taught you how bad still air is at conducting) before the moving air beyond it can carry the heat off.

Engineers wrap all of that messy fluid physics into one honest number, h, and write Newton's law of cooling: heat stolen by the air = h × surface area × (how much hotter the surface is than the room). Double the difference, double the theft. Bigger h, thinner blanket.
$$ q \;=\; h\,A\,(T_s - T_\infty) $$
  • q — heat flow, W. A — surface area, m².
  • Ts — the surface temperature.
  • T∞ — "T-infinity": the air temperature far from the surface — the room.
  • h — the convection coefficient, W/m²K: how good the air is at hauling heat away from each square metre per degree of difference. Still room air manages only about 5–25; a fan can push it toward 100.

A useful way to picture h: it behaves like a still-air film of effective thickness δ ≈ kair/h (with kair ≈ 0.026 W/m·K from Lesson 3) — crank h up and the blanket thins. The stats show that thickness.

A mug of coffee: outer surface 0.03 m², coffee at 60 °C in a 20 °C room, still air h ≈ 10.

  1. The difference: 60 − 20 = 40 K.
  2. Newton's law: q = 10 × 0.03 × 40 = 12 W — a dim night-light of heat leaking out.
  3. How long to cool 10 degrees? Lesson 2: 0.3 kg × 4186 × 10 ≈ 12,600 J must leave. At 12 W that takes 12,600 ÷ 12 ≈ 1050 s ≈ 17 minutes.
  4. Sanity check: coffee goes lukewarm on exactly the timescale of a meeting.
  • Moving air steals heat from surfaces: q = hA(Ts − T∞) — Newton's law of cooling.
  • The bottleneck is the boundary layer: a thin film of nearly-still air hugging the surface that heat must conduct across first.
  • h bundles all the fluid physics into one number: bigger h = thinner blanket = faster theft.
  • Double the temperature difference and you double the heat flow — the line through the origin has slope hA.

Background: blow on it — forced convection

Your soup instinct is real engineering. When you blow across a hot spoonful, you rip the warmed air blanket off the surface and replace it with fresh cool air before it has time to heat up. In the language of Tab 1: blowing thins the boundary layer, which raises h. Convection you have to pay for with a fan, wind, or your own lungs is called forced convection; the free version driven by warm air floating upward is natural convection.

How much does wind help? A famous old rule of thumb, measured on flat surfaces: h starts near 6 even in still air, and every extra metre-per-second of wind adds about 4.
$$ h \;\approx\; 5.7 \;+\; 3.8\,v $$
  • v — wind speed in m/s. h comes out in W/m²K.
  • Be honest about what this is: an empirical fit from W. H. McAdams' 1954 textbook, good to order of magnitude for air blowing along a flat surface — not a law of nature. The trend is exactly right.
Now the payoff: how long does a hot object take to cool? A small object that stays close to one temperature throughout slides toward room temperature in a curve that keeps flattening. The curve has one characteristic time, the time constant: the time to close 63% of the remaining gap. And the time constant is simply thermal mass ÷ (h × area) — so triple h and the whole cooling movie plays three times faster. That is, precisely, why you blow on soup.
$$ \tau \;=\; \frac{m\,c}{h\,A} $$
  • τ — "tau", the time constant, in seconds. After one τ the object has closed 63% of the gap to room temperature; after 3τ, 95%.
  • m, c — mass and specific heat (Lesson 2): together the thermal mass mc.
  • h, A — the convection coefficient and the surface area (Tab 1).

The curve's exact shape is an exponential, T(t) = T∞ + (T0 − T∞)e−t/τ; the sim draws it for you. Tab 4 says when "small enough to be one temperature" is a safe assumption (the Biot number).

The sim's object is a small hot copper ball: 20 g, c = 385, surface 8.2 cm² (0.00082 m²), starting at 90 °C in a 20 °C room.

  1. Thermal mass: mc = 0.02 × 385 = 7.7 J/K.
  2. Still air (v = 0): h ≈ 5.7, so τ = 7.7 ÷ (5.7 × 0.00082) ≈ 1650 s ≈ 27 min.
  3. A breeze (v = 4 m/s): h ≈ 5.7 + 3.8 × 4 ≈ 21, so τ ≈ 7.7 ÷ (21 × 0.00082) ≈ 450 s ≈ 7.5 min.
  4. The payoff: blowing made the cooling 27 ÷ 7.5 ≈ 3.7× faster — same ball, same room, thinner blanket.
  • Wind thins the boundary layer, so h climbs — roughly h ≈ 5.7 + 3.8v (an old empirical wind formula; order of magnitude only).
  • Small objects cool along a flattening curve with time constant τ = mc/(hA).
  • τ ∝ 1/h: triple the convection, triple the cooling speed. Blowing on soup is legitimate thermal engineering.
  • Even "still" air gives h ≈ 5 for free — natural convection never fully switches off.
  1. Start at Wind speed = 0. Watch the particles near the plate crawl and glow — the boundary layer is thick. Now slide to 15 m/s and watch it visibly thin as everything streams past.
  2. Find the wind speed that halves the still-air time constant τ (hint: τ ∝ 1/h, so you need h ≈ 11.4 — check the stats as you slide).
  3. Compare the Breeze and Leaf blower scenarios: read the "time to reach 30 °C" stat for each. Worth the noise?
  4. Look at the h-vs-v plot: it's a straight line, yet the cooling curves bunch up at high wind. Going 0→5 m/s changes τ far more than 10→15 m/s. Why? (τ ∝ 1/h.)

Background: can't move the air harder? Grow more surface

Look inside a computer, at a motorcycle engine, at the back of an old refrigerator — all spiky, ridged, bladed. Why? Newton's law gives an engineer three knobs. The temperature difference is usually fixed by the job (your CPU must stay below ~90 °C in a 25 °C case). Raising h costs a bigger, louder fan. But area is nearly free — it's just shaped metal. So we bolt on fins: thin blades sticking out into the air, each one adding two whole faces of extra surface for the air to steal heat from.

Total area = base area + (number of fins) × (two faces each) × (fin length × fin depth). Then Newton's law as before: heat flow = h × total area × temperature difference.
$$ A_{\text{total}} \;=\; A_{\text{base}} \;+\; N \cdot 2\,L\,D \qquad\qquad q \;=\; h\,A_{\text{total}}\,(T_s - T_\infty) $$
  • N — the number of fins. L — how far each fin sticks out. D — the fin's depth (into the screen). Each fin contributes both of its faces, 2LD.
  • In this sim the base plate is 10 cm × 10 cm, fins are 2 mm thick, the surface runs 40 K above the room, and h = 15 is held fixed — only the geometry changes. (We also assume the fins are perfectly conductive, uniformly hot metal — Tab 4 confesses and repairs that lie.)

One catch, and you can find it with the sliders: crowding. Pack fins too close and the channels between them choke — fresh air can no longer flow through, each fin sits in its neighbour's warmed exhaust, and extra fins stop paying rent. Our sim applies a gentle spacing-based derating to capture this, and we say plainly: the derating is qualitative — real heatsink design uses measured flow correlations. But the lesson it teaches is real: there is an optimum fin spacing (a few millimetres, in gently moving air), and "more fins" is not always "more cooling".

Add fins to a bare 10 cm × 10 cm plate (h = 15, 40 K above the room).

  1. Bare plate: A = 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01 m², so q = 15 × 0.01 × 40 = 6 W.
  2. Add 10 fins, 30 mm long, 0.1 m deep: fin area = 10 × 2 × 0.03 × 0.1 = 0.06 m². Total = 0.07 m².
  3. New flow: q = 15 × 0.07 × 40 = 42 W — seven times the bare plate, from the same footprint.
  4. Crowding: at this spacing (about 7 mm) the sim's crowding factor is 0.93, trimming the answer to ≈ 40 W.
  • Fins attack the A in q = hAΔT: surface area is the cheapest knob an engineer has.
  • Each fin adds two faces: Atotal = Abase + N × 2LD — that's why heatsinks look like combs.
  • Crowding is real: fins packed too tightly choke the airflow between them, and the gain rolls off (our derating is qualitative, the effect is not).
  • There is a sweet spot in fin count — after it, more metal buys almost no more cooling.
  1. Set Fin length to 30 mm and sweep Fin count from 0 to 30. Watch the blue curve peel away from the dashed ideal line as the channels narrow.
  2. Hold N = 10 and stretch the fins from 5 to 60 mm. Longer fins add area with no crowding penalty — watch q climb.
  3. Load the Crammed fins scenario and read the spacing and crowding-factor stats. Thirty fins, and most of them are suffocating.
  4. Hunt for the sweet spot: at L = 40 mm, find the fin count that gives the biggest q (it's near N ≈ 19–20, not 30!).

Level up: fin efficiency and the Biot number

Everything in Tabs 1–3 stands on its own. This tab repairs Tab 3's one white lie.

Tab 3 assumed every fin is uniformly hot from root to tip. A real fin can't be: heat must conduct along the metal while it continuously leaks out the sides into the air, so the temperature sags toward the tip — and the cool tip steals less heat than we paid for. One number captures the competition: m, which measures how quickly the fin's temperature sags per metre of length. Small m × length → the whole fin stays hot. Big → the outer end is dead metal.
$$ m \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{h\,P}{k\,A_c}} \qquad\quad \eta \;=\; \frac{\tanh(mL)}{mL} $$
  • m — the fin parameter, in 1/m — careful, not mass. Sideways leakage (h × perimeter P) on top, along-the-fin conduction (k × cross-section Ac) on the bottom. Roughly, 1/m is how far heat penetrates along the fin before the air has bled it away.
  • P — the fin's perimeter (the exposed edge length of its cross-section); Ac — the cross-sectional area the heat conducts through; k — the metal's conductivity.
  • mL — the dimensionless product that tells you everything: mL ≪ 1 → the whole fin stays hot; mL ≫ 1 → the outer end is dead metal.
  • η — "eta", the fin efficiency: the fraction of the ideal, uniformly-hot heat flow the fin actually delivers. It falls from 1 as mL grows; tanh is just the curve shape a calculator gives you (the sim computes it).

The full temperature profile along the fin, from an energy balance on a slice with an insulated tip, is θ(x)/θb = cosh m(L−x) / cosh mL, where θ = T − T∞ is the "excess temperature" and θb its value at the base. The second canvas draws it.

The same physics gives the general "is it all one temperature?" test used everywhere in heat transfer: the Biot number, the ratio of the conduction resistance inside a body to the convection resistance at its surface. Below about 0.1 the inside equalises much faster than the surface can leak, and the body is effectively one temperature — exactly what Tab 2's cooling curve assumed.
$$ \mathrm{Bi} \;=\; \frac{h\,L_c}{k} \qquad (\mathrm{Bi} < 0.1 \Rightarrow \text{"all one temperature"}) $$
  • Lc — a characteristic length: for our fin's cross-section, the half-thickness t/2. Metals in air almost always pass; that's why fin theory only worries about the sag along the fin, not across it.

An aluminum plate fin, per metre of width: k = 205 W/m·K, thickness 2 mm, length 30 mm, h = 25.

  1. Perimeter and cross-section per metre of width: P ≈ 2 m (two faces), Ac = 0.002 m².
  2. The fin parameter: m = √(25 × 2 ÷ (205 × 0.002)) = √122 ≈ 11 per metre.
  3. mL: 11 × 0.03 = 0.33 — small. Efficiency η = tanh(0.33) ÷ 0.33 ≈ 0.97: a short, fat aluminum fin is 97% of perfect.
  4. Swap in steel (k = 45): m = √(50 ÷ 0.09) ≈ 23.6, mL ≈ 0.71, η = tanh(0.71) ÷ 0.71 ≈ 0.86 — and falling fast if you make it any longer.
  5. Biot check: Bi = 25 × 0.001 ÷ 205 ≈ 0.00012 ≪ 0.1 — across its thickness the fin really is one temperature.
  • Real fins sag toward the tip; the fin parameter m = √(hP/(kAc)) (not mass!) sets how fast.
  • Fin efficiency η = tanh(mL)/mL: keep mL small — short, thick, high-k fins stay hot and earn their area.
  • If η drops below ~0.75, the outer fin is dead metal: make it shorter, fatter, or more conductive.
  • Biot number Bi = hLc/k < 0.1 means "all one temperature" — the license behind lumped cooling (Tab 2) and 1-D fin theory itself.
  1. Load the Aluminum scenario and check the stats against the worked example: m ≈ 11 m⁻¹, mL ≈ 0.33, η ≈ 0.97.
  2. Switch to Steel. Same shape, same air — watch the profile sag and the tip go cold. η drops to ≈ 0.86.
  3. Staying on steel, rescue the fin two different ways: shorten Fin length L until η is back above 0.95 (≈ 16 mm), or instead thicken Thickness t toward 9 mm. Shorter or fatter — both shrink mL.
  4. Back on aluminum, crank Convection h to 100. Even the best conductor struggles when the air steals faster than the metal can resupply — watch η and the verdict.
