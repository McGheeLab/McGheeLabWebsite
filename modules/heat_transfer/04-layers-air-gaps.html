Layers, Air Gaps & Insulation — Boundaries Inside Boundaries

Heat Transfer Foundations · Lesson 4 of 6 — A puffy winter jacket is mostly nothing: feathers holding millions of tiny pockets of trapped air. So why does a bag of nothing keep you warmer than a solid sheet of metal ever could? Stack some walls and find out.

Background: boundaries inside boundaries

In Lesson 3 you pushed heat through one wall. Real walls are almost never one material: a house wall is brick, then foam, then plasterboard; a coffee mug is ceramic, then glaze; a double-pane window is glass–air–glass. When heat has to cross two layers in a row, it must fight through the first, then the second — the difficulties don't average out, they add up.

Each layer has a thermal resistance: \(R = L/k\), where \(L\) is the layer's thickness (in meters) and \(k\) is its thermal conductivity (in W/m·K — how easily that material passes the jiggle along, from Lesson 1). Big \(R\) means "hard to cross." For layers stacked in series, the same heat flow \(q\) (in watts) must pass through every layer, so:

$$ R_{\text{tot}} = R_1 + R_2 = \frac{L_1}{k_1} + \frac{L_2}{k_2} \qquad\qquad q = \frac{A\,\Delta T}{R_{\text{tot}}} $$

Here \(\Delta T\) is the total temperature difference across the wall (hot side minus cold side, in kelvin) and \(A\) is the wall area (m²; the sim uses 1 m²). Now the beautiful part: since the same \(q\) crosses both layers, each layer's share of the temperature drop is proportional to its resistance — \(\Delta T_i = q\,R_i\). Plot temperature across the wall and you get a staircase: a steep ramp across the hard-to-cross layer, a nearly flat shelf across the easy one. The worst conductor eats almost the whole temperature drop — and that layer is doing essentially all the insulating. The chain is only as slow as its slowest link.

Brick + foam wall, 1 m², inside 20 °C, outside −5 °C (\(\Delta T = 25\) K). Brick: \(k_1 = 0.72\) W/m·K, \(L_1 = 0.10\) m → \(R_1 = 0.10/0.72 = 0.139\) m²K/W. Foam: \(k_2 = 0.035\) W/m·K, \(L_2 = 0.05\) m → \(R_2 = 0.05/0.035 = 1.43\) m²K/W. Total \(R = 1.57\) m²K/W, so \(q = 25/1.57 \approx 16\) W. The foam, though half as thick, holds \(1.43/1.57 = 91\%\) of the temperature drop: the brick–foam interface sits at \(20 - 16\times0.139 \approx 17.8\) °C — the brick barely notices winter. Remove the foam and \(q\) jumps to \(25/0.139 \approx 180\) W.

  • Layers in series: resistances add, \(R_{\text{tot}} = \sum L_i/k_i\), and the same \(q\) crosses every layer.
  • Each layer's temperature drop is proportional to its resistance: \(\Delta T_i = qR_i\). The profile is a staircase, steep where \(R\) is big.
  • The highest-resistance layer dominates — one good insulating layer does nearly all the work.
  • Adding a conductive layer (metal, glass) barely changes anything; its \(R\) is a rounding error.
  1. Start on Brick + foam. Look at the temperature staircase: which layer is steep? Check the stats — what fraction of the 25 K drop does the foam carry?
  2. Drag Layer 2 conductivity up toward brick's value. Watch the staircase flatten into one straight ramp and \(q\) climb.
  3. Switch to Copper + steel. Both resistances are microscopic — read \(q\) in the stats. Why do metal walls make terrible houses?
  4. On Glass + glass, double Layer 2's thickness. Does \(q\) halve? (It shouldn't — only \(R_2\) doubled, not \(R_{\text{tot}}\). Predict the new \(q\) before you look.)

Background: air — terrible conductor, great insulator

Here is the number that explains jackets, thermoses, and double glazing: still air has \(k_{\text{air}} = 0.026\) W/m·K. Compare copper's 401 — air conducts about 15,000× worse than copper, and about 6× worse than wood. Air molecules are far apart, so the atomic jiggle from Lesson 1 gets handed on only during rare collisions. A layer of air is one of the best thermal barriers you can buy, and it's free.

So why not build walls with huge air cavities? Because the number above has a catch: it holds only if the air stands still. Air is a fluid. In a wide gap, the air touching the warm side heats up, expands, becomes lighter, and rises; air on the cold side sinks. A slow circulation loop forms — natural convection — and now the air physically carries hot molecules across the gap instead of waiting for collision-by-collision handoff. In a narrow gap, friction against the two walls smothers the loop before it can start; past a critical width the loop wins. We summarize this with an effective conductivity:

$$ k_{\text{eff}} = \mathrm{Nu}\cdot k_{\text{air}} \qquad R_{\text{gap}} = \frac{d}{k_{\text{eff}}} \qquad \mathrm{Nu} \approx 1 \text{ (still, narrow gap)}, \quad \mathrm{Nu} > 1 \text{ (circulating)} $$

Here \(d\) is the gap width (m) and \(\mathrm{Nu}\) (the Nusselt number) is the "convection multiplier": how many times worse the gap performs than perfectly still air. In real enclosures the switch is set by the Rayleigh number (a balance of buoyancy vs. friction), which grows like \(d^3\) — the sim below uses a qualitative threshold model with the loop switching on near \(d \approx 15\) mm, faithful in trend, not a design correlation. Real gaps also exchange heat by radiation (Lesson 6), which is why handbook air-space values are lower than the pure-conduction numbers here.

A 12 mm gap of still air: \(R = d/k = 0.012/0.026 \approx 0.46\) m²K/W. The same 12 mm of copper: \(0.012/401 = 3.0\times10^{-5}\) m²K/W — the air layer resists about 15,400× more. Now widen the gap to 40 mm hoping for \(0.040/0.026 = 1.54\): the circulation loop switches on, \(\mathrm{Nu}\) climbs to several, and the effective resistance falls back toward ~0.2 m²K/W. More air, less insulation. The sweet spot for a vertical air gap is roughly 10–20 mm.

  • Still air is a superb insulator: \(k = 0.026\) W/m·K, ~15,000× worse a conductor than copper.
  • Air only stays still in a narrow gap; wide gaps grow a convection loop that ferries heat across.
  • \(R_{\text{gap}} = d/(\mathrm{Nu}\,k_{\text{air}})\): resistance rises with width, then convection (\(\mathrm{Nu}>1\)) claws it back. There is an optimum width.
  • Filling the gap with foam kills convection permanently — that's the entire job of insulation: keep the air, stop the motion.
  1. Start on Thin trapped gap (6 mm). The air particles just quiver in place — no loop. Watch \(R_{\text{gap}}\) in the stats as you nudge the width up 1 mm at a time.
  2. Keep widening past ~15 mm. The circulation loop appears and speeds up. Find the gap width where \(R_{\text{gap}}\) peaks on the curve — that's the design sweet spot.
  3. Go to Wide cavity (40 mm). More air than ever, yet check the verdict — where did the resistance go?
  4. Click Foam-filled. The loop dies. Compare \(R_{\text{gap}}\) at 40 mm foam vs. 40 mm open air — same width, wildly different wall.

Background: millions of tiny boundaries — foams and fluff

Tab 2 left us with a problem: air is a wonderful insulator, but only in gaps too narrow to let it circulate. So here's the engineering trick behind almost every insulation ever made: chop one big air gap into millions of tiny ones. A foam, a fiberglass batt, a down jacket — each is a flimsy solid skeleton whose only real job is to hold air pockets so small that convection can never start inside them. The material is mostly nothing; the nothing is the insulation.

Two numbers describe such a material. Porosity \(\phi\) (phi) is the fraction of the volume that is air (a fiberglass batt is ~95%+ air). Pore size \(d\) is how big each trapped pocket is. Heat crossing the material must thread a maze: through slow air pockets, across countless solid–air interfaces, along skinny solid struts. The exact effective conductivity \(k_{\text{eff}}\) depends on the maze's geometry, but two clean limits bracket it — layers of solid and air stacked along the heat flow (parallel, best case for conduction) or across it (series, worst case):

$$ k_{\parallel} = \phi\,k_{\text{air}} + (1-\phi)\,k_s \qquad k_{\perp} = \left(\frac{\phi}{k_{\text{air}}} + \frac{1-\phi}{k_s}\right)^{-1} \qquad k_{\text{eff}} \approx \sqrt{k_{\parallel}\,k_{\perp}} $$

Here \(k_s\) is the conductivity of the solid skeleton (glass fiber, polystyrene…) and \(k_{\text{air}} = 0.026\) W/m·K. The geometric-mean blend in the last step is an honest estimate, not a law — real foams land between the two bounds, and ours simply picks the middle. Pore size sneaks in twice at the extremes: pores wider than ~10 mm start to convect (Tab 2's loop, in miniature), while pores smaller than about 100 nanometers — aerogel territory — are narrower than the average distance an air molecule flies between collisions, so the trapped gas conducts even worse than free air. That's how aerogel reaches \(k \approx 0.015\) W/m·K, below still air itself.

Fiberglass batt: glass skeleton \(k_s = 1.0\) W/m·K, porosity \(\phi = 0.95\), pores ~0.1 mm (no convection, no nano effects, so the pocket air keeps \(k_{\text{air}} = 0.026\)). Bounds: \(k_{\parallel} = 0.95(0.026) + 0.05(1.0) = 0.0747\); \(k_{\perp} = (0.95/0.026 + 0.05/1.0)^{-1} = (36.6+0.05)^{-1} = 0.0273\) W/m·K. Estimate: \(k_{\text{eff}} \approx \sqrt{0.0747 \times 0.0273} \approx 0.045\) W/m·K — the measured value is ~0.04, so the simple blend lands close. A 100 mm batt gives \(R = 0.1/0.045 \approx 2.2\) m²K/W; the same 100 mm of solid glass gives only 0.10. Being 95% nothing made the wall 22× better.

  • Insulation = a solid skeleton whose job is to trap air in pockets too small to convect. Down, foam, fiberglass, and double glazing are all the same trick.
  • Porosity \(\phi\) sets how close \(k_{\text{eff}}\) gets to \(k_{\text{air}}\); the skeleton's \(k_s\) matters surprisingly little once \(\phi\) is high.
  • Series/parallel bounds bracket the truth; our geometric-mean blend is an estimate, not a design value.
  • Pore size has sweet spots: >10 mm pores convect (bad); <100 nm pores choke gas conduction below still air (aerogel's superpower).
  1. Start on Fiberglass batt. Drag Porosity from 0% to 95% and watch the cross-section fill with pockets while \(k_{\text{eff}}\) collapses toward air's 0.026.
  2. At 95% porosity, drag Skeleton conductivity from glass (1.0) up to copper (401). A 400× worse skeleton — how much does \(k_{\text{eff}}\) actually rise? Why so tame? (Look at which bound moves.)
  3. Click Aerogel and watch the pore-size readout drop to nanometers. Check the stats: the pocket air now conducts worse than 0.026 — and \(k_{\text{eff}}\) dips below still air.
  4. Drag Pore size all the way up past 10 mm. The verdict flips: your "insulation" has become a stack of convecting cavities.

Level up: resistance networks, U-values, and the R-value on the package

Time to assemble the whole toolkit. A real wall is a series resistance network: every layer contributes \(L_i/(k_i A)\), and — as you learned the hard way in Lesson 3 — the thin films of nearly-still air clinging to each surface contribute resistances of their own. For a wall of area \(A\) (m²) separating temperatures \(T_{\text{in}}\) and \(T_{\text{out}}\):

$$ q = \frac{T_{\text{in}} - T_{\text{out}}}{R_{\text{tot}}}, \qquad R_{\text{tot}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{h_{\text{in}}A}}_{\text{inside film}} + \sum_i \frac{L_i}{k_i A} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{h_{\text{out}}A}}_{\text{outside film}} $$

Here \(h_{\text{in}}\) and \(h_{\text{out}}\) are the surface heat-transfer coefficients (W/m²K — properly introduced in Lesson 5; for now the films are a fixed 0.17 m²K/W of area-specific resistance, a typical still-indoor + outdoor pair). Working per square meter (\(A = 1\) m²), engineers compress the whole network into two ratings. The U-value (or U-factor), \(U = 1/R''_{\text{tot}}\) in W/m²K, is the heat a window or wall leaks per square meter per kelvin — lower is better. Insulation packaging instead quotes the R-value; in US units (ft²·°F·hr/BTU) it is 5.678× the SI value, so "R-13" fiberglass means \(R_{\text{SI}} \approx 13/5.678 \approx 2.3\) m²K/W (often written RSI 2.3). Same physics, different unit costume.

The interface temperatures fall straight out of the network: walking from the warm side, each element drops \(\Delta T_i = q R_i\), giving the staircase you've been reading all lesson — now with two extra small steps at the surfaces where the films live. Build any three-layer wall below and the sim solves the full chain.

Single pane vs. double pane, 1 m² of window, \(\Delta T = 20\) K, films totaling 0.17 m²K/W. Single pane: 4 mm glass (\(k = 1.0\)) adds \(0.004/1.0 = 0.004\), so \(R'' = 0.17 + 0.004 = 0.174\) m²K/W and \(q = 20/0.174 \approx 115\) W (\(U \approx 5.7\) W/m²K) — Lesson 3's answer. Double pane: two 4 mm panes (\(2 \times 0.004 = 0.008\)) + a 12 mm still-air gap (\(0.012/0.026 \approx 0.46\)) + the same films: \(R'' \approx 0.17 + 0.008 + 0.46 \approx 0.64\) m²K/W, so \(q \approx 20/0.64 \approx 31\) W (\(U \approx 1.6\)). A ~4× improvement, and the glass contributes barely 1% of it — the upgrade you pay for is a 12 mm sliver of trapped air.

  • \(R_{\text{tot}} = 1/(h_{\text{in}}A) + \sum L_i/(k_i A) + 1/(h_{\text{out}}A)\): layers AND surface films, all in series, one \(q\) through everything.
  • \(U = 1/R''_{\text{tot}}\) (W/m²K) rates whole assemblies; US package R-value is \(5.678 \times\) the SI R-value (R-13 ≈ RSI 2.3).
  • Interface temperatures come from \(\Delta T_i = qR_i\) — the staircase is the network, drawn.
  • The double-pane window wins ~4× over single glass, and essentially all of the win is one thin layer of still air.
  1. Load Single pane, then Double pane. Confirm the worked example live: q ≈ 115 W drops to ≈ 31 W, U from ≈5.7 to ≈1.6. Which resistor in the chain grew?
  2. On Double pane, slide the middle layer's thickness from 12 mm up to 50 mm. q keeps (slowly) falling here — but the verdict warns you: in a real window Tab 2's convection loop would eat the gain past ~20 mm.
  3. Build the Insulated stud wall and read its US R-value in the stats. Where does the brick–insulation interface temperature sit on a −5 °C day (ΔT = 25)?
  4. Load Steel box. The metal contributes ~nothing; the films do all the insulating — note that q is still lower than you might fear. Now swap one steel layer to aerogel and watch the network transform.
