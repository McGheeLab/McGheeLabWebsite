TAB 1Missing Neighbors

Goal: discover why an atom at the surface feels a net inward pull while an atom in the bulk feels nothing.

Predict

Do

  2. Drag the Probe depth slider (range 0–6) down one layer at a time. At each depth in the table, read the stats panel: Neighbors present, Neighbors missing, and Net inward pull (% of surface).

TAB 2Surface Energy

Goal: connect bond strength to surface energy γ, using γ ≈ Nε / 2A — strong bonds make a hungry, high-γ surface.

Predict

Which fresh surface stores the most energy (highest γ)?  ☐ waxy polymer   ☐ glass / oxide   ☐ metal

Do

  1. Drag Cleave progress (0–100%) slowly from 0 to 100% and watch the bonds crossing the plane stretch, snap, and leave dangling stubs on both new surfaces.
  2. Click each scenario in turn — Waxy polymer (weak bonds), Glass / oxide, Metal (strong bonds) — and read the Estimated γ stat (in mJ/m²) for each.
  3. On the plot, compare each γ against the dashed reference lines: PTFE ~20, water 72.8, glass ~300, copper ~1800 mJ/m².
  4. Pick one scenario and double the Bond energy ε slider (0.1–5 eV). Does γ double too? Then nudge Surface packing and watch γ move.

TAB 3Water Coats Everything

Goal: show that in ordinary air every surface is buried under a water film, and find where the film first reaches one full monolayer.

Predict

A hungry surface (BET c = 50) first reaches one full monolayer of water (θ = 1) at about…  ☐ 5% RH   ☐ 20% RH   ☐ 60% RH   ☐ 95% RH

Do

  1. Watch the live animation at Room air (45% RH) — molecules land, sit, and leave. Note it is two-way traffic, not a one-way pile-up.

  3. Find the RH where θ first crosses 1 monolayer and write it below.
  4. Lower Surface hunger (BET c) toward Teflon-like values and note how much more humid the air must be before even one monolayer forms.

TAB 4Level Up: Vacuum & Kinetics

Goal: see how pumping down empties the chamber (long mean free path) and slows recontamination — but the water film desorbs slowly, so bake-out helps.

Predict

When you drop the pressure from ambient to high vacuum, the adsorbed water film…  ☐ vanishes instantly   ☐ desorbs slowly over seconds   ☐ stays forever

Do

  1. Click Ambient (760 Torr). Read Mean free path λ, Monolayer time, and Water film left from the stats.
  2. Click Rough vacuum (10⁻² Torr), then High vacuum (10⁻⁶ Torr), recording the same three stats each time. Watch the film desorb layer by layer — it does NOT vanish the instant pressure drops.
  3. From High vacuum, click Bake-out (10⁻⁶ Torr, 420 K) (or raise the Temperature slider, 250–500 K). Note how much faster the last layer leaves.
  4. The Pressure slider is logarithmic (1e-8 to 760 Torr) — sweep it and watch λ and monolayer time on the log–log plot.
