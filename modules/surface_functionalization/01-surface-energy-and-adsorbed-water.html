Surfaces Are Hungry — Surface Energy & Adsorbed Water

Surface Functionalization Foundations · Lesson 1 of 4 — Every surface you have ever touched is a layer of atoms that got cheated out of half their neighbors. That makes surfaces tense, energetic, and hungry — and it's why they're never actually clean.

Background: The atoms at the surface got a bad deal

Picture an atom deep inside a solid. It is surrounded on every side — above, below, left, right — by neighbors, and every neighbor bond pulls on it. Because the pulls come from all directions equally, they cancel out. A bulk atom feels no net force at all. It doesn't even know which way "up" is.

Now slide that atom up to the surface. Suddenly all the neighbors that should have been above it are gone — there's just air (or vacuum) up there. The pulls from below and from the sides are still there, but nothing balances them from above. The result is a net inward pull: every surface atom is being tugged down into the solid by the neighbors it still has.

$$ \vec F_{\text{net}} \;=\; \sum_{\text{neighbors}} \vec f_{\text{bond}} \;=\; \begin{cases} \vec 0 & \text{bulk atom (pulls cancel)}\\[2pt] \text{inward} & \text{surface atom (pulls from above are missing)} \end{cases} $$

This unbalanced tug is the seed of everything in this course. It is why liquid drops pull themselves into spheres (surface tension), why surfaces store extra energy, and why they grab onto almost any molecule that lands on them. A surface atom is unhappy — it is missing bonds it would love to make. Chemists say the surface has dangling bonds and the surface is high-energy or, more vividly, hungry.

One subtlety worth noticing in the sim: the imbalance is shallow. Go just two or three atomic layers below the surface and the pulls balance out again. All of the drama of surface science happens in a skin about a nanometer thick.

In a simple cubic packing, a bulk atom has 6 nearest neighbors (4 in-plane, 1 up, 1 down). An atom at the surface has only 5 — it lost the one above. Count the pulls: the 4 in-plane pulls cancel each other, and the pull from below (1 bond) has nothing to cancel it. So the surface atom feels a net inward force equal to one full bond's worth of pull — with a metallic bond force scale of ~1 nN, that's a huge force for one atom. One layer down, the atom has all 6 neighbors back and the net force drops to zero (longer-range attractions leave only a few-percent residual).

  • Bulk atoms are pulled equally from all sides — net force zero.
  • Surface atoms are missing their upper neighbors — net inward pull.
  • The imbalance dies off within ~2–3 atomic layers; surfaces are a nanometer-thick skin.
  • Unbalanced bonds = stored energy = a surface that wants to grab new partners.
  1. With the probe at the surface (depth 0), look at the thin green arrows — those are the pulls from each neighbor. Where are they missing? Which way does the thick red resultant arrow point?

Background: Making surface costs energy — and the surface remembers

Here's a thought experiment you can actually do with a piece of chalk: snap it in half. You just created two brand-new surfaces that didn't exist a second ago. Where did they come from? You cut bonds. Every atomic bond that used to cross your break plane is now dangling, and cutting each one cost energy — energy that came from your hands and is now stored in those two unhappy, bond-starved surfaces.

That stored energy has a name: surface energy, written \(\gamma\) (gamma). It is the energy cost per square meter of new surface. Count the bonds crossing a plane, multiply by the energy per bond, and divide by two (because one cut makes two surfaces):

$$ \gamma \;\approx\; \frac{N_{\text{bonds}}\; \varepsilon_{\text{bond}}}{2A} \qquad \left[\gamma\right] = \frac{\text{J}}{\text{m}^2} \;\;(\text{usually quoted in mJ/m}^2) $$

Level up — real numbers. Surface energies span a huge range, and that range is the whole story of this course. Strong bonds → hungry surfaces; weak bonds → content ones:

· Metals (metallic bonds): \(\gamma \approx\) 1000–2000 mJ/m² — ravenous.
· Glass & oxides (ionic/covalent): \(\gamma \approx\) 200–500 mJ/m² — hungry.
· Water (hydrogen bonds): \(\gamma = 72.8\) mJ/m² — the surface tension of water.
· Polymers & waxes (van der Waals): \(\gamma \approx\) 20–40 mJ/m² — content.
· PTFE / Teflon: \(\gamma \approx\) 18–20 mJ/m² — nearly nothing sticks.

Note that surface tension and surface energy are the same quantity in different clothes: J/m² is the same unit as N/m. A liquid minimizes its area (drops go round) because area costs energy. A solid can't flow into a ball, so it does the next best thing: it grabs whatever lands on it to satisfy its dangling bonds. High-\(\gamma\) surfaces are the grabby ones — hold that thought for Tab 3.

Estimate \(\gamma\) for copper. A copper surface has about \(n_s = 1.5\times10^{19}\) atoms per m², each metallic bond is worth roughly \(\varepsilon \approx 3.5\ \text{eV} = 5.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\), and cleaving breaks about one bond per surface atom while making two surfaces: $$\gamma \approx \frac{1.5\times10^{19} \times 5.6\times10^{-19}}{2} \approx 4.2\ \text{J/m}^2 = 4200\ \text{mJ/m}^2.$$ The measured value is ~1800 mJ/m² — our estimate is high because cleaving doesn't fully break the bonds (the leftover electrons rearrange and recover some energy). But it lands within a factor of ~2 and nails the trend: strong bonds → big \(\gamma\).

  • Creating surface = cutting bonds = spending energy. \(\gamma\) is that cost per m².
  • \(\gamma \approx N\varepsilon/2A\): stronger or denser bonds → higher surface energy.
  • Metals ~1000+, glass ~300, water 72.8, Teflon ~20 mJ/m². Memorize water's number — it will run the whole course.
  • High-\(\gamma\) surfaces are "hungry": they lower their energy by grabbing adsorbates.
  1. Drag Cleave slowly from 0 to 100% and watch the bonds crossing the plane stretch, snap, and leave dangling stubs on both new surfaces.
  2. Read the \(\gamma\) value in the stats. Now double the Bond energy — does \(\gamma\) double too? Check the plot.

  4. Chalk question: when you snap chalk, the energy in your hands ends up stored in the two new surfaces. Which would take more energy to snap (same size bar): chalk or copper? Why?

Background: The air is full of water, and your surface knows it

Here is the fact that surprises everyone: in ordinary air, every surface around you is already coated in water. Not visibly wet — a film a few molecules thick. The air always carries water vapor (that's what "humidity" is), water molecules constantly slam into every exposed surface, and a hungry, high-energy surface doesn't let them bounce away. It grabs them. This grabbing is called adsorption (with a "d" — molecules sticking onto a surface, not soaking into it).

It doesn't stop at one layer. The first layer of water bonds to the surface itself; then more water molecules land on the water and hydrogen-bond to the first layer; then a third layer sticks to the second. Water stacks on water. The film gets thicker as the air gets more humid — from well under one layer in desert-dry air to many layers in a steamy room.

Level up — the BET isotherm. In 1938 Brunauer, Emmett and Teller wrote down the classic model for multilayer adsorption. The coverage \(\theta\) (in monolayers) as a function of relative humidity \(x = \text{RH}\) is:

$$ \theta(x) \;=\; \frac{c\,x}{(1-x)\bigl(1 + (c-1)\,x\bigr)} \qquad c \approx e^{(E_1 - E_L)/RT} $$

The constant \(c\) measures how much more strongly the first layer binds to the solid (\(E_1\)) than later layers bind to each other (\(E_L\), the liquefaction energy). A hungry surface has a big \(c\): the first monolayer forms fast, at low humidity — that's the steep initial rise and the "knee" of the curve. Then multilayers pile up, gently at mid humidity and steeply as RH → 100% (the curve blows up as \(x \to 1\): that's condensation — actual dew). This S-shape is called a Type II isotherm, and it is what real water on real glass or metal looks like.

The punchline for functionalization: whatever chemistry you want to do to your surface, you won't be doing it to the surface at all — you'll be doing it to a film of water, unless you get rid of it first. Which is Tab 4's job.

Room air, RH = 45%, a hungry surface with \(c = 50\): $$\theta = \frac{50 \times 0.45}{(1-0.45)\,(1 + 49\times0.45)} = \frac{22.5}{0.55 \times 23.05} \approx 1.8\ \text{monolayers}.$$ A water monolayer is ~0.3 nm thick, so the film is ~0.5 nm — invisible, but it's there, completely burying the atoms you might want to react with. At RH 90% the same formula gives \(\theta \approx 10\) layers (~3 nm), and lab experiments on glass agree: a few nanometers of water in humid air.

  • Ambient air always contains water vapor; hungry surfaces adsorb it on contact.
  • Water stacks in multilayers — a film, not just one molecular layer.
  • BET: steep first-monolayer "knee," then multilayer growth that diverges as RH → 100%.
  • Adsorption is a dynamic equilibrium — molecules land and leave constantly; humidity sets the balance.
  • Any chemistry you attempt in air happens on the water film, not the bare surface.

Level up: Vacuum — how to actually get a bare surface (and why it takes patience)

Tab 3 left us with a problem: our surface is buried under water. The fix sounds simple — put it in a chamber and pump the air out. With no water vapor arriving, the adsorbed film's two-way traffic becomes one-way: molecules keep leaving (desorbing) but nothing replaces them, and the surface gradually bares itself. Gradually! Desorption is not instant — each molecule needs enough thermal energy to break free, so the film evaporates layer by layer. That's why real vacuum systems get baked out (gently heated) to hurry the stragglers along: the first layers leave in seconds, the last tightly-bound monolayer can take hours at room temperature.

Kinetic theory, part 1 — how empty is "vacuum"? A gas molecule travels a distance called the mean free path \(\lambda\) between collisions. It's set by how many other molecules are in the way, i.e. by pressure:

$$ \lambda \;=\; \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi d^2 P} $$

where \(d \approx 0.37\) nm is a molecule's diameter. At atmospheric pressure \(\lambda \approx 68\) nanometers — a molecule can't move a thousandth of a hair's width without crashing into another. At \(10^{-6}\) Torr, \(\lambda \approx 50\) meters: molecules fly clear across the chamber without meeting anyone. Same formula, nine orders of magnitude apart.

Kinetic theory, part 2 — how fast does a surface get dirty again? The Hertz–Knudsen equation counts the molecules striking each square meter per second:

$$ \Phi \;=\; \frac{P}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_B T}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad t_{\text{monolayer}} \;\approx\; \frac{n_s}{\Phi} $$

with \(n_s \approx 10^{19}\) sites/m². If every arriving molecule sticks, \(t_{\text{monolayer}}\) is how long a freshly cleaned surface stays clean. Vacuum scientists honor this with a unit: one Langmuir = \(10^{-6}\) Torr·s ≈ one monolayer's worth of exposure.

Nitrogen at \(T = 300\) K, \(m = 4.65\times10^{-26}\) kg, \(\sqrt{2\pi m k_B T} = 3.5\times10^{-23}\) (SI units).
Ambient (\(P = 101{,}325\) Pa): \(\Phi = 2.9\times10^{27}\) hits/m²·s → \(t \approx 10^{19}/2.9\times10^{27} \approx 3.5\) nanoseconds per monolayer. A surface in air can never, ever be clean.
High vacuum (\(10^{-6}\) Torr \(= 1.33\times10^{-4}\) Pa): \(\Phi = 3.8\times10^{18}\) → \(t \approx\) 2.6 seconds per monolayer. Now you have a working window — and at \(10^{-9}\) Torr it stretches to ~45 minutes, which is why true surface-science experiments live in ultra-high vacuum.

  • Vacuum removes the water film by cutting off the arrivals; departures then win — but desorption takes time and is helped by heat (bake-out).
  • Mean free path: 68 nm in air → 50 m at 10⁻⁶ Torr. "Vacuum" means molecules stop meeting each other.
  • Hertz–Knudsen: a clean surface recontaminates in ~ns at ambient, ~seconds at 10⁻⁶ Torr. Cleanliness is a race against pressure.
  • A pumped-down, baked, bare surface is the blank canvas every functionalization in Lessons 2–3 starts from.
  1. Start at ambient and drag Pressure down slowly. Watch the gas thin out — and notice the water film does NOT vanish the moment the pressure drops. It desorbs layer by layer over a few seconds. (That lag is real physics, not lag in the sim.)
  2. At 10⁻⁶ Torr, raise the Temperature to 400 K and watch the last layer leave faster. You just did a bake-out.
