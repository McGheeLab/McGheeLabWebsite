We do not own one. This tab designs the coil; Tab 2 computes the force it puts on an iron bead; Tab 3 says what that bead does inside a 1–20 kPa gel; Tab 4 runs the whole chain backwards from the displacement you want to the coil you should wind. Every panel ends in a number you can take to the shop, and every number states where its model stops being honest.

1.1 · What a field costs

Start with the naive design: a spool of magnet wire, a bench supply, nothing in the middle. The field on the axis of a finite solenoid of \(N\) turns, length \(L\), radius \(R\), carrying current \(I\) is

E1.1.1 $$B(z) = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2L}\left[\frac{z + L/2}{\sqrt{(z+L/2)^2 + R^2}} - \frac{z - L/2}{\sqrt{(z-L/2)^2 + R^2}}\right]$$

and everything about it is set by one product, the ampere-turns \(NI\). At the centre of a long coil it tends to \(\mu_0 NI/L\); a 40 mm coil at 1,700 A·turns gives about 50 mT. That is a refrigerator magnet. Before deciding how to get more, be precise about what \(NI\) costs — because the answer is not what most people expect.

Wind a bobbin of inner radius \(r_i\), outer radius \(r_o\), length \(L\) with wire of bare cross-section \(A_w\). A fill factor \(f \approx 0.65\) of the window \((r_o - r_i)L\) is copper; the rest is enamel and air:

E1.1.2 $$N = \frac{f\,(r_o - r_i)\,L}{A_w}, \qquad L_{\rm wire} = 2\pi\bar r\,N, \qquad R = \frac{\rho\,L_{\rm wire}}{A_w}$$

Now drive it with power \(P = I^2 R\). Substitute \(I = \sqrt{P/R}\) and multiply by \(N\):

E1.1.3 $$NI = \sqrt{\frac{P\,f\,(r_o - r_i)\,L}{\rho\,2\pi\bar r}}$$
The wire gauge has vanished. \(A_w\) cancels. For a given bobbin and a given power, thin wire and thick wire deliver identical ampere-turns — thin wire as many turns at low current and high voltage, thick wire as few turns at high current and low voltage. Gauge chooses which bench supply you need. It does not choose the field. What buys field is power and winding volume, and Tab 1's whole argument is about how much power the copper will tolerate for how long.

1.2 · The iron core, and its ceiling

Iron multiplies the field — but not by the 5,000 in the handbook. A rod of finite length demagnetizes itself: its own poles push back with a field \(N_d M\), where \(N_d\) is the demagnetizing factor set purely by the aspect ratio \(L/D\). The magnetization the rod actually reaches under an applied \(H\), and the field it produces, are

E1.1.4 $$M = \min\!\left(\frac{H}{N_d + 1/\mu_r},\; M_s\right), \qquad B = \mu_0\,(H + M), \qquad N_d = \frac{1}{m^2-1}\left[\frac{m}{\sqrt{m^2-1}}\ln\!\left(m + \sqrt{m^2-1}\right) - 1\right],\ m = \tfrac{L}{D}$$

Two consequences run the design. First, the effective permeability is \(1/N_d\), not \(\mu_r\): an \(L/D = 10\) rod gives about 50, a stubby \(L/D = 2\) slug about 6. Aspect ratio beats turns. Second — and this is the ceiling — iron saturates at \(M_s = 1.71\times10^6\) A/m, i.e. \(B_{sat} = \mu_0 M_s = 2.15\) T. Past the ampere-turns that reach it, more current adds only the \(\mu_0 H\) of the empty coil: millitesla per thousand ampere-turns. The designer below draws that knee. Everything to its right is wasted heat.

  1. Change the gauge from AWG 22 to AWG 30 at fixed power. Watch N·I not move while turns, current and voltage all change by an order of magnitude. That is E1.1.3 refusing to care.
  2. Set the core to air and read B. Now set L/D = 10. Then L/D = 2. The knee moves — a stubby core needs almost ten times the ampere-turns to saturate.
  3. Push the power past the knee. B stops at 2.15 T. Find the power where you just saturate: that is the coil worth building, and the design map below tells you how long you can hold it there.

A long thin rod saturates at ~1,400 A·turns — well inside what 100 W buys. A short fat slug needs ~12,000, which is a kilowatt. The cheapest field you will ever buy is a longer core.

1.3 · The shot: current, heat, and how long you have

E1.1.3 says field costs power, and power in a coil is heat in copper. Held on, the coil settles where \(I^2R\) balances what its surface can shed — a few watts for a bobbin this size in still air, which is a few thousand ampere-turns at most. But a pulse does not need to shed anything: for a fraction of a second the copper simply absorbs the heat. With current density \(J = I/A_w\), and letting the resistivity climb with temperature as \(\rho = \rho_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)\), the wire's temperature rise obeys

E1.1.5 $$\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{J^{2}\rho(T)}{d\,c_p} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int_0^{t} J^{2}\,dt = \frac{d\,c_p}{\rho_0\,\alpha}\,\ln\!\big(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T\big)$$

The right-hand side is the action integral — a property of copper and of the temperature you are willing to reach, nothing else. Reach 180 °C (the enamel's class rating) and it is \(2.5\times10^{16}\) A²s/m⁴; reach 1085 °C (the copper melts) and it is \(8.6\times10^{16}\). The insulation gives out three and a half times sooner than the metal. A coil does not die by melting; it dies when two adjacent turns touch, and it does that at a temperature the metal barely notices.

One more clock. An iron-cored coil is an inductor — a large one:

E1.1.6 $$L_{\rm ind} = \frac{\mu_0\,\mu_{\rm eff}\,N^{2}A_{\rm core}}{L}, \qquad \tau_{LR} = \frac{L_{\rm ind}}{R}, \qquad I(t) = I_\infty\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_{LR}}\right)$$

With \(\mu_{\rm eff} \approx 50\) from the demagnetization above, \(\tau_{LR}\) is tens of milliseconds. A 10 ms shot never reaches its current, let alone its field. Iron makes the coil strong and slow in the same stroke.

One honest calibration before the panel. For a bobbin with a 400 mm² window driven from a 30 V / 5 A bench supply, the copper never gets warm inside a one-second shot — \(J\) is a few A/mm² and the enamel clock reads minutes. The thermal limit bites in two places only: steady drive, where 100 W into a coil that can shed 13 W runs away past the rating; and small coils, where the same ampere-turns mean far higher \(J\). For this rig at this size, the limit on the field is your supply, and the design map on the previous panel is what tells you whether that matters.

  1. Play a single shot at the defaults — a kilowatt for one second. Watch three things happen in order: the current climbs through τ, the temperature climbs, and the current then sags — the copper's resistance is rising under it. Read the wire temperature at the end: warm, not hot.
  2. Shorten the shot to 10 ms. The current never plateaus; B never arrives. Now push the power to 10 kW and lengthen the shot until the enamel line is crossed. Between those two failures is the window you can actually use — and notice how far above any bench supply you had to go to find the copper's limit for a bobbin this size.
  3. Switch to steady drive. At 100 W the temperature runs away past the rating. Cut the power until it settles below it: that is the field you can leave on all afternoon — and it is small.

1.4 · The other geometry: a Helmholtz pair, wired the Maxwell way

Everything so far pushed field out of one iron pole. There is a second classical answer, and the lab should know why it is not the one we build. Two identical thin coils of radius \(R\), coaxial, a distance \(d\) apart, each carrying \(NI\) ampere-turns, produce on their common axis

E1.1.7 $$B(z) = \frac{\mu_0 N I R^{2}}{2\left[R^{2} + (z - d/2)^{2}\right]^{3/2}} \;\pm\; \frac{\mu_0 N I R^{2}}{2\left[R^{2} + (z + d/2)^{2}\right]^{3/2}}$$

With the plus sign (currents aiding) and \(d = R\), the second derivative of \(B\) vanishes at the centre: this is the Helmholtz pair, the standard way to make a uniform field. With the minus sign (currents opposing) and \(d = \sqrt{3}\,R\), the third derivative vanishes instead: the field is zero at the centre and its gradient is uniform — the Maxwell pair, the standard way to make a uniform gradient (it is how MRI gradient coils began). At the centre,

E1.1.8 $$B_{\rm Helmholtz} = \left(\tfrac{4}{5}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\mu_0 N I}{R} = 0.716\,\frac{\mu_0 N I}{R}, \qquad \left.\frac{dB}{dz}\right|_{\rm Maxwell} = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2}\left(\tfrac{4}{7}\right)^{5/2}\frac{\mu_0 N I}{R^{2}} = 0.641\,\frac{\mu_0 N I}{R^{2}}$$

Now remember what a bead needs (Tab 2 will derive it): a force needs both a moment and a gradient, \(F = V\,M(B)\,\nabla B\). The Helmholtz pair supplies \(B\) and no gradient: it magnetizes and does not pull. The Maxwell pair supplies a gradient and, at its centre, no field: a soft bead there has no moment and feels nothing. The textbook magnetic tweezer therefore nests both — a Helmholtz bias to set \(M\), a Maxwell gradient to pull — and buys something the iron tip can never offer: the same force on every bead in the sample, computable from the current alone. Run the three wirings below, then read the comparison chart. The price of that uniformity is about two orders of magnitude of force.

  1. Start on Maxwell. Read the gradient: tens of T/m, flat to a fraction of a percent across the whole sample. Now read the force on the bead at the centre. Zero, to the precision shown — there is no field to magnetize it. A perfect gradient pulling on nothing.
  2. Switch to Helmholtz. A clean uniform field, no gradient, no force. Drag \(d/R\) off 1 and watch the flatness go.
  3. Switch to bias + gradient. Now every bead in the ±5 mm window feels the same force — read it, then read the ratio to the iron tip. That ratio is the decision this notebook makes: Tabs 2–4 use the tip.
  4. Raise the ampere-turns to their maximum. The pair scales linearly (no iron, no ceiling), but the thermal clock from §1.3 applies to both coils at once — it never closes the gap.

2.1 · The field, drawn

On the axis of a cylinder of radius \(R_t\) and length \(L_t\), magnetized to \(M\), the field a distance \(z\) outside the flat pole face is

E1.2.1 $$B(z) = \frac{\mu_0 M}{2}\left[\frac{z + L_t}{\sqrt{(z+L_t)^2 + R_t^2}} - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R_t^2}}\right]$$

At the face of a long rod this is \(\mu_0 M_s/2 = 1.07\) T — exactly half the 2.15 T inside, because half the flux has already leaked out the sides before it reaches the end. Differentiate once and evaluate close to the face, \(z \ll R_t\):

E1.2.2 $$\left|\frac{dB}{dz}\right|_{z \ll R_t} \approx \frac{\mu_0 M_s}{2\,R_t}$$

A 0.5 mm tip gives about 2,000 T/m. A 0.1 mm tip gives 10,000 — and loses it all within a quarter of a millimetre. Sharper tip, more gradient, less reach. Working distance is the design decision of this tab, so the lower chart puts several electromagnets against the same bead at once: a sharp tip, a 0.5 mm tip, a blunt 2 mm tip, Tab 1's rod exactly as wound with no taper at all, an under-driven core, the §1.4 coil pair, and — as a dashed envelope — the best tip you could turn for each distance. The chips jump the sliders to each one; the bold line is whatever the sliders say now.

  1. Read the colour map: the field is 1 T at the face and falls a thousandfold within a few tip radii. Trace a white field line with your eye from the face round to the side of the rod — that is where the flux goes.
  2. Step through the electromagnet chips and watch the force chart. The sharp tip wins inside 0.2 mm and is the worst of all beyond 1 mm; the untapered rod is a poor tweezer everywhere — the taper is the tweezer. The dashed red envelope is the ceiling: no tip beats it at any distance.
  3. Shrink the tip radius to 0.1 mm. The gradient at the bead jumps; the force jumps with it. Now move the bead out to 2 mm and watch the force collapse to nothing. Put the tip back to 2 mm radius: weaker up close, far stronger out there.
  4. Switch the bead to magnetite, then to a polymer bead. The saturation boundary on the force chart slides toward the tip. A polymer bead is saturated almost everywhere — its force is set by ∇B alone.

2.2 · What a bead becomes in that field

A soft-iron sphere in a field \(B\) magnetizes until its own demagnetizing field (\(N_d = 1/3\) for a sphere) stops it: \(M = 3B/\mu_0\) for \(\mu_r \to \infty\). It cannot exceed the material's \(M_s\), so

E1.2.3 $$M(B) = \min\!\left(\frac{3B}{\mu_0},\; M_s\right), \qquad \text{saturating at } B = \frac{\mu_0 M_s}{3}$$

Iron saturates at 0.72 T, magnetite at 0.20 T, a 20 % polymer bead at 0.04 T. Read those against the field map: a 0.5 mm tip puts 0.32 T on a bead 0.5 mm away — an iron bead there is not saturated and a magnetite bead is. The crossover sits inside the working range, so the force law has to be computed with it, not assumed either way. The force on an induced dipole is the gradient of its energy:

E1.2.4 $$F = V\,M(B)\,\nabla B \;=\; \begin{cases} \dfrac{3V}{\mu_0}\,B\,\nabla B & \text{linear, } B < \mu_0 M_s/3 \\[6pt] V M_s\,\nabla B & \text{saturated}\end{cases} \qquad V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi a^{3}$$

In both regimes the force is proportional to the bead's volume. A 5 µm bead feels 125 times the force of a 1 µm bead; a 20 µm bead, 8,000 times. That is the whole story of bead size on this tab: more iron, more pull, as \(a^3\). Tab 3 will show why it is not the whole story once the bead is in a gel.

37 nN on a 5 µm iron bead half a millimetre from a half-millimetre tip; 0.3 nN on a 1 µm bead in the same spot. Had the bead been magnetite (saturates at 0.20 T) it would be saturated here and the force would be \(V M_s \nabla B\) = 24 nN — less, despite the simpler formula, because magnetite carries a third of iron's moment. Regime first, then arithmetic.
Honesty line. E1.2.4 treats the bead as a point dipole. That needs \(a \ll R_t\) and \(a \ll z\): a bead that is not small compared with the tip sits in a field that varies across it and magnetizes the pole back. The force chart draws those beads dashed and grey; the readout flags them. For the 500 µm end of the lab's bead range and a sub-millimetre tip, the honest answer is "use a bigger tip or a finite-element model" — not the number.
  • The pole face of a saturated rod carries µ₀M_s/2 = 1.07 T whatever the coil did (E1.2.1); the gradient there is ~µ₀M_s/2R_t (E1.2.2) — the tip radius, not the power, sets the pull.
  • Three regimes with distance: gradient ~B/R_t at the face; B ∝ 1/z², ∇B ∝ 1/z³ for R_t ≪ z ≪ L_t (the face is a magnetic monopole); dipole 1/z⁴ beyond L_t.
  • A bead saturates at B = µ₀M_s/3 (E1.2.3): 0.72 T iron, 0.20 T magnetite. The crossover is inside the working range — compute both regimes.
  • F = V·M(B)·∇B (E1.2.4) is ∝ a³ either way: 5 µm iron at 0.5 mm from a 0.5 mm tip feels ~37 nN.
Next: 37 nN on a bead in water would fling it at the tip. In a gel it moves a distance — and you would guess a bigger bead, with more area to push against, moves less per unit force. Tab 3 asks you to predict how displacement scales with bead size, then measures it.
Predict before you look. Tab 2 left you with a force that grows as the bead's volume, \(a^3\). Now the bead is in a gel, and the gel pushes back. 3.2 · The reveal — the gel resists like a radius, not an area

The slope is 2. Displacement grows as \(a^2\), not \(a\). The projected-area guess undercounted by a full power of the radius, and the reason is worth owning because it recurs everywhere in soft-matter mechanics. A rigid sphere of radius \(a\) pulled through an elastic medium of shear modulus \(G\) and Poisson ratio \(\nu\) by a force \(F\) displaces by

E1.3.1 $$u = \frac{F\,(5 - 6\nu)}{24\pi\,G\,a\,(1-\nu)} \;\xrightarrow{\;\nu = 1/2\;}\; u = \frac{F}{6\pi G a}, \qquad G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} = \frac{E}{3}$$

This is Stokes' law with \(G\) in place of \(\eta v\) — the elastic Stokes law — and its denominator carries a single power of \(a\). Why not area? Because the medium's resistance is not a contact pressure on the bead's face; it is strain in a shell of gel that extends about one radius out. The strain is \(u/a\), the stress is \(G\,u/a\), and it acts over an area \(\sim a^2\): force \(\sim G\,u\,a\). The area and the strain trade one power of \(a\) between them. Combine with Tab 2's force and the saturated closed form is

E1.3.2 $$u = \frac{V M_s \nabla B}{6\pi G a} = \frac{2\,a^{2} M_s\,\nabla B}{9\,G}$$
Bigger beads are the cheap upgrade. Double the bead and you get eight times the force against twice the resistance — four times the displacement, for free. For a tracking experiment fighting a 20 nm noise floor, bead diameter is worth more than any coil change on Tab 1.
The slope, by hand — and what area would have said
  1. Write what you know. Saturated bead, so E1.3.2 applies. Compare \(a_1 = 2.5\) µm and \(a_2 = 25\) µm at the same \(\nabla B\) and \(G\).
    \(\dfrac{u_2}{u_1} = \left(\dfrac{a_2}{a_1}\right)^{2} = 10^{2} = 100 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{slope} = \dfrac{\log 100}{\log 10} = 2.00\)
  2. What the area guess predicts. Force \(\propto a^3\), resistance \(\propto a^2\):
    \(\dfrac{u_2}{u_1} = \dfrac{a_2}{a_1} = 10 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{slope} = 1\)
  3. Put a real number on it. 5 µm iron bead, \(E = 5\) kPa (\(G = 1667\) Pa), saturated at \(\nabla B = 2000\) T/m:
    \(u = \dfrac{2\times(2.5\times10^{-6})^{2}\times1.71\times10^{6}\times2000}{9\times1667} \approx 2.8\ \mu\mathrm{m}\)
The deposited points read ≈1.96; the area guess said 1. (The missing 0.04 is E1.3.3 — the depth correction shaves the largest beads; deepen the gel and the slope walks to 2.00.) A 50 µm bead moves a hundred times further than a 5 µm one, not ten times. (And 2.8 µm on a 2.5 µm radius is u/a > 1 — the model has already broken for that soft a gel; see the honesty line.)

3.3 · A floor underneath, a clock inside, and where the model stops

Two corrections matter at the lab's scales. A bead a depth \(h\) above the coverslip feels the glass through the gel — a Faxén-type stiffening,

E1.3.3 $$u \;\to\; \frac{u}{1 + 9a/8h}$$

a 23 % effect for a 10 µm bead 50 µm up. And a hydrogel is viscoelastic: pulled suddenly it creeps toward its resting displacement with a time constant \(\tau_{ve} = \eta/G\), then recovers when the field drops —

E1.3.4 $$u(t) = u_\infty\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_{ve}}\right), \qquad \tau_{ve} = \frac{\eta}{G}$$

For a 5 kPa gel with an effective viscosity of 100 Pa·s that is 60 ms; for 1 kPa it is 300 ms. Set that beside Tab 1's shot clock — 280 ms to the enamel limit at 300 A/mm² — and the design problem announces itself: the pulse has to outlast the gel's clock and end before the copper's. For soft gels those two windows can fail to overlap. Tab 4 checks.

Outlasting the gel
  1. Write what you know. \(\eta = 100\) Pa·s. Gel A: \(E = 5\) kPa, \(G = 1667\) Pa. Gel B: \(E = 1\) kPa, \(G = 333\) Pa. The shot clock from Tab 1 at \(J = 300\) A/mm²: 0.28 s to the enamel rating.
    \(\tau_{ve} = \eta/G, \qquad \text{need } t_{\rm pulse} \gtrsim 3\tau_{ve} \text{ to reach } 95\% \text{ of } u_\infty\)
  2. Gel A.
    \(\tau_{ve} = 100/1667 = 0.06\ \mathrm{s}, \qquad 3\tau_{ve} = 0.18\ \mathrm{s} < 0.28\ \mathrm{s}\ \checkmark\)
  3. Gel B.
    \(\tau_{ve} = 100/333 = 0.30\ \mathrm{s}, \qquad 3\tau_{ve} = 0.90\ \mathrm{s} > 0.28\ \mathrm{s}\ \times\)
  4. What fixes B. The enamel time goes as \(1/J^2\): halve \(J\) (thicker wire at the same ampere-turns, E1.1.3) and the clock quadruples to 1.1 s — enough.
    \(t_{\rm enamel} \propto J^{-2}: \quad 0.28\ \mathrm{s} \times 4 = 1.1\ \mathrm{s} > 0.90\ \mathrm{s}\ \checkmark\)
A 5 kPa gel is comfortably inside the shot window; a 1 kPa gel is not, and the fix is thicker wire, not more power. This is why Tab 4 carries both clocks in its ledger.
Honesty line. E1.3.1 assumes a linear, isotropic, incompressible, infinite medium with small strain (\(u/a \lesssim 0.1\); tolerable to ~0.3). A granular hydrogel is none of those at the granule scale — this is the continuum limit, honest when the bead is much larger than a granule and much smaller than its displacement is not. The verdict flags \(u/a\) in three bands; treat red as "the number is wrong," not "the number is approximate." And the 20–50 nm tracking floor is a microscope limit, not a physics one.
  • Elastic Stokes (E1.3.1): u = F/(6πGa) at ν = ½. The gel resists like a radius — strain u/a over area a² — not like an area.
  • Force ∝ a³ over resistance ∝ a gives u ∝ a² (E1.3.2): doubling the bead quadruples the displacement. Bead size is the cheapest gain in the whole rig.
  • Creep clock τve = η/G (E1.3.4) is ~0.06–0.3 s for 1–5 kPa gels — the same order as the enamel shot clock. The pulse must fit between them.
  • Trust the linear model only for u/a ≲ 0.1–0.3. Stiff gels and small beads are where it is honest.

4.3 · How to make it, and how to know it works

In the order the shop would do it. Each step names the number from this notebook that it has to hit.

  1. Turn the core. Soft iron or 1018 low-carbon steel rod at the diameter and length on the sheet (the L/D you chose on Tab 1 is the demagnetizing factor — E1.1.4 — do not shorten it to save stock). Turn one end down to the tip diameter over the 30° taper; face and polish the tip flat. If the shop can anneal it afterwards (~800 °C, slow cool), do: machining work-hardens the surface and raises coercivity, which leaves a remanent field after each shot.
  2. Make the bobbin. ID a sliding fit on the core, OD and flange spacing from the sheet. The bobbin sits inside the hottest copper, so its material must outlast the enamel class (§1.3): PEEK, glass-filled nylon or Delrin — not PLA, which softens near 60 °C.
  3. Wind it. Magnet wire of the sheet's gauge and enamel class, the sheet's number of turns as layers × turns-per-layer, under light constant tension. A strip of Kapton or Nomex every three or four layers protects the outer turns during a hot shot. Bring both leads out through one flange; strain-relieve them. Measure the DC resistance and compare with the sheet's \(R\) — within 5 % means you have the turns you think you have (E1.1.2).
  4. Pot or varnish. A dip in electrical varnish stops the turns moving under their own magnetic forces (they do — \(J \times B\) on every turn is real at these currents).
  5. Wire the driver. Bench supply in constant-voltage mode at the sheet's voltage, a logic-level MOSFET low-side switch, a flyback diode across the coil rated per the sheet, a gate signal from a timer or microcontroller set to the shot length. Note the diode makes the turn-off tail as long as the turn-on rise — \(\tau_{LR}\) both ways (E1.1.6). If you need a fast release, put a Zener or a resistor in series with the diode and accept the higher kick.
  6. Mount and align. Core axis horizontal on an xyz micromanipulator; the pole face approaches the sample from the side at the working distance \(z\). Because force falls roughly as \(z^{-3}\) here, the repeatability of \(z\) is the repeatability of the force — fix \(z\) with a hard stop, not by eye.
  7. Calibrate before believing anything. Three checks, cheapest first. (a) Resistance, above. (b) Field: a Hall probe against the face should read near \(\mu_0 M_s/2 \approx 1\) T at the saturating current (E1.2.1) — most cheap probes top out at 0.3–0.5 T, so use the gaussmeter in the MSE core or read the field a few millimetres out and compare with E1.2.1's fall-off. (c) Force: a bead in a gel of known modulus (or a bead in glycerol, where Stokes drag \(6\pi\eta a v\) gives the force from the speed) — the displacement or velocity should match Tab 3 within the honesty band. If (c) disagrees with (b) by more than the point-dipole caveat allows, the bead's \(M_s\) is not what the datasheet says; measure it.
  8. Then the experiment. Gel on a coverslip at the depth \(h\) you used on Tab 3, beads sparse (beads closer than ~10 diameters shadow each other), shots no longer than the sheet's enamel time and no shorter than \(3\tau_{ve}\) — and a thermocouple under the outer layer wired to stop the driver at the enamel rating.
Honesty line. The drawing models the pole as a cylinder of radius \(R_t\); the real part is a 30° cone ending in that cylinder, and the field in the cone's neck runs above the shank's until the neck saturates — which it does first, and which is fine, because a saturated tip is what Tab 2 assumed. The BOM's thermal numbers are the adiabatic estimates of §1.3 with one convective coefficient; a potted coil sheds heat faster, an enclosed one slower. Treat the enamel time as a ceiling, and the thermocouple as the truth.
  • Design from the displacement backwards: Freq = 6πG·a·u fixes the force, the working distance fixes the tip and the force available, the core fixes the ampere-turns, the copper fixes the clock.
  • The tip radius that maximizes force at distance z sits near Rt ≈ z. Distance is the one variable power cannot buy back.
  • Two clocks must nest: the shot must outlast 3τLR and 3τve and end before the enamel's action integral runs out.
  • When the ledger fails on force, change the bead before the coil: u ∝ a² is the cheapest gain on the bench.
  • The part: one turned rod (L/D from Tab 1, a 30° taper to Tab 4's tip radius), one bobbin that outlasts the enamel, layers × turns of magnet wire, a diode and a MOSFET — and three calibrations before you trust a number.
Default rig: ~680 turns of AWG 22 on a 12 × 40 mm bobbin around a 12 mm × 120 mm soft-iron rod (L/D = 10), tip turned to ~1 mm radius. Drive at ~5 V / 2 A for 0.3 s to saturate the core; expect ~1 µm on a 10 µm iron bead in 5 kPa gel half a millimetre from the face. The enamel gives you seconds at this current — the field runs out long before the wire does.
