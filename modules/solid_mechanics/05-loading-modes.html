Loading Modes — Tension, Compression, Bending, Torsion

Solid Mechanics Foundations · Lesson 5 of 5 — pull it, squash it, bend it, twist it: every complicated load on a solid decomposes into a few basic modes, and geometry decides where inside the solid the stress concentrates.

Background: Tension and compression — the uniform modes

Grab a bar and pull it straight along its axis: that is tension. Push it straight along its axis: that is compression. These are the simplest ways you can load anything, and they are also the friendliest, because of one beautiful fact: every atomic chain shares the load equally. Remember Lesson 1 — a bar is millions of parallel chains of atoms. Pull the bar end-to-end and each chain carries exactly the same slice of the force. So the stress (Lesson 4) is uniform across the cross-section: one number, \( \sigma = F/A \), the same at the skin, the same at the core, the same everywhere.

We keep the books straight with a sign convention: tension is positive, compression is negative. A hanging weight puts the wire above it at \( \sigma = +F/A \); the leg of a table lives its whole life at \( \sigma = -F/A \). Same formula, opposite sign.

One wrinkle before you trust compression completely: slender things cheat. Take a plastic ruler and push its ends together — long before the plastic itself is anywhere near failing, the ruler suddenly bows out sideways and folds. That escape route is called buckling: a stability failure of the shape, not a strength failure of the material. Short, fat columns fail by crushing; long, thin struts buckle first. We will only flag it qualitatively here — the full theory is a course of its own.

$$ \sigma \;=\; \pm\,\frac{F}{A} \qquad \text{(uniform — the same value at every point of the cross-section; } + \text{ tension, } - \text{ compression)} $$

A 60 kg person (\(F \approx 590\ \text{N}\)) hangs from a steel wire of diameter \(d = 2\ \text{mm}\). The cross-section area is \(A = \pi d^2/4 = \pi (0.002)^2/4 = 3.14\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\). The stress is \(\sigma = 590 / 3.14\times10^{-6} = 188\ \text{MPa}\) — everywhere in the wire at once. Mild steel yields at about 250 MPa, so the wire holds with a margin of about \(250/188 \approx 1.3\times\). Thin wire, whole person — that is how efficient pure tension is.

  • Axial loading is uniform: \( \sigma = F/A \) at every point of the section — every fiber shares equally.
  • Sign convention: tension positive, compression negative.
  • Tension is the most efficient way to use material — a 2 mm wire holds a person.
  • Slender members in compression buckle sideways before the material fails — shape instability, not material failure.

Background: Bending — tension on one face, compression on the other

Bend a beam and watch its faces carefully: one surface shortens (compression) while the opposite surface stretches (tension). Somewhere in between there must be a layer that does neither — it keeps exactly its original length. That layer is the neutral axis. So bending is not a new kind of stress at all: it is tension and compression from Tab 1, distributed cleverly through the thickness.

How cleverly? The stress grows linearly with the distance \(y\) from the neutral axis: zero at the axis, maximum at the outer faces. Material far from the axis works hardest; material sitting near the axis does almost nothing — it is dead weight along for the ride. Hold that thought: it is the reason I-beams have their flanges at the extremes and the reason your bones are tubes rather than solid rods. We cash it in fully in Tab 4.

Our test rig is a cantilever: a beam clamped into a wall at one end, loaded at the free tip. The load \(F\) acting at distance \(L\) creates a bending moment \(M = F \cdot L\) at the wall — and since the lever arm is longest there, the moment (and the stress) is largest at the support. That is why diving boards, tree branches, and shelf brackets always break where they attach, not where you push.

$$ \sigma \;=\; \frac{M\,y}{I} \qquad\qquad I_{\text{rect}} = \frac{b\,h^{3}}{12} $$

\(M\): bending moment (N·m). \(y\): distance from the neutral axis (m). \(I\): the second moment of area (m⁴) — the shape's resistance to bending. \(b\): section width, \(h\): section depth.

Cantilever, \(L = 0.5\ \text{m}\), end load \(F = 100\ \text{N}\): the wall moment is \(M = 100 \times 0.5 = 50\ \text{N·m}\). Rectangular section \(b = 20\ \text{mm}\), \(h = 10\ \text{mm}\): \(I = 0.02 \times (0.01)^3 / 12 = 1.67\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}^4\). Peak stress, at the wall's top and bottom faces: \(\sigma_{\max} = M(h/2)/I = 50 \times 0.005 / 1.67\times10^{-9} = 150\ \text{MPa}\). Now double the depth to \(h = 20\ \text{mm}\): \(I\) grows by \(2^3 = 8\times\) while \(h/2\) only doubles, so \(\sigma_{\max}\) drops by \(4\times\) to \(37.5\ \text{MPa}\). Depth is the magic ingredient — it enters \(I\) cubed.

  • Bending = tension on one face + compression on the other, with a stress-free neutral axis between them.
  • Stress is linear in \(y\): \( \sigma = My/I \) — outer fibers work hardest, the core loafs.
  • For a cantilever, \(M = F L\) is largest at the wall — beams fail at the support.
  • \(I = bh^3/12\): doubling the depth cuts peak stress \(4\times\). Depth beats width, cubed versus linear.

Background: Torsion — shear in circles

Now twist. Grip a shaft at one end and rotate the other: each circular cross-section rotates a little relative to its neighbor, like a stack of coins being fanned. The material between two neighboring sections is not being pulled apart or squashed — its layers are sliding past each other. That is shear stress, \( \tau \) (tau), and it is a genuinely third mode, distinct from pull and push. You have met shear before: in Lesson 3, shear was the one load honey could only resist by flowing. A solid resists shear elastically and springs back — that is exactly what a twisted shaft is doing.

Like bending, torsion plays favorites with position — but radially. The shear stress grows linearly with the radius \(r\): zero on the central axis, maximum at the outer skin. The center of a solid shaft does essentially nothing; the skin does everything. Which suggests a trick: if the core is loafing, remove it. That is why drive shafts, bike frames, and bones are hollow tubes — throw away the lazy core, keep the hard-working skin, save the weight.

$$ \tau \;=\; \frac{T\,r}{J} \qquad J_{\text{solid}} = \frac{\pi R^{4}}{2} \qquad J_{\text{tube}} = \frac{\pi\,(R_o^{4} - R_i^{4})}{2} $$

\(T\): applied torque (N·m). \(r\): radius from the shaft axis (m). \(J\): the polar second moment of area (m⁴) — the twist-resisting cousin of \(I\). \(R_o, R_i\): outer and inner radii of a tube.

A solid steel shaft, \(R = 10\ \text{mm}\), carries \(T = 100\ \text{N·m}\): \(J = \pi (0.01)^4/2 = 1.57\times10^{-8}\ \text{m}^4\), so the surface stress is \(\tau_{\max} = TR/J = 100 \times 0.01 / 1.57\times10^{-8} = 63.7\ \text{MPa}\) — and exactly zero at the center. Now bore out a hole of \(R_i = 6\ \text{mm}\): \(J\) only drops to \(\pi(0.01^4 - 0.006^4)/2 = 1.37\times10^{-8}\ \text{m}^4\), so \(\tau_{\max}\) rises just 15% to \(73.1\ \text{MPa}\) — while you removed \((6/10)^2 = 36\%\) of the weight. The core you drilled out was barely working.

  • Torsion loads the material in shear — layers sliding past each other, a third mode beyond pull and push.
  • \( \tau = Tr/J \): shear stress is zero at the axis and maximal at the skin.
  • Hollow shafts are the rational conclusion: remove the idle core, keep the working skin, save weight.
  • Mild steel yields in shear around 150 MPa — roughly half its tensile yield.

Level up: combined loading and the shape game

Real parts rarely get the courtesy of a single clean mode. Push on a bike pedal and the spindle feels bending (your weight on a lever) and torsion (that lever twisting the crank axle) at the same time. A lamppost in wind bends and twists. The good news: while stresses stay elastic, they superpose — the normal stresses simply add point by point, \( \sigma_{\text{total}} = \sigma_{\text{bend}} + \sigma_{\text{axial}} \), while the torsional shear \( \tau \) acts on different planes and is tracked alongside. Combining a σ and a τ at one point into a single "how close to failure" number takes machinery we will not build here — Mohr's circle and the von Mises criterion — and that, honestly, is the opening act of your next course.

What we can finish here is the payoff of the whole series: the shape game. Look at the definition \(I = \int y^2\,dA\). Every patch of area counts by the square of its distance from the neutral axis — so the same kilograms of steel, pushed away from the axis, buy wildly more bending stiffness. That is the entire idea of the I-beam. And it closes the loop on this course: atoms set the stiffness \(E\) (Lesson 1), grains set the strength (Lesson 2), the material law converts load to stress (Lessons 3–4), and shape decides where the stress goes (Lesson 5). Materials are given to you; shape is where you get to be clever.

$$ \sigma_{\text{total}} = \sigma_{\text{bend}} + \sigma_{\text{axial}} \qquad\qquad I = \int y^{2}\,dA $$

Elastic stresses superpose; torsional shear \( \tau \) lives on different planes and combines via Mohr's circle / von Mises (next course). The integral says: area far from the neutral axis counts quadratically.

A solid square \(20\times20\ \text{mm}\) has \(A = 400\ \text{mm}^2\) and \(I = 20 \times 20^3/12 = 13{,}333\ \text{mm}^4\). Reshape the same 400 mm² into an idealized I-section 60 mm deep: two flanges \(35\times5\ \text{mm}\) centered at \(y = \pm27.5\ \text{mm}\), joined by a thin \(1\times50\ \text{mm}\) web. Parallel-axis theorem: \(I = 2\,[\,35\times5^3/12 + (35\times5)\times27.5^2\,] + 1\times50^3/12 \approx 275{,}800\ \text{mm}^4\) — about 20.7× stiffer in bending with the same amount of material. (The 1 mm web is idealized; real webs are thicker so they don't buckle.)

  • Real loads are combinations; elastic stresses superpose point by point. Full combination machinery (Mohr, von Mises) is next course.
  • \(I = \int y^2\,dA\): pushing area away from the neutral axis buys stiffness quadratically — same mass, ~20× stiffer.
  • No free lunch: open thin sections (I-beams) are excellent in bending and terrible in torsion; closed tubes are the twist-resistant choice.
  • The course in one line: atoms set \(E\), grains set strength, the material law makes stress, and shape decides where the stress goes.

  2. At full I-beam, compare the two bar charts: I up ~20.7×, σmax down. How much more End load can the I-beam take before matching the square's stress?

  4. Design challenge: find the smallest morph % that keeps σmax under 150 MPa at F = 300 N with zero torque.
