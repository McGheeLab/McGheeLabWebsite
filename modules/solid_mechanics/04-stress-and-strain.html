Stress and Strain — Why Force and Displacement Aren't Enough

Solid Mechanics Foundations · Lesson 4 of 5 — Measure force vs. displacement on two steel rods of different sizes and you get two different curves — for the SAME material. Divide out the geometry and one single curve remains: a description of the material itself.

Background: the problem — same material, different curve

In Lesson 1 you built a solid out of atomic springs and found that an object's stiffness is \(k = k_{\text{bond}}\,M/N\): it depends on how many chains share the load (\(M\), the fatness) and how many bonds share the stretch (\(N\), the length). In other words, stiffness depends on shape. That sounds like a footnote. It is actually a crisis.

Here's why. Suppose you pull on a steel rod in a testing machine and carefully record force \(F\) versus stretch \(\Delta L\). You get a beautiful straight line. Now hand that plot to an engineer designing a bridge. It is useless to them — because your line is a fact about one particular rod. A thick short steel rod and a long thin steel rod, pulled in the same machine, give wildly different \(F\)–\(\Delta L\) lines. Same atoms. Same bonds. Identical steel. You cannot publish a handbook of materials if every entry depends on the size of the sample you happened to test.

How different are the lines? The slope of the \(F\)–\(\Delta L\) line is the object stiffness \(k\), and for a rod pulled along its length it works out to:

$$ k \;=\; \frac{E\,A}{L_0} \qquad\qquad \text{stiffness} \;=\; \text{material number} \times \text{area} \div \text{length} $$

Here \(A\) is the cross-section area of the rod (how fat it is — more parallel chains of atoms), \(L_0\) is its starting length (more bonds in series), and \(E\) is "the material number" — one single number that belongs to the steel itself. We'll define \(E\) properly in Tab 4; for now just notice that everything else in the formula is geometry.

Steel has \(E = 200\ \text{GPa} = 200\times10^{9}\ \text{N/m}^2\). Rod A: \(L_0 = 1\ \text{m}\), diameter \(d = 20\ \text{mm}\), so \(A = \pi d^2/4 = 3.14\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\) and \(k = 200\times10^{9} \times 3.14\times10^{-4} / 1 = 6.3\times10^{7}\ \text{N/m}\). Rod B: \(L_0 = 2\ \text{m}\), \(d = 10\ \text{mm}\), so \(A = 7.85\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2\) and \(k = 200\times10^{9} \times 7.85\times10^{-5} / 2 = 7.9\times10^{6}\ \text{N/m}\) — 8× softer. Same steel, same bonds, completely different curve.

  • A force–displacement plot describes one object, not a material.
  • Object stiffness \(k = EA/L_0\): fatter → stiffer, longer → softer.
  • Two rods of identical steel can differ in stiffness by 8× (or 800×) from geometry alone.
  • Whatever \(E\) is, it's the only part of \(k\) that belongs to the material itself.
  1. Load the Rod A scenario and note where your line sits on the plot. Now drag Diameter down to 10 mm and Length up to 2 m — watch your line swing down onto Rod B's. Not one atom of the steel changed.
  2. Find a third geometry (neither A nor B) that gives the same stiffness as Rod A. Hint: \(k = EA/L_0\) — what happens if you double both \(A\) and \(L_0\)?
  3. Try the Stubby bolt scenario. Read the highlighted stiffness stat. How many times stiffer than Rod B is it?
  4. Keeping the force fixed, list which sliders move the current dot on the plot. Which one does not change the slope?

Background: the fix — divide out the geometry

The escape from Tab 1's trap takes exactly two divisions. Slow down here — this is the most important mental move in all of solid mechanics.

Division one: force per area. Remember from Lesson 1 that the pull you apply is shared by every parallel chain of atoms in the cross-section. A fat rod feels a big total force but each chain barely notices; a thin rod puts the same total force through far fewer chains, so each one is worked hard. So don't ask "how hard is the rod being pulled?" — ask "how hard is each atomic chain being pulled?" That's force divided by cross-section area: \(\sigma = F/A\), called stress (Greek sigma). Its units are newtons per square meter, named pascals (Pa). Real materials live at millions of pascals, so we usually quote megapascals (MPa).

Division two: stretch per length. The total stretch \(\Delta L\) is shared by every bond along the rod's length. A 2 m rod that stretches 2 mm and a 1 m rod that stretches 1 mm are in exactly the same condition atom-by-atom: each bond is stretched by the same tiny fraction. So don't ask "how far did it stretch?" — ask "how far is each bond being stretched?" That's stretch divided by original length: \(\varepsilon = \Delta L/L_0\), called strain (Greek epsilon). It's a pure ratio — dimensionless — often quoted as a percent.

$$ \sigma = \frac{F}{A}\ \ [\text{Pa}] \qquad\quad \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0}\ \ [-] \qquad\quad 1\ \text{MPa} = 10^{6}\ \text{N/m}^2 \approx \text{10 kg of force on a fingernail-sized mm}^2 $$

Now the magic. Re-plot the same pulling experiments as \(\sigma\) versus \(\varepsilon\) instead of \(F\) versus \(\Delta L\), and rods of every size collapse onto one single line. Thick, thin, long, short — one line. That line is no longer a fact about a rod. It is a property of steel itself, fit for a handbook. Watch it happen in the twin plots below: the left plot squirms every time you touch a geometry slider; the right plot doesn't care.

Take Rod B: \(L_0 = 2\ \text{m}\), \(d = 10\ \text{mm}\) (\(A = 7.85\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2\)), pulled with \(F = 15\ \text{kN}\). Stress: \(\sigma = 15\times10^{3} / 7.85\times10^{-5} = 1.91\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa} = 191\ \text{MPa}\). The rod stretches \(\Delta L = 1.91\ \text{mm}\), so the strain is \(\varepsilon = 1.91\times10^{-3}\ \text{m} / 2\ \text{m} = 9.55\times10^{-4} \approx 0.096\%\). Each bond in the steel is stretched by about a tenth of a percent — and any other steel rod at 191 MPa would show exactly the same 0.096%.

  • Stress \(\sigma = F/A\): the force each parallel chain of atoms actually feels (Pa, MPa).
  • Strain \(\varepsilon = \Delta L/L_0\): the fractional stretch each bond actually feels (dimensionless).
  • On a \(\sigma\)–\(\varepsilon\) plot, every rod of the same material lands on one line.
  • That collapse is what turns "a measurement of my rod" into "a property of steel".

Background: contact — same force, very different stress

Dividing force by area isn't just a bookkeeping trick for rods. It matters most where two bodies touch. Press your palm against a wall with 50 N — you feel gentle pressure. Put the same 50 N behind a thumbtack and the point goes through the wall. The force is identical; only the contact area changed, and with it the stress. Force never tells you whether something dents, cuts, or punctures — stress does.

Now go one level deeper, because the surface isn't the whole story. Below the contact patch, the stress spreads out into the solid in a bulb: intense just under the contact, fading with depth, and mostly gone below a depth of roughly the contact width. A wide flat punch makes a gentle, shallow, spread-out bulb. A narrow punch (or a point) makes a fierce, concentrated one that hammers a small volume of material. This is exactly why we spread loads on purpose: washers under bolt heads, snowshoes on snow, concrete footings under buildings — all of them are area-enlarging devices that tame the stress bulb.

$$ p = \frac{F}{A_{\text{contact}}} \qquad\qquad \sigma_z = \frac{3F}{2\pi z^2}\ \ (\text{Boussinesq, on the axis below a point load}) $$

The first formula is the mean contact pressure. The second (Boussinesq, 1885; see Johnson) gives the vertical stress at depth \(z\) directly below a concentrated point load on an elastic solid: stress dies off as \(1/z^2\) — spread with depth is automatic, but near a sharp point it starts brutally high.

A 700 N person (about 71 kg). Standing on two boots, \(A \approx 0.06\ \text{m}^2\): \(p = 700/0.06 \approx 12\ \text{kPa}\) — the floor doesn't even notice. The same person on one stiletto heel, \(A \approx 1\ \text{cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\): \(p = 700/10^{-4} = 7\ \text{MPa}\) — about 600× more. That exceeds the bearing strength of pine floorboards (~5 MPa across the grain, as an order-of-magnitude figure), which is why heels leave dents. Same person, same weight.

  • Force alone predicts nothing about damage at a contact — pressure \(p = F/A\) does.
  • Below the surface the stress forms a bulb: intense within a depth ≈ the contact width, fading as ~\(1/z^2\).
  • Wide contact → shallow gentle bulb; narrow contact → concentrated fierce bulb, same total force.
  • Washers, snowshoes, and footings are all the same trick: buy area, lower stress.
  1. Load Thumbtack (narrow), then drag Contact width slowly up to 80 mm. Watch the bulb go from a hot needle to a faint wide glow — the total force never changed.
  2. At any width, double the Force. Does the bulb change shape or just intensity? Compare with halving the width instead.
  3. Check the "depth where stress halves" stat as you widen the contact. Confirm the rule of thumb: the bulb reaches roughly one contact-width deep.
  4. Find the widest contact that still triggers the red "punch-through" verdict at 1000 N. Now you know how big a washer needs to be.

Level up: Young's modulus, and Hooke's law in material form

In Tab 2, every steel rod collapsed onto one \(\sigma\)–\(\varepsilon\) line. The slope of that line finally deserves its name: it is Young's modulus, \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon\), measured in pascals (usually GPa). It is the material stiffness — the number that goes in the handbook. And with it, Hooke's law graduates. The object version you met in Lesson 1, \(F = kx\), becomes the material version:

$$ \sigma = E\,\varepsilon \qquad\quad E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} \qquad\quad k = \frac{E A}{L_0} \qquad\quad \nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{trans}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{axial}}} $$

Now the whole chain of ideas from Lesson 1 clicks together. Bond stiffness sets the modulus: \(E \approx k_{\text{bond}}/r_0\) (stiff bonds, small atoms → stiff material). The modulus and the geometry set the object stiffness: \(k = EA/L_0\). Atom → material → object, with \(E\) as the handoff in the middle. That's why \(E\) spans such a spectacular ladder across real materials (typical values): rubber ~0.01–0.1 GPa (bonds barely engaged — coiled molecules uncoil instead), wood along the grain ~10 GPa, aluminum 69 GPa, steel 200 GPa, diamond ~1100 GPa (the stiffest covalent bonds, packed tight). A factor of 100,000 from rubber to diamond — all of it traceable to the bond springs of Lesson 1.

One more character joins the cast: pull a bar longer and it also gets thinner — the sideways shrink is proportional to the axial stretch. The ratio is Poisson's ratio, \(\nu = -\varepsilon_{\text{lateral}}/\varepsilon_{\text{axial}}\), about 0.3 for metals (rubber approaches 0.5: it conserves volume; cork is near 0, which is why it pushes straight into a bottle). No new sim needed — the block below shows it happening.

An aluminum rod: \(E = 69\ \text{GPa}\), \(L_0 = 1.5\ \text{m}\), \(d = 12\ \text{mm}\) (\(A = \pi d^2/4 = 1.13\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\)), pulled with \(F = 8\ \text{kN}\). Stress: \(\sigma = 8\times10^{3}/1.13\times10^{-4} = 70.8\ \text{MPa}\). Strain: \(\varepsilon = \sigma/E = 70.8\times10^{6}/69\times10^{9} = 1.03\times10^{-3}\). Stretch: \(\Delta L = \varepsilon L_0 = 1.03\times10^{-3}\times1.5 = 1.54\ \text{mm}\). Sideways, with \(\nu = 0.33\): \(\varepsilon_t = -\nu\varepsilon = -0.33\times1.03\times10^{-3} = -3.4\times10^{-4}\), so the 12 mm diameter shrinks by \(12\times0.33\times1.03\times10^{-3} \approx 0.004\ \text{mm}\) — four microns.

  • Young's modulus \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon\) is the slope of the collapsed line — the material's own stiffness.
  • Hooke's law, material edition: \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\). The object edition \(F = kx\) follows via \(k = EA/L_0\).
  • \(E\) spans ~0.01 GPa (rubber) to ~1100 GPa (diamond) — set by bond stiffness, \(E \approx k_{\text{bond}}/r_0\).
  • Poisson's ratio \(\nu \approx 0.3\) for metals: stretch it longer, it gets thinner.
