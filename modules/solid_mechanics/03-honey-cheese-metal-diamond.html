Honey, Cheese, Metal, Diamond — Force vs. Displacement

Solid Mechanics Foundations · Lesson 3 of 5 — Push on honey, cheese, a steel bar, and a diamond, and you get four completely different force–displacement stories. Two simple cartoon elements — a spring and a dashpot — are enough to tell all of them.

Background: same thumb, same speed, four totally different answers

Imagine pressing your thumb — same thumb, same speed — into four things sitting on the kitchen counter: a jar of honey, a block of soft cheese, a steel bar, and a diamond.

Steel and diamond answer like the springs from Lesson 1: the force grows in proportion to how far you push. Push twice as deep, feel twice the force. Diamond's line is far steeper than steel's — it is a stiffer spring — but both play the same game, and both spring back the instant you let go, leaving no trace.

Honey plays a different game entirely. The force it pushes back with doesn't care how far you've pushed — only how fast you're pushing right now. Stop moving and the force vanishes on the spot. And honey never springs back: the dent you made simply flows shut and the honey forgets you were ever there.

Cheese sits in between: part spring (it pushes back harder the deeper you go, and partly recovers when you let go) and part honey (it slowly gives way under your thumb and keeps some of the dent forever).

These two behaviors get names you'll use for the rest of the course. ELASTIC means the material stores the work you do on it and springs back — force tracks displacement. VISCOUS means the material flows and forgets — force tracks speed. And the cartoon elements that capture them are the spring and the dashpot (picture a loose piston in an oil-filled cylinder):

$$ F_{\text{spring}} = k\,x \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad F_{\text{dashpot}} = c\,v $$

Here \(x\) is the displacement (how far you've pushed, in m), \(v\) is the speed (how fast you're pushing, in m/s), \(k\) is the stiffness (N/m — newtons of push-back per meter of depth), and \(c\) is the damping coefficient (N·s/m — newtons of push-back per meter-per-second of speed). Spring force forgets speed; dashpot force forgets depth.

Press a flat 1 cm² contact (\(A = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\), on a roughly 1-cm-sized piece, \(L = 0.01\ \text{m}\)) in by \(x = 1\) mm at \(v = 1\) mm/s. Order-of-magnitude estimates: Steel (\(E \approx 200\) GPa): \(k = EA/L = 2\times10^{11} \times 10^{-4} / 0.01 \approx 2\times10^{9}\ \text{N/m}\), so \(F = kx \approx 2\times10^{6}\ \text{N}\) — two hundred tonnes of push-back. You dent your thumb, not the steel. Diamond (\(E \approx 1100\) GPa) is ~5× stiffer still. Honey (viscosity \(\eta \approx 10\) Pa·s at room temperature): \(F \approx \eta (v/L) A = 10 \times (0.001/0.01) \times 10^{-4} = 10^{-4}\ \text{N}\) — a tenth of a millinewton, no matter how deep you are. Soft cheese (\(E \sim 10^{5}\) Pa): \(F \approx EA\,x/L = 10^{5} \times 10^{-4} \times 0.1 = 1\ \text{N}\) — exactly thumb-scale. These are order-of-magnitude estimates, not precise values — but the ten decades separating honey from steel are real.

  • Elastic materials (steel, diamond) push back in proportion to depth: \(F = kx\). They store and spring back.
  • Viscous materials (honey) push back in proportion to speed: \(F = cv\). They flow and forget.
  • Viscoelastic materials (cheese) do both at once — part spring, part dashpot.
  • The forces span ten orders of magnitude: ~10⁻⁴ N for honey to ~10⁶ N for steel at the same poke.

Background: the defining experiment — same depth, different speeds

Here is the single experiment that separates springs from dashpots forever: push to the same depth at different speeds. A spring material could not care less about speed — only the final depth sets the force. A dashpot's force is proportional to speed and drops to zero the instant you stop moving.

The clean lab version is called ramp-and-hold: push in steadily over a ramp time \(t_{\text{ramp}}\), then hold perfectly still. Watch what the force does:

Spring: force climbs with the ramp, then HOLDS — for as long as you keep the stretch, the spring keeps pushing. Perfect memory. Dashpot: the speed is constant during the ramp, so the force is a constant plateau — then the moment you stop, \(v = 0\) and the force is instantly ZERO. No memory at all. Cheese: force rises, then RELAXES gradually during the hold. That gradual fade is the fingerprint of viscoelasticity, and the time it takes to fade is the material's characteristic time \(\tau\) (tau) — how long the material remembers being pushed.

$$ F_{\text{dashpot}} = c\,v \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \text{during hold } (v = 0)\!:\; F = 0 \qquad\qquad \tau = \frac{c}{k} \;\; \text{("how long the material remembers")} $$

Where does \(\tau = c/k\) come from? When a spring and a dashpot team up (next tab), the spring supplies the memory and the dashpot slowly erases it — stiff spring or runny dashpot means fast forgetting, so the ratio \(c/k\) sets the clock. Keep that formula in your pocket; Tab 3 earns it properly.

Honey channel test: drag a flat plate across a honey layer at 1 mm/s and measure the drag force. Now drag at 10 mm/s: the force is exactly 10× bigger, because \(F = cv\) and \(v\) went up 10×. Now instead double how far the plate has traveled while keeping the speed at 1 mm/s: the force doesn't change at all — displacement never enters the dashpot law. A spring tested the same way would do precisely the opposite: 10× the speed, same force; 2× the displacement, 2× the force.

  • Ramp-and-hold is the diagnostic: watch the force during the hold.
  • Spring → force holds forever. Dashpot → force is zero the instant motion stops.
  • Viscoelastic (cheese) → force relaxes gradually, on a timescale \(\tau = c/k\).
  • Whether a material "acts solid" depends on how your test time compares to its \(\tau\).
  1. On Cheese (both), set ramp time 2 s and memory \(\tau\) = 3 s. Read the highlighted "force remembered" stat after the hold.
  2. Now drag ramp time down to 0.2 s (a fast jab). The verdict should tell you the cheese is acting like a spring. Why — what didn't it have time to do?
  3. Drag ramp time up to 10 s with \(\tau\) = 0.5 s (a slow lean). Now what does the force curve look like during the ramp, and what's left after the hold?
  4. Switch to Pure spring, then Pure dashpot: confirm those two are the extreme cases your cheese curve lives between.

Background: two elements, two ways to wire them, two classic materials

One spring, one dashpot. There are only two ways to connect them, and each way is a famous material model.

SERIES — the Maxwell model. Wall — spring — dashpot — handle, in a line. The same force runs through both elements, and their displacements add. Now stretch the pair and hold the stretch fixed: at first the spring carries all the force, but the dashpot slowly gives way, letting the spring shorten while the total length stays put. The force quietly drains away. That is STRESS RELAXATION — the behavior of cheese, bread dough, and glacier ice (yes, glaciers: hold the load for centuries and ice flows like a fluid).

PARALLEL — the Kelvin–Voigt model. Spring and dashpot side by side between two bars. Now both elements see the same displacement, and their forces add. Hang a constant weight on it: the spring "wants" to stretch to \(F_0/k\) immediately, but the dashpot only permits motion at a finite speed, so the stretch creeps gradually toward that limit. That is CREEP — the behavior of memory foam and the cartilage in your knees (which is why you're measurably taller in the morning).

Both models share the same clock, the characteristic time \(\tau = c/k\): Maxwell relaxes on it, Kelvin–Voigt creeps on it.

$$ \text{Maxwell (series), hold stretch:}\;\; F(t) = F_0\,e^{-t/\tau} \qquad \text{Kelvin–Voigt (parallel), hang weight:}\;\; x(t) = \frac{F_0}{k}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) \qquad \tau = \frac{c}{k} $$

Read the exponentials like a clock: after one \(\tau\), Maxwell has kept only \(e^{-1} \approx 37\%\) of its force, and Kelvin–Voigt has covered \(1 - e^{-1} \approx 63\%\) of its final creep. After \(3\tau\), the story is ~95% over either way.

A memory-foam-like Kelvin–Voigt pad: \(k = 500\ \text{N/m}\), \(c = 2500\ \text{N·s/m}\) \(\Rightarrow \tau = c/k = 5\ \text{s}\). Set a 10 N weight on it. Final sink: \(x_\infty = F_0/k = 10/500 = 0.02\ \text{m} = 20\ \text{mm}\). After 5 s (one \(\tau\)): \(x = 20(1 - e^{-1}) = 12.6\ \text{mm}\). After 15 s (three \(\tau\)): \(x = 20(1 - e^{-3}) = 19.0\ \text{mm}\). The foam "slowly accepts" your shape — and that 5-second lag is exactly what makes memory foam feel like memory foam.

  • Series (Maxwell): same force, displacements add → stress relaxation under held stretch (cheese, dough, glacier ice).
  • Parallel (Kelvin–Voigt): same displacement, forces add → creep toward \(F_0/k\) under held load (memory foam, cartilage).
  • One number runs both clocks: \(\tau = c/k\). At \(t = \tau\): 37% force left (Maxwell) or 63% of the creep done (KV).
  • Wiring, not ingredients, decides the story — same two elements, opposite behaviors.

Level up: stress relaxation with real units

Time to talk the way a mechanics course talks. Instead of force \(F\) and displacement \(x\) — which describe your particular block and your particular thumb — we switch to stress \(\sigma\) (sigma) and strain \(\varepsilon\) (epsilon). Lesson 4 defines them properly; for now it's enough to know they are force and displacement normalized so the numbers describe the material, not the object. In material form the Maxwell model's spring becomes a modulus \(E\) (in Pa) and its dashpot becomes a viscosity \(\eta\) (eta, in Pa·s), with the same clock: \(\tau = \eta/E\).

Because series elements share the stress while their strains add, the Maxwell model obeys a real governing equation — your first viscoelastic ODE:

$$ \frac{d\varepsilon}{dt} = \frac{1}{E}\frac{d\sigma}{dt} + \frac{\sigma}{\eta} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{step strain } \varepsilon_0:\;\; \sigma(t) = E\,\varepsilon_0\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{\eta}{E} $$

Read the ODE aloud: the strain rate has an elastic part (stress changing stretches the spring) plus a viscous part (standing stress makes the dashpot flow). Squeeze the material by a fixed strain \(\varepsilon_0\) and hold: the stress starts at \(E\varepsilon_0\) and decays exponentially.

One honest limitation: a bare Maxwell model relaxes all the way to zero — wait long enough and it's indistinguishable from a fluid, but real cheese keeps some stress forever. The one-sentence fix is to add a spring \(E_\infty\) in parallel with the Maxwell branch — the resulting three-element circuit is the Standard Linear Solid, and it relaxes to a plateau \(\sigma_\infty = E_\infty\varepsilon_0\) instead of to zero. The sim below has an \(E_\infty\) slider, set to 0 by default so you start with pure Maxwell.

A soft-cheese-like Maxwell solid: \(E = 100\ \text{kPa}\), \(\eta = 1\ \text{MPa·s}\) \(\Rightarrow \tau = \eta/E = 10^{6}/10^{5} = 10\ \text{s}\). Apply a step strain \(\varepsilon_0 = 0.1\) (a 10% squeeze) and hold. Initial stress: \(\sigma_0 = E\varepsilon_0 = 10^{5} \times 0.1 = 10\ \text{kPa}\). After 10 s (one \(\tau\)): \(\sigma = 10\,e^{-1} = 3.7\ \text{kPa}\). After 30 s (three \(\tau\)): \(\sigma = 10\,e^{-3} \approx 0.5\ \text{kPa}\). The cheese "forgets" 95% of the squeeze in half a minute — which is why the slice flattens under the sandwich instead of pushing the bread back up.

  • Stress \(\sigma\) and strain \(\varepsilon\) are force and displacement normalized to describe the material (formal definitions: Lesson 4).
  • Material Maxwell model: spring → modulus \(E\) (Pa), dashpot → viscosity \(\eta\) (Pa·s), \(\tau = \eta/E\).
  • Step strain \(\varepsilon_0\) → \(\sigma(t) = E\varepsilon_0 e^{-t/\tau}\): stress relaxation in real units.
  • Bare Maxwell relaxes to zero (secretly a fluid); adding a parallel spring \(E_\infty\) makes it a Standard Linear Solid with plateau \(E_\infty\varepsilon_0\).
