1.1 · Open the door and watch

Lesson 1 ended with a promise: measure what happens when someone opens the incubator. Here it is properly — with the two things that actually set the answer exposed as sliders: how much stuff is inside the box that stores heat, and which of the two doors is open while you stand there.

Because there are two. Ours — a Heracell VIOS 160i — has an outer door you swing open and an inner glass door behind it. You can see your whole rack through the glass with the outer door open, and the box barely notices. It notices a great deal when the glass is open.

Do not read the curve yet. Open the doors, watch it come back, and find which second is the expensive one.

  1. Start on our VIOS 160i: outer door 40 s, glass door 6 s. That is the routine — open up, find your flask, then open the glass and take it. Note the dip.
  2. Now drag glass door open up to 40 s without touching anything else. Same total opening, same everything — and watch the grey ghost curve you have just turned into.
  3. Put the glass back to 6 s and drag the outer door from 10 s to 120 s. How much does a full two minutes of hunting cost, if the glass stayed shut?
  4. Switch to air-jacketed and water-jacketed at the same door times. The dip changes — but watch what happened to the recovery time.
  5. Drag thermal mass across its whole range. There is a trade-off; state it in one sentence before you read on.

Temperature after the door opens

Name it: one differential equation, two time constants

A body at temperature T losing heat to surroundings at Tenv through a surface loses it in proportion to how far from equilibrium it is:

eq. 2.1.1 $$\frac{dT}{dt} \;=\; -\frac{T-T_{\rm env}}{\tau},\qquad \tau \;=\; \frac{mc}{hA}$$

Solve it and you get the curve you have been watching — and it is the equation Lesson 1 handed you without earning:

eq. 1.5.1 again — previewed in Lesson 1, earned here $$X(t) \;=\; X_{\rm set} - \Delta X\,e^{-t/\tau}$$

Now the trade-off is visible in the symbols. Thermal mass m sits on the top of τ. A heavy box:

  • dips less while the door is open, because the same amount of escaping heat is a smaller fraction of what is stored;
  • recovers more slowly afterwards, because τ is longer.
There is no incubator design that both holds temperature during an opening and recovers instantly afterwards — τ cannot be large and small at the same time. Manufacturers pick a point on that trade-off and market it as a feature. What you control is not the design; it is the number of openings and how long each one lasts.

And now the second door, which is the one you actually control

An inner glass door does not stop heat leaving. It stops the air leaving — and bulk air exchange, not conduction through glass, is what carries almost all of the loss. So the same opening has two phases with two different time constants, and eq. 1.5.1 runs through both in series. Multiply the two exponentials and they simply add in the exponent:

eq. 2.1.2 $$\Delta T \;=\; \Delta T_{\max}\left(1-e^{-\left(\frac{t_{1}}{\tau_{\rm outer}}+\frac{t_{2}}{\tau_{\rm glass}}\right)}\right)$$

where t1 is the time with only the outer door open and t2 the time with the glass open as well. Each second is charged at the rate of whichever door is open. On a real box τouter is close to an order of magnitude longer than τglass — the sim uses 9×, and the exact ratio is not the point. The point is that it is not 1.

Hunting is cheap. Grabbing is expensive. A second spent looking through the glass costs roughly a ninth of a second spent with it open — so the whole discipline is: get the outer door open, find your flask through the glass without hurrying, then open the glass and take it in one movement.
Worked example — why does a 60 s opening not cost twelve times a 5 s one?
  1. Write what you know. While the door is open the box is losing toward room temperature with its own open-door time constant τo ≈ 26 s, from 37 °C toward 22 °C, so ΔTmax = 15 °C.
  2. Assemble the equation. The approach to room temperature is the same exponential (eq. 1.5.1) read the other way:
    $$\Delta T(t_{\rm open}) \;=\; \Delta T_{\max}\left(1-e^{-t_{\rm open}/\tau_o}\right)$$
  3. Plug in both openings.
    $$\Delta T(5) = 15\left(1-e^{-5/26}\right) = 2.6\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \qquad \Delta T(60) = 15\left(1-e^{-60/26}\right) = 13.5\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$$
  4. Compare. Twelve times the door time gives 5.2 times the temperature loss, not twelve.
The cost of an opening saturates — the first few seconds are the expensive ones, because that is when the gradient is steepest. Practically: a quick, decisive, well-planned opening is worth far more than a short one, and "I'll just leave it ajar while I find my flask" is the single most expensive thing you can do to an incubator.
Worked example — how many seconds of hunting is one second of grabbing worth?
  1. Write what you know. Our box, m = 30 kg, so τglass = 0.47(30) + 5 = 19 s and τouter = 9 τglass = 171 s.
  2. Assemble the equation. In eq. 2.1.2 the two doors appear only through the sum in the exponent, so two routines cost the same when their exponents match:
    $$\frac{t_{1}}{\tau_{\rm outer}}+\frac{t_{2}}{\tau_{\rm glass}} \;=\; \text{the same number}$$
  3. Solve symbolically first. One second of glass time is worth Δt1 seconds of outer-door time when
    $$\frac{\Delta t_{1}}{\tau_{\rm outer}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm glass}} \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta t_{1}=\frac{\tau_{\rm outer}}{\tau_{\rm glass}}=9$$
  4. Sanity-check it against the sim. A 40 s opening with 6 s of glass gives an exponent of 34/171 + 6/19 = 0.52, and a dip of 6.0 °C. The same 40 s with the glass open throughout gives 40/19 = 2.11 and a dip of 13.2 °C.
One second with the glass open costs nine seconds of standing there with only the outer door open. That is the whole reason the routine is "open, look, then open the glass" — and it is why the honest instruction is not "be quick", it is "be quick with the glass, and unhurried with the outer door." Rushing the wrong one is how people drop flasks.
McGhee Lab practice — our VIOS 160i Note from Dr. McGhee. The routine, in order, every time: (1) know which shelf and which rack before you touch anything; (2) open the outer door and take your time looking through the glass — this part is nearly free; (3) open the glass door and take your flask in one movement, as quickly as you can do it safely; (4) close the glass first, then the outer door. If you find yourself hunting with the glass door open, stop, close it, and look again. That is not fussiness — it is a factor of nine.

The numbers on this page for our unit (thermal mass, τ) are representative of a 165 L direct-heat box, not measured on ours. The shape is right and the ratio between the two doors is the lesson; if you want the real figures, they are a morning's work with a datalogger and would make a good first project.

1.2 · Not everywhere in the box is 37 °C

"The incubator is at 37" describes the sensor, which is in one place. During and after an opening, the box has a map, and where you put a flask decides which part of that map it lives in.

Then there is a second map over the same six positions, and the two of them want opposite things. Watch the temperatures first. Then turn up reaches to the back and watch what the back positions cost the front ones.

  1. Open the door 30 s and watch which shelf positions recover last. On temperature alone, decide where you would put a culture you care about.
  2. Now drag reaches to the back from 0 to 18 a day — a shared box in a busy room. The fills do not change. The borders do.
  3. Ask the awkward question: if the good thermal position is the back, who is paying for it?
  4. Tick each project has its own pull-out rack. Which of the two problems does that fix, and why does "be careful" not fix it?
  5. Turn stacked on. Which dish in a stack of four is warmest, and why?

Two true rules that contradict each other

Every guide will tell you that mission-critical cultures go at the back, and the thermal map is why. Almost none of them mention the other half: the back is only reachable through the front. Every trip to a back position takes a sleeve over somebody's flask, and that sleeve is the payload you met in Lesson 1 §3.3 — the same one, on a shorter trip.

Nothing lands in a closed flask. It lands on the neck, the cap and the shoulder, and it goes in the next time that flask is opened in the cabinet — by somebody who did everything right.

The two maps cannot both be satisfied by choosing a position, because they are not about the same person. The thermal map is about your culture. The traffic map is about everybody else's. Whoever takes the good position is charging the cost to whoever has the bad one — which is exactly the kind of problem that never gets solved by asking people to be careful.

What does solve it is removing the reach: give each project a rack it can be pulled out on, so the arm never travels over anything. That is the only intervention on this page that takes a number to zero rather than making it smaller.

Now the chemistry that produced the colour

CO₂ from the gas phase dissolves in the medium in proportion to its partial pressure — Henry's law, with a solubility constant measured in plasma-like fluid at 37 °C:

eq. 2.2.1 $$[\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\rm aq} \;=\; 0.0307 \times p\mathrm{CO_2}\quad\mathrm{(mM,\ with\ }p\mathrm{CO_2\ in\ mmHg)}$$

Dissolved CO₂ becomes carbonic acid and then donates a proton, giving bicarbonate. That is a weak-acid/conjugate-base pair, so its pH follows the Henderson–Hasselbalch relation:

eq. 2.2.2 $$\mathrm{pH} \;=\; \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}$$

Substituting the pair, with the apparent pKa of 6.1:

eq. 2.2.3 $$\mathrm{pH} \;=\; 6.1 + \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{0.0307\;p\mathrm{CO_2}}\right)$$

Read what that says. The pH of your medium is a ratio — the bicarbonate the manufacturer put in the powder, over the CO₂ the incubator is holding. Change either one and the pH moves. Which is why:

  • 5% is not a magic number — it is the CO₂ that balances about 24 mM bicarbonate at pH 7.4. RPMI has 2.0 g/L NaHCO₃ ≈ 24 mM, and is designed for 5%.
  • DMEM has 3.7 g/L ≈ 44 mM, and eq. 2.2.3 says that runs alkaline at 5% — which is exactly what the sim showed you and exactly why high-bicarbonate DMEM formulations specify 10% CO₂.
  • The percentage on the incubator belongs to the medium, not to the cells. Match the gas to the bottle you are actually using.
Honesty about the model Where eq. 2.2.3 stops being the whole story. Take CO₂ to 0.04% and the equation predicts pH 9.6. Real medium left on the bench drifts to about 8.2–8.5 instead, because bicarbonate is not the only buffer once you leave the physiological range — serum protein and any HEPES in the formulation take over — and because outgassing is slow. The equation is right about the direction and about how fast it starts; it is not right about where it stops. The sim marks that region.

2.2 · Why the pH crashes instead of drifting

Cultures do not go gently acid. They sit at 7.3 for two days and then look orange on the third and yellow on the fourth. That shape is not about the cells speeding up. It is about the buffer running out.

Add acid to the medium and watch how hard it fights back — then keep going.

  1. Add lactate a few mM at a time and read the buffer-capacity number.
  2. Find the lactate concentration at which the capacity has halved.
  3. Compare a 24 mM bicarbonate medium with a 44 mM one. Which survives more acid, and by how much?
  4. Put the same amount of acid into 5 mL and into 10 mL of medium. What changed?

Controls

pH as acid is added — the titration your cells are running

For a bicarbonate system held open to a fixed CO₂ tension — which is exactly what an incubator provides — the buffer capacity is proportional to the bicarbonate that is left:

eq. 2.2.4 $$\beta \;=\; 2.303\,[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]$$

Every millimole of lactic acid the cells make consumes a millimole of bicarbonate. So the buffer does not just get used; it gets weaker as it is used, and the last few millimoles buy almost nothing. The curve is flat, then it is a cliff.

This is the answer to "it looked fine yesterday". It was fine yesterday. A culture consuming acid-equivalents at a constant rate holds pH almost perfectly until about three quarters of the bicarbonate is gone, and then falls through two-tenths of a pH unit in a few hours. The colour change you notice is not the beginning of the problem; it is the end of the buffer.

2.3 · Friday 5 pm to Monday 9 am

The actual problem: it is Friday evening, you have a T-25 to seed, and you want to walk in on Monday to a healthy flask at 70–90% confluence, ready to passage. Sixty-four hours, three knobs, and no one in the building.

Everything on this timeline is something you measured in Tabs 1–3. Growth fills the flask, glucose is consumed, lactate is made, lactate eats bicarbonate, eq. 2.2.3 turns what is left into a pH, and phenol red turns the pH into a colour you would see through the door.

  1. Start on what most people do: 25,000 cells/cm² in 5 mL. Scrub the clock to Monday 9 am. What colour is it, and what is the pH?
  2. Find the hour the medium first goes visibly orange. Look at the glucose reading at that moment — is the flask starving?
  3. Now cut the seeding density in half and re-check Monday. Did it fix the pH? Did it cost you anything?
  4. Put the density back and raise the volume to 10 mL — the advice everybody gives. The pH is fixed. Now read the bill under the flask, and the orange trace on the chart.
  5. Push the volume to 14 mL. The pH gets better and better. Name the two things that are getting worse, and say which of them the phenol red would tell you about.
  6. Find a combination that lands on Monday between 70% and 90% confluent, pH above 7.1, and oxygen at the cells above 70%. Then try to find one that is deeper than 3.5 mm.

The three equations behind the timeline

Cells fill a finite flask, so they grow fast while there is room and slow as they run out of it — logistic growth, with carrying capacity K set by the surface area:

eq. 2.4.1 $$N(t) \;=\; \frac{K}{1 + \left(\dfrac{K}{N_0}-1\right)e^{-\mu t}}$$

Every cell burns glucose and — because cultured mammalian cells run glycolysis hard even with oxygen present — most of that carbon leaves as lactate. Each millimole of lactic acid consumes a millimole of bicarbonate:

eq. 2.4.2 $$[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}](t) \;=\; [\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]_0 \;-\; Y_{L/G}\,\frac{\Delta n_{\rm glc}(t)}{V}$$

Then eq. 2.2.3 takes that bicarbonate, divides by the CO₂ the box is still holding at 5%, and gives the pH. Three equations, no free parameters that you get to choose except the two on the sliders: how many cells, and how much medium.

Both levers have a bill, and the bill for volume is the one nobody quotes. Static medium is a stagnant film: oxygen only reaches the monolayer by molecular diffusion through the whole depth. At steady state the flux the cells consume must equal the flux the column can deliver, which fixes the concentration at the cells and a depth beyond which it is zero:

eq. 2.2.5 $$C_{\rm cells} \;=\; C_{\rm surface}-\frac{q\,h}{D} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h_{\rm crit} \;=\; \frac{D\,C_{\rm surface}}{q}$$

And the CO₂ that sets the pH has to make the same trip in the other direction, so a change in the box takes this long to reach the cells:

eq. 2.2.6 $$t_{\rm eq} \;\approx\; \frac{h^{2}}{2D}$$

Note the square. Doubling the depth halves the oxygen at the cells and quadruples the lag on everything the incubator is trying to do for you. Neither of those turns the medium a different colour, which is exactly why the volume lever feels free when it is not.

Why a bigger flask does not help — one line of algebra
  1. Write what you know. Acid production is proportional to cell number, so per unit area it is proportional to the seeding density ρ. Buffer available is proportional to volume, so per unit area it is proportional to the depth h.
  2. Assemble. The time to spend the bicarbonate is buffer-per-area over acid-rate-per-area:
    $$t_{\rm buffer}\;\propto\;\frac{[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]_0\,h}{Y_{L/G}\,q_{\rm glc}\,\rho}$$
  3. Notice what is missing. The growth area A appears in the numerator and the denominator and cancels exactly.
Moving the same cells into a bigger flask with proportionally more medium buys you nothing: same depth, same density, same weekend. There are only two levers, they are depth and density, and the flask you chose is not one of them.
eq. 2.4.3 $$t_{\rm buffer}\;\propto\;\frac{h}{\rho}\qquad\text{— the growth area cancels}$$
So: is 10 mL the answer? No. 10 mL in a T-25 is 4 mm — twice the depth every catalogue quietly recommends, and §2.3 showed why that number is a diffusion length rather than a packaging convention. It fixes Monday's pH and it takes the oxygen at the monolayer from 84% of surface down to 68%, the CO2 lag from 18 minutes to 70, and it does both worst at the moment the flask is fullest — Monday morning, when you were going to use it. None of that changes the colour, which is precisely the problem: you have not fixed the flask, you have moved the failure somewhere the phenol red cannot report it.

What actually works is unglamorous. Sweep both sliders over their whole range and score Monday on all three criteria — 70–90% confluent, pH above 7.1, oxygen above 70% of surface. Roughly 370 combinations pass the first two. Only about 50 pass all three, and every one of them lies between 2.6 and 3.4 mm of depth. The answer is not a volume; it is a band, and it is the same band §2.3 derived from diffusion alone.

Getting into that band means spending a little of both levers rather than a lot of one — something like 32k/cm² in 7.5 mL: a touch more density to hit confluence on time, a touch more depth to hold the buffer, and neither of them pushed far enough to break something else. That is what the a little of both levers chip does, and it is worth comparing directly against the 10 mL one.

Check yourself

1. You come in Monday to a T-75 with bright yellow medium and cells still attached and looking normal under the scope. What do you do?

Answer

Change the medium immediately and look again in a few hours — cells that are still attached at low pH usually recover. What you must not do is passage them into an experiment and treat that passage as normal: they have spent an unknown number of hours acid-stressed, which changes gene expression, and you will not be able to tell later whether a result came from your treatment or from Sunday night. Note it in the log; that is what the log is for.

2. Same flask, but the medium is bright pink-purple and the cells are rounded and poorly attached. Different problem — which one?

Answer

Alkaline, not acid — so it is the CO₂ side of eq. 2.2.3, not the lactate side. Either the tank ran out, the door was left ajar, the CO₂ sensor has drifted, or the flask sat on the bench too long before going in. Check the incubator's CO₂ reading against an independent measurement before you blame the cells; if the box is fine, the flask spent time out of it.

3. Why does adding HEPES to the medium not simply solve all of this?

Answer

It helps — HEPES buffers well near pH 7.4 and does not depend on CO₂, which is exactly why it is used for work on the bench and on a microscope stage. But it does not remove the lactate, it is toxic to some lines at higher concentrations, and it is photoactivated by fluorescent light to produce hydrogen peroxide and free radicals. It buys you time out of the incubator; it does not buy you a longer weekend.

  • Medium pH is a ratio: bicarbonate over dissolved CO₂ (eq. 2.2.3).
  • The CO₂ setting belongs to the medium formulation. DMEM at 5% runs alkaline; that is not a fault, it is a mismatch.
  • Buffer capacity falls as the buffer is spent, so pH holds flat and then crashes.
  • Phenol red is a measurement. Learn to read it and you get a free daily assay on every vessel in the box.
  • And the obvious fix — more medium — is a trade: pH headroom bought with oxygen at the monolayer (eq. 2.2.5) and with a lag that grows as h2 (eq. 2.2.6).
  • There are only two levers, depth and density (eq. 2.4.3 — flask area cancels), and everything that survives all three tests sits between 2.6 and 3.4 mm of medium. 10 mL in a T-25 is not the answer.

3.1 · Take the pan out and watch what happens

The water tray in the bottom of the incubator is the part everyone is suspicious of, and the part nobody can justify removing. So do the experiment: run the same culture at different humidities and watch the medium.

You are not looking for the cells to die of thirst. You are looking at what happens to the concentration of everything dissolved in what is left.

  1. Start on pan full (93% RH — this box's own number), 35 mm dish, 3 days. Note the volume lost and the osmolality.
  2. Drop the humidity to 70% — a pan that nobody topped up. Same three days. What is the osmolality now?
  3. Switch the vessel to a 96-well edge well. Why is that the well that always looks wrong?
  4. Try the sealed flask. It barely evaporates — so why is that not the answer?

What is left in the vessel

Name it: evaporation is a gradient, and humidity is how you flatten it

Water leaves an open vessel at a rate set by how far the air above it is from saturation. Mass flux is a transfer coefficient times that gap:

eq. 2.3.1 $$J \;=\; h_m\,\rho_{\rm sat}\,(1-\mathrm{RH}),\qquad h_m \;\approx\; \frac{D_{\rm H_2O,air}}{\delta}$$

δ is the depth of still air the vapour has to cross — the lid gap, the headspace of a flask, the shoulder of a well. That is why vessel geometry matters as much as humidity, and why the outer wells of a plate lose water several times faster than the middle ones.

The cells do not experience the missing water. They experience what is left behind, at a higher concentration:

eq. 2.3.2 $$\mathrm{Osm}(t) \;=\; \mathrm{Osm}_0\,\frac{V_0}{V_0 - J A t}$$

Fresh medium is 300–320 mOsm/kg. Mammalian cells are noticeably stressed above about 380 and in real trouble past 400 — and osmolality is a ratio of volumes, so it rises faster and faster as the volume falls.

The water pan is not there to stop your dish drying out. It is there to stop your medium getting saltier. A dish that has lost a fifth of its volume still looks perfectly normal — there is liquid in it, the cells are covered, the colour is fine — and it is 400 mOsm.

The same plate is not the same experiment in every well

Everything above treated a vessel as one number. A 96-well plate is ninety-six vessels under one lid, and they do not share a fate. Water leaving a well has to do two things in series: get out of the well, and then travel sideways under the lid to the edge of the plate. So eq. 2.3.1's δ is not a property of the plate — it is a property of where the well is on it:

And now the ceiling: why nobody runs the box wetter

The obvious response to all of this is to turn the humidity up. The box will not let you, and the reason is worth knowing, because it is the same reason your plate lids fog.

Air at 37 °C and a given RH has a dew point — the temperature at which it is saturated. Any surface colder than that grows water on it:

eq. 2.3.3 $$T_{\rm dew} \;=\; \frac{243.04\,\gamma}{17.625-\gamma},\qquad \gamma \;=\; \ln \mathrm{RH} + \frac{17.625\,T}{243.04+T}$$

At 37 °C that gives a margin — how cold a surface is allowed to be before it condenses — of 1.9 °C at 90%, 1.3 °C at 93%, 0.9 °C at 95% and 0.4 °C at 98%. The margin is what you are spending when you turn the humidity up, and it runs out fast.

Your incubator spends it in two places. The first is itself, and it has been engineered around: the walls, the ceiling, the door frame and the outer door are all separately heated precisely so the chamber stays above its own dew point, and the box holds its interior to ≤ ± 0.3 °C spatially. At 93% it clears that comfortably — which is why you can still see through the glass.

The second place is your plate, and nothing is heating that. A plate carried in from the cabinet at 22 °C is fifteen degrees below the dew point. It fogs immediately and keeps fogging until its own thermal mass catches up — about 27 minutes at 93% — and the water that condenses on the underside of the lid drips back into wells that are not necessarily the ones it came from. This is not a hypothetical: the manufacturer ships a Low Humidity setting that drops the box from 93% to 90%, and its stated purpose is exactly this — "if condensation occurs on the culture containers due to high relative humidity."

Notice the shape of that trade. Going from 93% to 90% buys back roughly four minutes of fogging and costs you a permanently faster-drying plate — and the manual is explicit that it has to be a permanent setting, not something you flip for an afternoon. There is no free humidity. 93% is not a biological optimum; it is the point where evaporation and condensation cost about the same.

4.1 · The door is an inlet

Both of these are equations you already have

The swing. A door panel is a piston, and the side view is where you can see why: the rectangle it sweeps has to be filled by something, and the only air available is the room's. If the panel face moves faster than the air can get out of its way, it pushes a slug of that room air ahead of it into the chamber. That is the disturbance ratio from Lesson 1, unchanged —

$$\Pi \;=\; \frac{v_{\rm door}}{v_{\rm exchange}} \qquad\text{(eq. 1.3.2, Lesson 1)}$$

— with the incubator's own exchange velocity underneath instead of the cabinet's air curtain. Same ratio, same threshold at Π = 1, same rule: below 1 the air keeps up with you and nothing is entrained; above 1 it cannot, and you carry a volume in with you. A door is heavier and larger than a hand, so it is easy to exceed without noticing.

The swing is also a dwell. This is the term missing from every version of this advice, including the one this section used to give. The aperture is not shut and then suddenly open — it is open the whole time it is opening, and the buoyancy exchange runs through it from the first crack. Take the open fraction as sin θ, sweep θ from 0 to 90° in the time the panel needs to travel its own width, and the integral is clean:

$$t_{\rm eff} \;=\; \frac{2}{\pi}\,\frac{W}{v_{\rm door}} \qquad\text{— the swing is worth this many seconds of a fully open door}$$

A 0.9 m panel opened at a gentle 5 cm/s takes eighteen seconds, which is worth eleven and a half seconds of standing there with it wide open — before you have reached in. At 130 cm/s it is worth 0.4 s.

So "open it slowly" is wrong, and the people who fling doors are responding to something real. The two penalties pull in opposite directions — the piston grows with speed, the opening exchange grows as you slow down — so there is a best door speed rather than a limit — and on this box it is not a slow one.

The dwell. Once the door is open the box is a warm cavity connected to a cool room. Warm air leaves across the top of the opening, room air enters along the bottom, and the chamber composition approaches the room's on the same first-order law as everything else in this lesson:

$$V_{\rm exchanged} \;=\; V_{\rm box}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau_{x}}\right) \qquad\text{(eq. 1.5.1, again)}$$

Which explains the shape you just watched. The exchange saturates: the first twenty seconds are most of it, and after about a minute the box is simply full of room air and further waiting adds nothing. That is not reassuring — it means the damage is done early, by the part of the opening everybody thinks of as free.

Which leaves two instructions, and only one of them is "quick". Match the swing — about three seconds for the panel, because faster and slower both cost you. 5.1 · The problem is the trip, and there is more than one way to fix it

A culture cannot stay in the incubator. Every few days it has to be fed, split or looked at, and for those minutes it is in a cabinet at 22 °C and 0.04% CO2 under 0.32 m/s of downflow. That is three separate problems, and different fixes solve different ones:

  1. The dish is off 37 °C for minutes. The big one, and purely thermal.
  2. It is off 5% CO2 for the same minutes. Smaller, slower, and not fixed by any of the hardware below.
  3. The chamber is disturbed by everyone's openings and has to climb back each time.

Get the scale right before ranking anything. Somebody else opening the glass door moves a 35 mm dish by about 0.1 °C, because the medium is an 18-minute low-pass filter (eq. 2.2.6) and the disturbance is eight seconds long. Five minutes on a cold work surface moves the same dish by eight. Problem 1 is two orders of magnitude bigger than problem 3.

5.2 · What a shelf full of metal does to the whole box

§5.1 left one box unable to justify itself. Judged on the dish inside it, it is worth nothing measurable; judged on the chamber it is worth a re-warm τ of 5.3 s against 5.9, which is real and far too small on its own. One box is the wrong unit. The plan is the top two shelves converted to drawers for long-term maintenance, the bottom two left open for ongoing experiments, so the chamber holds two populations and every number below is reported twice. This section also adds the one quantity the lesson has never carried: every door opening is paid for in CO2 from a cylinder.

Less air comes in, because there is less room for it

The chamber is 165 L and each drawer takes 5.9 L of that out of circulation. Two converted shelves — four drawers, which is what fits a 423 × 465 mm insert — leave 141 L freely exchanging; all four leave 117 L. What "behind a filter" is worth is eq. 2.3.5: a membrane disc passes gas far faster than a lid gap of the same open area, and still slowly enough that eight seconds of open door goes past unnoticed.

eq. 2.3.5 $$\tau_{\rm gas} \;=\; \frac{V\,L}{D_{\rm eff}\,A},\qquad D_{\rm eff}=D\,\frac{\varphi}{\vartheta}$$

The exchange itself is buoyancy — warm air out of the top of the doorway, room air in at the bottom — and that flow needs somewhere to circulate to. The same metal that displaces the air also blocks the counter-flow, so the rate falls with the free volume and the time constant does not move:

$$Q \;\propto\; V_{\rm free} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tau_x \;=\; \frac{V_{\rm free}}{Q} \;=\; \text{20 s, whatever is on the shelves}$$

So a full box turns over the same fraction per opening and a smaller volume. The dip on an open shelf goes 0.88% → 0.87 → 0.84 as shelves convert — flat, marginally better; a culture in a drawer sees 0.80. What changes properly is the quantity of gas.

And the bill nobody counts

Whatever CO2 went out of the door has to be put back from a cylinder, and it is the free volume times the shortfall: about 2.1 L per opening at an eight-second dwell. Four openings a visit, a visit every four hours, and that is 50 L a day or 2.8 kg a month — roughly eight months on a 50 lb (22.7 kg) cylinder, which is why the one in the corner does not seem to need changing often. Drawers help for the same reason they flatten the dip: 58 L a day with none, 50 at the plan, 42 fully converted.

But the sensitivity to dwell dwarfs everything else on this page. Leave the shelves alone and change only how long the door is held: eight seconds is 2.1 L an opening and eight months; thirty seconds is 4.5 L and under four. Add traffic — two openings a visit at four seconds, every eight hours — and it falls to 7 L a day and over four years. Nothing about the incubator changed. That is §4.1 and §1.2 with a quantity attached: open it deliberately, one trip instead of three, and a drawer per person so nobody is hunting with the door open.

The metal is the part that helps everybody

The chamber's air has almost no heat capacity — 140 J/K — so what matters is not the metal as a battery but as warm surface. Each drawer adds 0.22 m² of 37 °C aluminium for the incoming air to be re-warmed against while shrinking the pocket that has to be re-warmed. Four bare shelves are 3.4 m² against 165 L, and eq. 2.5.2 gives a re-warm τ of 5.9 s; two converted shelves make it 4.2 m² against 141 L and 4.0 s; all four, 2.8. The chamber's excursion falls from 2.82 °C to 2.28 to 1.76, and the mass behind that surface from 1.0 kJ/K to 21.9, which holds the setpoint between visits rather than during them. Watch the zoomed panel: the whole event is over in half a minute and neither culture trace moves.

And that improvement belongs to everybody. The metal does not know whose culture it is protecting: a shelf full of drawers makes the chamber calmer and the cylinder last longer for every flask in the box, including the ones belonging to whoever never made a drawer. So the plan is a good split, and now for a simple reason — every number improves, and the drawers add a small extra shield for whatever is behind a filter. The open shelves hold the cultures you were going to open the box for anyway; the drawers hold the long-term stocks nobody should be touching. The split is by need. Split it by who got round to building a drawer and it is the same arithmetic with none of the justification.

6.1 · Next lesson's headline, measured today

Both lessons so far have assumed one thing without ever saying it: that when you set out to move 5 mL of medium, 5 mL of medium moved. Test that.

The pipettor in your hand does not touch the liquid. It moves a column of air, and the liquid follows. Every property of that air column — its length, its temperature, its pressure — is between you and the volume you think you delivered.

  1. Set the target volume to 100 µL and the pipettor to its 100 µL range. Read the error.
  2. Now dispense the same 100 µL from a 1000 µL pipettor. What did the bigger air cushion cost you?
  3. Warm the pipettor in your hand (raise the body temperature). Which way does the volume go, and why?
  4. Turn pre-wetting on. How much of the error was just the tip being dry?

An assumption from this lesson that expires

Everything in Tabs 1–4 treated the medium as a well-mixed pool with one pH, one osmolality and one glucose concentration. Over hours, in 5 mL, that is fair.
