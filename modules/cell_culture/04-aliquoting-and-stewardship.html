1.1 · Flip it two hundred times and count

No equation yet. Two hundred identical bottles, each opened N times, each entry an independent coin flip with the same small probability of letting something in. Run it and count how many bottles are still clean at the end.

Then run it again with a different seed and see how much the answer moves.

  1. Set the risk per entry to 0.5% and the entries to 40. Run it. How many of the 200 bottles survived?
  2. Re-run with a new seed a few times. Is the answer stable to within a few percent?
  3. Now push the entries to 120 without touching the risk. What fraction survives?
  4. Predict the answer for 0.25% and 80 entries before you set it. Then check.

Now the equation that explains what you counted

A bottle survives an entry with probability (1 − p). Entries are independent, so it survives all N of them with probability (1 − p)N, and therefore:

eq. 4.1.1 again — previewed in Lesson 3, earned here $$P(\text{contaminated}) \;=\; 1-(1-p)^{N}$$

The sim drew the black line from that expression after counting the bottles, and they agree. Now read what the equation says, because it is not what most people expect:

  • N is in the exponent and p is not. For small p, P ≈ Np — so halving your risk per entry and halving the number of entries buy you the same thing.
  • But you cannot halve p. Lessons 1 and 3 were entirely about getting p down, and you have now done that work. There is a floor, and it is set by physics and by being a human being.
  • N you can change this afternoon. Nothing about your technique has to improve.
This is the whole argument for aliquoting, and it has nothing to do with being tidy. You are not reducing the chance of a mistake. You are reducing the number of opportunities, and — as the next tab shows — the amount that each one can destroy.
Worked example — your medium bottle, honestly counted
  1. Write what you know. A 500 mL bottle of medium. You feed six flasks twice a week using ~10 mL a time, so it lasts about four weeks and is opened roughly 40 times. Suppose your technique is good: p = 0.5% per entry.
  2. Assemble the equation.
    $$P \;=\; 1-(1-p)^{N}$$
  3. Plug in.
    $$P \;=\; 1-(1-0.005)^{40} \;=\; 1-(0.995)^{40} \;=\; 1-0.818$$
  4. Read it. P = 0.182.
About 18% — roughly one bottle in five gets contaminated before it is used up, with technique most people would call good. That is not a statement about you. It is a statement about doing anything forty times.
Check yourself

1. Two people share a bottle and each is individually excellent, with p = 0.5%. Does sharing change the per-entry risk?

Answer

No — but it doubles N, which is the term in the exponent. Shared stocks are not riskier per entry; they are riskier because they get opened twice as often, and because when one goes down it takes two people's work with it. That is an argument for aliquoting to the person, not for distrusting your colleagues.

2. Why is "I have never contaminated anything" not evidence that your p is zero?

Answer

With p = 0.2% you would get through about 350 entries before an even chance of one event — most of a year. The absence of a visible failure over a few months is entirely consistent with a p that will cost you a bottle a year, and mycoplasma at 10⁷–10⁹ organisms/mL is invisible anyway. This is why the lab tests rather than trusts.

  • P = 1 − (1 − p)N, and for small p it is roughly Np.
  • Good technique with 40 entries still loses about one bottle in five.
  • You have already spent Lessons 1 and 3 on p. N is the lever you have left.
Next: probability is only half of it. The other half is how much you lose when it happens.

2.1 · Split the same bottle and watch the loss fall

Same bottle, same technique, same number of entries. The only thing that changes is how many containers the liquid is sitting in. Drag the number of aliquots and watch the expected loss.

Then find the point where it stops improving, and work out what is holding it up.

  1. Start at 1 aliquot — that is one bottle. Read the expected loss in mL.
  2. Go to 20. How much better is it, as a ratio?
  3. Keep going to 100. The curve flattens. What is the number it flattens onto?
  4. Set the master-transfer risk to zero. Where does the floor go? What does that tell you about which single operation matters most?

Expected loss vs how many containers you used

Name it: probability times blast radius

What a contamination event costs you is not "a bottle". It is however much material that vessel was holding:

eq. 4.1.2 $$E[\text{loss}] \;=\; P \times V_{\rm at\ risk}$$

Split the stock into k aliquots and three things happen at once. Each aliquot is entered fewer times (N/k), so its own P falls. Each aliquot holds less (V/k), so its blast radius falls. But making the aliquots is itself an operation, with one entry into the master and k fills:

eq. 4.2.1 $$E[\text{loss}] \;=\; V\Big[\underbrace{p_{\rm master}}_{\text{loses everything}} \;+\; \underbrace{p_{\rm fill}}_{\text{loses one aliquot, }k\text{ times}} \;+\; \underbrace{1-(1-p)^{N/k}}_{\text{use}}\Big]$$

The first two terms do not fall with k. So the curve improves steeply and then flattens onto a floor that is set entirely by the transfer that made the aliquots.

Aliquoting concentrates all the risk you did not eliminate into one moment. The master transfer is the highest-stakes thing you do with that reagent all month: one lapse there and every aliquot is contaminated identically, and you will not find out for weeks. That is not an argument against aliquoting — the floor is still ten to twenty times better than one bottle. It is an argument for doing that one operation with a fresh cabinet, full attention, and a sterility check afterwards.
  • Loss = probability × blast radius, and aliquoting shrinks both.
  • Splitting a bottle 20 ways cuts the expected loss by roughly an order of magnitude — with no change in technique.
  • The improvement floors on the master transfer. Do that one operation properly and check it.
Next: so split it into a hundred. Except that a second clock has been running the whole time, and it points the other way.

3.1 · Nothing in that bottle is waiting for you

The reagent has been degrading since the day it was made. An aliquot that sits at 4 °C for three months is not the reagent you thought you were using, and the experiment that stops working in week ten was not a biological result.

Watch what happens to the activity when the same stock is used up over the same twelve weeks, split different ways.

  1. Start on L-glutamine in medium, 1 aliquot. Read the mean activity across the twelve weeks.
  2. Now 20 aliquots. Same total time, same total reagent. Why did the mean activity go up?
  3. Switch to a labile growth factor. How bad is one bottle now?
  4. Turn on freeze–thaw and put the aliquot count back to 4. What did partial use cost?

Controls

Activity of what you actually pipetted

Chemical decomposition of a single species is first order, so the activity of an opened aliquot falls exponentially from the moment it is thawed:

eq. 4.3.1 $$A(t) \;=\; A_0\,e^{-t\ln 2 / t_{1/2}}$$

What matters is not the activity at the end — it is the average activity across everything you did with that aliquot, because each experiment got whatever was there on the day:

eq. 4.3.2 $$\bar{A} \;=\; \frac{1}{t_{\rm use}}\int_0^{t_{\rm use}}\! A(t)\,dt \;=\; \frac{t_{1/2}}{t_{\rm use}\ln 2}\left(1-e^{-t_{\rm use}\ln 2/t_{1/2}}\right)$$

With k aliquots, each one is open for tuse = shelf/k. More aliquots means each is open briefly, so the mean activity rises toward 100%. Decay, like contamination, argues for splitting.

Honesty about these numbers The half-lives on the chips are order-of-magnitude. Real stability depends on formulation, pH, light exposure and how many times the tube was warmed. Treat them as a way to see the shape of the problem, and get the real figure from the certificate of analysis for the lot you have. The one thing that is not order-of-magnitude is the direction: everything degrades, and nothing gets better in the fridge.

Freeze–thaw is the other term, and it is the one people create for themselves: thaw a tube, use half, refreeze it, and every cycle costs a fixed fraction. An aliquot sized so it is used once has no freeze–thaw term at all — which is the real argument for single-use aliquots, quite separate from contamination.

3.2 · Both clocks say "more aliquots". So find the bottom.

Contamination says split it. Decay says split it. If nothing pushed back, the answer would be "one aliquot per use, forever", and for a bottle of medium that is very nearly right.

It stops being right for small, precious stocks — and the thing that stops it is a film you already measured in Lesson 3.

  1. Start on 500 mL medium. Find the bottom of the curve. Is it sharp or flat?
  2. Switch to 1 mL antibody. Find the bottom again. What happens past it?
  3. Turn the unrecoverable film to zero. Where does the minimum go?
  4. State the rule for yourself: when does "smaller aliquots" stop being free?

Total cost vs number of aliquots

Add up everything the choice of k costs you, as a fraction of the stock:

eq. 4.4.1 $$C(k) \;=\; \underbrace{p_m + p_f + 1-(1-p)^{N/k}}_{\text{contamination}} \;+\; \underbrace{1-\bar{A}(k)}_{\text{decay}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{k\,V_{\rm film}}{V}}_{\text{film}} \;+\; \underbrace{h\,k}_{\text{handling}}$$

The first two terms fall with k. The last two rise with it. The film term is eq. 3.2.1 again — the liquid you cannot get back out of a tube or off a pipette wall — and it is a fixed volume per tube, so as a fraction it grows as the tubes get smaller.

For a 500 mL bottle this curve's minimum is broad and lives somewhere around forty tubes of ten millilitres. For a 1 mL vial of antibody, the curve turns back up, and splitting it a hundred ways throws away more in unrecoverable film than contamination would ever have cost you. Same equation, opposite advice, and the thing that flipped it is the ratio of the film to the aliquot. The bottom is flat for the bottle because the only rising term in this equation is negligible at that size — and two more are coming.
  • Decay argues for splitting, exactly as contamination does — for a different reason.
  • An aliquot sized for one use has no freeze–thaw term at all.
  • What limits splitting is the unrecoverable film per tube, so it only bites on small, precious stocks.
  • Small precious stock: the film is the floor. Big cheap stock: the floor is somewhere else, and §3.3 and §4.1 are where it is.
Next: this curve still does not know two things that decide the answer at the bench — how much you actually use in one go, and what the tubes cost. Neither is anywhere in eq. 4.4.1, and each one moves the bottom.

3.3 · The tube has to fit the job

Nothing in eq. 4.4.1 knows what you do on a Tuesday. It counts entries and weeks. It does not know that a T-75 takes about ten millilitres of medium, that you feed four flasks at a sitting, or that you cannot use half a tube and put the half back without undoing the thing you split it for.

So ask the question the curve cannot. How much do you use in one go? Call it u — one media change, one flask trypsinised, one plate dosed. An aliquot of size a then forces you to open

eq. 4.4.2 $$m \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{u}{a} \right\rceil \ \text{tubes per session}, \qquad \eta \;=\; \frac{u}{m\,a}$$

That ceiling bracket is the whole section. u/a is how many tubes the job needs; m is how many you actually open, because there is no such thing as opening two-thirds of a tube. η is the fraction of what you took out of the freezer that reaches a cell — the rest is in the bin, or it is back in the fridge in a tube that is now open.

Set the aliquot size against your session and watch both.

  1. Start on 500 mL medium with the aliquot at 1.00 × a session. Read η, and count the tubes in the picture.
  2. Now drag the aliquot to 0.95 × a session — a 5% change. What happened to η, and why is it so violent?
  3. Drag it up to 2.0 × a session instead. η is bad again, for the opposite reason. Say both reasons out loud.
  4. Find every setting where η = 100%. What do they have in common?
  5. Turn off strict single use. η goes to 100% everywhere — read the verdict for what you just paid for it.

One session, tube by tube
What reaches a cell

The curve is a comb, and it only touches 100% at a = u, u/2, u/3, … — the sizes that divide a session exactly. Everywhere else you are paying the rounding. The teeth are brutally asymmetric: an aliquot slightly smaller than a session forces a second tube that you then barely touch, so η falls off a cliff to about 1⁄2, while an aliquot slightly larger than a session costs you only the few percent you did not use.

Round up, never down. If your session is 10 mL and you are choosing between 9 mL and 11 mL tubes, the 9 mL tube costs you 45% of the bottle and doubles the number of things you open in the cabinet; the 11 mL tube costs you 9%. The failure is not symmetric, so neither should your rounding be.

The other end of the same argument is the one people actually break. Making the aliquot much bigger than a session does not make η bad — it makes you stop believing in single use. You put the rest back, and the moment you do, the tube is entered a second time (eq. 4.1.1), it sits at 4 °C for another session's worth of decay (eq. 4.3.2), and if it was frozen you have just bought a freeze–thaw cycle. That is not a small correction; it is the entire reason the lesson said "size it for one session" in the first place.

McGhee Lab practice Pick the aliquot from the job, then the tube from the aliquot — in that order. Write the session volume on the protocol first ("one media change = 10 mL"), then choose the aliquot as that number or a clean half of it, and only then go and see what tube it fits in. Choosing the tube first is how people end up with 15 mL conicals holding 12 mL for a 10 mL job, binning a fifth of every bottle and never noticing.
Check yourself

1. Your session is 10 mL and you have 15 mL conicals. Somebody suggests filling them to 12 mL "so they are not half empty". What does that cost, under strict single use?

Answer

u/a = 10/12 = 0.83, so m = 1 and η = 10/12 = 83%. You bin 2 mL every session and get 41 sessions out of the 500 mL bottle instead of 50. Fill them to 10 mL and leave them looking half empty — the tube is a container, not a target.

2. Why does m = 3 hurt more than the arithmetic in eq. 4.4.2 says it does?

Answer

Because eq. 4.4.2 only counts volume. Three tubes per session is also three thaws, three caps off and on, three entries into the cabinet, and three chances of the reach-over from L3 §3.4 — all inside one sitting. The probability terms in eq. 4.1.1 scale with the number of things you open, and your attention does not.

3. η = 100% at a = u/4. Is that as good as a = u?

Answer

Not at the bench. Nothing is wasted, so eq. 4.4.2 is happy — but you now open four tubes for one media change, pay the film of eq. 3.2.1 four times, and buy four times the plastic. It is on a tooth of the comb, and it is still the wrong tooth. Which is exactly what §4.1 is about.

  • The aliquot is chosen against u, the volume of one session — not against the bottle.
  • m = ⌈u/a⌉: you always round up, and you always pay for the rounding.
  • Just under a session is the worst place to be. Round up.
  • "Put the rest back" is not free — it re-buys every term aliquoting removed.
Next tab: the second thing eq. 4.4.1 does not know. Every one of those tubes is a thing somebody had to buy — and so is the experiment you lose when one of them goes down.

4.1 · The tubes cost money, and so does the experiment

A 500 mL bottle has arrived. Before the tube rack comes out there is one question left, and it is the one the last three sections have been quietly dodging.

Everything so far has been priced in reagent — every term of eq. 4.4.1 is a fraction of the stock. That quietly assumes the only thing at stake is the stuff in the bottle. Two things at the bench are not made of reagent.

The first is the plastic. A hundred and twenty aliquots means a hundred and twenty sterile tubes, and they are not free:

AliquotWhat it goes inRoughly
≤ 0.5 mL1.5 mL sterile microtube$0.09
0.5 – 2 mL2 mL cryovial$0.45
2 – 15 mL15 mL conical$0.35
15 – 50 mL50 mL conical$0.55
> 50 mLsterile media bottle$3.00

Read that table twice. It does not go down as the tube gets smaller — a 2 mL cryovial costs more than a 15 mL conical, because you are paying for a sealed cap rated for the freezer, not for polypropylene. Splitting finer can buy you more expensive plastic as well as more of it.

Compare a tube to what is in it. If the stock cost P for volume V, one aliquot holds aP/V of reagent, and

eq. 4.4.3 $$\rho \;=\; \frac{c_{\rm tube}}{a\,P/V} \;=\; \frac{c_{\rm tube}}{\text{price per mL} \times a}$$

ρ is the plastic-to-contents ratio, and it is the number that decides whether this section matters to you at all. At ρ ≈ 0.01 the tubes are noise. At ρ ≈ 1 the tube costs as much as what you put in it.

The second non-reagent term is the one that actually dominates: what a contamination event costs you. Eq. 4.1.2 priced the blast radius in millilitres, because that is what the bottle holds. But losing a bottle of medium is never the bill. The bill is the cells, the plates, the fortnight, and the incubator you now have to strip. Put a number on it — your number — and the whole thing lands in one currency:

eq. 4.4.4 $$\$(k) \;=\; \underbrace{C_{\rm event}\,P_{\rm contam}(k)}_{\text{what it costs you when it goes}} \;+\; \underbrace{P\big[(1-\eta) + (1-\bar{A}) + kV_{\rm film}/V\big]}_{\text{reagent you lose}} \;+\; \underbrace{k\,c_{\rm tube}(V/k)}_{\text{plastic you buy}}$$

Three prices under the tube count, and one of them moves the answer more than the other two together. Find out which.

  1. On 500 mL medium, read the cheapest split. Now do what a budget spreadsheet does: untick strict single use and drag "what a contamination costs you" to zero. Where does the answer go, and does it look like a lab you would want to work in?
  2. Put strict single use back on but leave the contamination at zero. The answer snaps back. Which term caught you — and why is that a different argument for the same tube?
  3. Put it back to $2,000. Read the plastic bill as a percentage of the reagent.
  4. Set the plastic price to zero. How much does the answer actually move? Now you know whether tubes were ever the problem here.
  5. Switch to 1 mL antibody and read ρ. Compare it with the ρ you had on medium.
  6. Find the green markers on the axis — those are the splits §3.3 allows, one tube per session, two per session, and so on. Is the cheapest k sitting on one?

Honesty about these prices The prices are order-of-magnitude US academic list, and they move. They are sliders precisely so you can put your own in — your medium may be $25 or $140 depending on the serum, and the lab's tube prices depend on what was on the standing order that quarter. Worked example — 500 mL of complete medium, priced three ways
  1. Write what you know. V = 500 mL, P = $70 (DMEM plus 10% FBS), one media change u = 10 mL, so the stock owes you N = 50 sessions. Half-life at 4 °C ≈ 8 weeks, shelf 12 weeks, p = 0.5% per entry.

    $$\$ = \underbrace{2000(0.0065+0.005)}_{\$23} \;+\; \underbrace{70\times0.0107}_{\$0.75} \;+\; \underbrace{50\times0.35}_{\$17.50} \;=\; \$41$$
  5. Check the neighbour. k = 100 doubles the plastic to $35 and saves nothing at all, because the contamination term was already on its floor at k = 50. The bill goes up to $58.
50 × 10 mL in 15 mL conicals — one tube, one media change. Which is where §3.2 landed before any of this. The money did not change the answer; it changed the reason, and it showed that the cheap-looking answer — ten big tubes — is only cheap if a contamination costs you nothing. Note also what the arithmetic says about ρ: at a = 10 mL, ρ = 0.35/(0.14 × 10) = 0.25, so a quarter of what you are spending is the tube. That is a real cost, and it is still the right call.
Film limits small precious stocks. Plastic limits big cheap ones. Your session volume limits both. Three different floors, three different mechanisms, and which one you hit depends entirely on the reagent in front of you. A 1 mL antibody split into 20 µL aliquots has ρ ≈ 0.013 — the tubes are free, and what decides the answer is the film of eq. 3.2.1, which by then is 15% of the vial. A 500 mL bottle of medium at the sensible split has ρ ≈ 0.25 — a quarter of the spend is plastic — and the film never gets a look in at all.

The five things on every label

A label is not for you today. It is for someone else in eight months, standing at an open freezer, deciding whether the tube in their hand is usable. Five fields, and every one of them answers a question that gets asked:

What it isThe full name and the concentration — not "GLN" and not a colour of tape. Acronyms only if everyone in the lab has agreed on them.
Lot / sourceWhich bottle it came from. When a lot turns out to be bad, this is the only field that lets you find every tube that came from it.
Date aliquotedNot the expiry — the date it was split. Expiry is derivable from that; the reverse is not.
VolumeSo nobody has to open it to find out. Every opening is an entry, and eq. 4.1.1 counts it.
InitialsNot for blame — so the one person who knows what happened that day can be asked. Published practice goes further: a colour per worker, for tape and pen.

The sterility check that costs almost nothing

A batch of aliquots is exactly the situation where a check is worth running, because a single master-transfer failure has been copied into every tube. The published method uses material you were going to throw away:

  • Take outdated, antibiotic-free medium with 3–5% serum — it is a perfectly good microbiological broth.
  • Put 10 mL in a screw-cap tube and inoculate it from the batch.
  • Incubate at 30 °C and 37 °C — two temperatures, because one misses organisms the other catches — for at least two weeks.
  • Turbidity means the batch is contaminated — and because every tube came from one master transfer, it means every tube is. No turbidity is not a guarantee — it is evidence, and mycoplasma will pass it silently.
McGhee Lab practice — agree the details with Alex Two things the method does not say: where the tubes go, and what happens when one turns turbid.

Not in the cell-culture incubator. A capped broth needs no CO₂, and nothing you are deliberately trying to grow belongs in the same box as your cells. Use a plain 30 °C and 37 °C incubator or oven.

And a failed batch is discarded. Every tube of it, through the lab's biohazardous liquid-waste route, and decontaminate whatever it stood in. Twenty minutes of splitting is the cheap part; the fortnight you would lose using it is not.

McGhee Lab practice — agree the details with Alex Note from Dr. McGhee. Do the master transfer at the start of a session in a cabinet nobody else has been using, with nothing else in it, and do not do it at the end of a long day. Then write the date on the log for whichever incubator you used and put the check tubes somewhere they will not be thrown out. The two weeks are the point: a check you read on Friday because you are impatient has told you almost nothing.
Worked example — Matrigel, where every lesson in this track shows up at once
  1. Write what you know. 10 mL of Matrigel, thawed overnight at 4 °C, sets to a gel once it warms into the 22–35 °C range and does not usefully come back, used ~250 µL at a time, and it is the most viscous thing in the lab. Everything about handling it is a margin against that transition: keep it below about 10 °C and you are nowhere near it.
  2. Contamination (eq. 4.1.1). 40 uses from one vial at p = 0.5% gives P = 18%, and losing it means losing the vial and the week. Split it.
  3. Film (eq. 3.2.1). At 100 mPa·s, a fast withdrawal leaves a film two orders of magnitude thicker than medium would — hundreds of microlitres on the pipette wall.
    $$t_{\rm film}\approx 0.94\,r\,\mathrm{Ca}^{2/3},\qquad \mathrm{Ca}=\frac{\mu U}{\gamma}$$
    So: pre-chill the tips, work slowly, and accept that the film sets a floor on how small an aliquot is worth making.
  4. Temperature. Everything — vial, tips, tubes, the pipette — goes on ice or in a cold block. Warm plastic gels it on contact and you lose the aliquot and the tip together.
Roughly 40 × 250 µL single-use aliquots, made on ice with pre-chilled tips, slowly, in one sitting, labelled with lot and date, and stored at −20 °C. Every one of those decisions came from a different lesson in this track — and the reason Matrigel has its own protocol is that it is the reagent where getting any one of them wrong is immediately visible.
  • Size the aliquot for one session, so the tube is opened once and never refrozen.
  • Five fields on every label: what, lot, date split, volume, initials.
  • The master transfer is the one operation worth a sterility check — 10 mL of outdated medium, 30 and 37 °C, two weeks, in a plain incubator and never the cell-culture box.
  • A clean check is evidence, not a guarantee. Mycoplasma passes it silently.
  • A batch that goes turbid is discarded, every tube of it, through the biohazard waste route.
