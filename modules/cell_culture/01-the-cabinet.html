1.1 · Watch something land

Before any theory: put a particle in still air and watch it come down. The slider sets its diameter. Everything else — the density of water, the viscosity of air, gravity — is fixed, because those are not things you get to choose at the bench.

You are looking for one number: how fast does it fall? Not "does it fall" — everything falls. How fast, in the units a room is measured in.

  1. Start on skin flake. Read the settling speed in the stats panel, in ft/min.
  2. Drag the diameter slider from 4 µm up to 28 µm — the size band that actually carries microbes. Watch the fall time at the bottom.
  3. Find the diameter that gives exactly 1 foot per minute. Write it down; you will meet that number again in a published document in §1.2.
  4. Now go to 1 µm. How long does that one take to cross the 40 cm from the sash to your plate? Is gravity what removes it?

Now the equation that explains the number you just measured

A particle falling in air reaches a steady speed almost immediately, because drag grows with speed until it exactly cancels weight. For a sphere small enough that the flow around it stays smooth, that drag is Stokes drag, \(F_d = 3\pi\eta d v\), and setting it equal to the buoyant weight gives the settling velocity:

eq. 1.1.1 $$v_s \;=\; \frac{(\rho_p-\rho_f)\,g\,d^{2}}{18\,\eta}$$

Every term is a lever you do not control, except one. Density enters linearly; the difference between a skin flake and a fungal spore is under 40%. Viscosity and gravity are constants of the room. Diameter enters squared — and that is the whole story. A 28 µm particle falls fifty times faster than a 4 µm one.

The squared term is why "particle size" is the only word that matters in this lesson. Doubling the diameter quadruples the settling speed. It is also why the two ends of the contamination size range behave like completely different physics: the big end is governed by gravity, the small end is governed by whatever the air is doing.

Stokes drag is only valid while the flow around the particle is smooth, which is the condition that the particle Reynolds number is far below one:

eq. 1.1.2 $$\mathrm{Re} \;=\; \frac{\rho_f\,v_s\,d}{\eta} \;\ll\; 1$$

The stats panel reports Re live. Across the entire 1–40 µm range it stays below 10−2, so eq. 1.1.1 is not an approximation we are getting away with — it is the right equation for this problem.

eq. 1.1.3 $$t_{\rm fall} \;=\; \frac{h}{v_s}$$
Worked example — where does "one foot per minute" come from?
  1. Write what you know. A published cell-culture reference states that microbe-carrying particles are "generally 4 to 28 µm in diameter and settle at a rate of approximately one foot per minute in still air." We want to check that, and find the diameter it corresponds to. 1 ft/min = 0.3048 m / 60 s = 5.08 × 10−3 m/s.
  2. Assemble the equation. Solve eq. 1.1.1 for diameter — symbolically first, no numbers yet:
    $$d \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{18\,\eta\,v_s}{(\rho_p-\rho_f)\,g}}$$
  3. Plug in. η = 1.81 × 10−5 Pa·s, ρp − ρf = 998.8 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s²:
    $$d=\sqrt{\frac{18(1.81\times10^{-5})(5.08\times10^{-3})}{(998.8)(9.81)}}=\sqrt{1.69\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m^2}}$$
  4. Read the answer. d = 1.30 × 10−5 m = 13 µm.
13 µm — dead centre of the quoted 4–28 µm band. The vendor's rule of thumb is not a rule of thumb; it is eq. 1.1.1 evaluated at the middle of the size range that carries microbes. The ends of the band are not close to it, though: 4 µm falls at 0.10 ft/min and 28 µm at 4.6 ft/min, a factor of 49 apart.
  • Settling speed goes as diameter squared — the single most important scaling in this lesson.
  • A 13 µm particle falls ≈ 1 ft/min; a 4 µm one takes over half an hour to fall a metre.
  • Large particles are removed by gravity. Small particles are removed by airflow, or not at all.

1.2 · Measure your own room

You now have a deposition rate. That means you can compute — and in a real lab, measure — how much lands on an open dish. Leave a plate of growth medium open for a fixed time, incubate it, and count colonies. This is a settle plate, and it is the cheapest environmental measurement in the building.

The number of particles that land is the airborne concentration times the settling velocity times the area times the time:

eq. 1.1.4 $$N \;=\; C\,v_s\,A\,t$$

Notice what is not in that expression: any property of the plate. A settle plate does not sample air — it catches what gravity delivers. That is exactly what it should do, because gravity is what delivers contamination into your open flask too.

  1. Set activity to empty room, doors shut and expose for 30 minutes. Count the colonies.
  2. Now switch to one person working quietly, then three people, door opening. Same 30 minutes.
  3. The published claim is that a still, sealed room is "virtually free of biological contaminants." Does the sim agree? Which scenario breaks it?

2.1 · Watch the air, not the box

A biosafety cabinet looks like a box with a window. It is not a box. It is a shape made of moving air, and the metal is just what holds the shape. Turn the blower off and the cabinet becomes a shelf.

There are two views below, and you need both. The circuit is the whole loop — including the half of it that happens under the work tray and up the back wall, where you cannot see it and where most of the air is. Down the left is the sash gauge off the real frame; underneath is the volume ledger, in the manual's own cubic metres per hour. The three move together, because they are one machine.

Run it with the cabinet off first. Particles from the room and from the person drift wherever they like and land on the work surface. Now turn it on and follow one parcel all the way round.

  1. Start on blower off. Watch where room particles land. That is a bench.
  2. Switch it on. In the circuit view, pick one tracer and follow it: down the chamber, through a grille, backwards along the drain-pan duct under the tray, up the duct behind the back wall, into the plenum. Where does it go from there?
  3. Find the dashed line marked the split, and the dot where it lands on the tray. Air forward of that line leaves at the front grille; air behind it leaves at the rear. What happens to something released exactly on the dot?
  4. Drag the sash from 8 in to 10 in. Watch the orange tracers, not the numbers. Did they get faster, or did more of them appear? Now read the inflow velocity and the exhaust volume, and explain what the cabinet just did.
  5. Keep going to 14 in. Something finally changes — what, and how far past the alarm did it take?
  6. Switch to an older, fixed-blower cabinet and drag the sash the same way. Same question: faster, or more of them? Which of the two is your cabinet?
  7. Put the sash back on the mark and pull the downflow setting down to 0.15 m/s. Watch the dashed line. Where does it end up, and what does the ledger say has happened to the share that goes round again?
  8. Try door opened behind you. The room pushes back through the front. How much room air reaches the dish?

Two jobs, two streams

A Class II Type A2 cabinet — the kind in almost every tissue-culture room — does two things at once, and it is worth being clear that they are different jobs done by different air:

StreamWhere it comes fromWhat it protects
Downflow 0.32 m/s
829 m³/h on our 4 ft cabinet		The supply HEPA in the ceiling of the chamberYour culture. A slow, even descent of sterile air over the work surface, carrying anything you shed downward and away from it.
Inflow 0.53 m/s
468 m³/h at the 8 in opening		The room, drawn in through the work openingYou. Room air is pulled in at the opening and captured at the front grille. It never crosses the work surface — the certification smoke test says so in as many words: "no smoke shall billow over the solid work surface or penetrate onto it."

The two streams meet a few centimetres inside the front opening and both turn down into the front grille. That meeting line is the air curtain: a boundary you cannot see, holding room air out and aerosols in.

The downflow does not fall vertically onto the work surface and stop. It has to leave, and there are only two ways out — the front grille and the rear grille. So somewhere in the middle of the chamber there is a dividing streamline, the split: forward of it the air turns forward and leaves at the front, behind it the air turns back and leaves at the rear. The sim marks it with a dashed line, and marks the spot where it lands on the tray: that is a stagnation line, where descending air has nowhere left to go but sideways. Anything released exactly there goes both ways. This is also the reason the front strip of the work surface is not work space — air there is on its way out, and it is on its way out past whatever you put in front of it.

The split is not fixed. Both grilles open into the same plenum, so the air divides where they pull equally hard — that is, where each grille ends up with the same face velocity through its own open area. The front grille has the whole inflow to swallow as well as its share of the downflow, so it can spare less of the descending air, which is why the split sits forward of centre: on our cabinet, about 37% of the way in at the 8 in work position. Move either slider and watch it travel. Raise the sash and the opening pulls more room air in, the front grille is busier, and the split moves forward. Turn the downflow down and it moves forward too, until it jams against the front edge — which is what a minimum downflow specification exists to prevent. Containment and product protection are two different jobs, and only one of them is the inflow's.

McGhee Lab practice Note from Dr. McGhee. The two most common wrong mental models I see are "the cabinet blows sterile air at me" (it does not — it pulls room air in at the front, which is what protects you) and "the cabinet sterilises things" (it does not — it only controls where air goes; anything already dirty that you carry in stays dirty).

The half of the journey you cannot see

Everything so far has been about the chamber, which is the part with the window. Most of the air's journey happens where you cannot look at it, and if you do not know that part you will not understand why some rules exist.

Follow one parcel round, in the manual's own words. Room air is drawn in at the opening; both grilles swallow it and the descending filter air together; and from there it goes down, not up. Under the work tray is a drain-pan air duct held at negative pressure, and it runs rearward along the bottom of the cabinet. At the back it turns upward into a duct between the inner and outer back wall — the one with the removable paper catch grids at its foot, which exist because people drop tissues down the grille and a tissue in a blower is a bad afternoon. That duct carries everything to the plenum in the top of the cabinet, where two blowers and two HEPA filters divide it: most of it goes back down through the supply filter, and the rest leaves the building through the exhaust filter.

So 64% of what the grilles swallow goes round again and 36% leaves. Three things follow, and none of them are obvious from the front:

  • The rear grille is not decoration. It is where about two-thirds of the downflow leaves — everything behind the split. Block it — with a rack pushed to the back wall, a stack of plates, a bottle — and that air has to find another way out, which means it turns forward across your work instead.
  • The air under your work tray is moving, and it is moving backwards. Spill something and it goes into the drain pan, which is in the airstream. That is why the drain pan is on the cleaning list and why the manual says not to block the ventilation slots in the tray.
  • The cabinet breathes its own air. Anything aerosolised in the chamber is drawn through a HEPA filter within seconds — that is the containment — but the volume that finally leaves the room is only the 468, and the other 829 comes straight back at your work. The filter is what makes that safe. Nothing else does.

Why there is a mark, and why the cabinet alarms one inch off it

Your cabinet has a dimple moulded into the frame and a green LED that goes red the moment the sash leaves it. The manual's tolerance is +0.25 inches, −0.75 inches, and the certification test is blunt: raise the window one inch above the work position and the alarm must sound. Why would a manufacturer be that strict about two centimetres?

The obvious answer is the one most people give, and for a lot of cabinets it is right. Volume flow is velocity times area:

eq. 1.2.1 $$Q \;=\; v\,A$$

The area in question is the work opening: the cabinet width times the sash height. So for a fixed Q, the inflow velocity you actually get is

eq. 1.2.2 $$v_{\rm in} \;=\; \frac{Q}{w\,h_{\rm sash}}$$

Inflow velocity is inversely proportional to sash height, so on a cabinet whose blower turns at one speed, opening the sash is the same thing as turning the protection down. Try the older, fixed-blower cabinet chip and watch it happen. Plenty of cabinets in plenty of labs behave exactly like that, and for them this is the whole answer.

Ours does not. Put the chip back on the 1300 Series, drag the sash from 8 inches to 10, and read the inflow: 0.53 m/s at both. Then read the exhaust volume: 468 m³/h at 8 inches, 585 at 10. The cabinet did not let the velocity fall — it spun the blower up and moved more air. You can check this against the manual without taking our word for it, and it is worth doing because the numbers are exact rather than approximately right.

Worked example — proving from the data label that the cabinet compensates
  1. Write what you know. From the specification table for a 4 ft 1300 Series A2: exhaust volume flow is 468 m³/h at the 8 in work position and 585 m³/h at 10 in. Inflow is quoted as 0.53 m/s at both.
  2. Note the one fact that makes this checkable. A cabinet has exactly two openings to the room: the work opening and the exhaust. Whatever goes in must come out, so
    $$Q_{\rm in} \;=\; Q_{\rm exhaust}$$
    and the exhaust figures are the inflow figures.
  3. Solve eq. 1.2.2 for the width, at 8 inches.
    $$w=\frac{Q}{v_{\rm in}h}=\frac{468/3600}{(0.53)(0.203)}=1.207\ \mathrm{m}$$
  4. Now use that width to predict the velocity at 10 inches — a genuine prediction, since nothing about the 10 in row was used to get it:
    $$v_{\rm in}=\frac{585/3600}{(1.207)(0.254)}=0.530\ \mathrm{m/s}$$
  5. Check the arithmetic another way. 585/468 = 1.2500, and 10/8 = 1.2500. The 6 ft models give 878/702 = 1.2507. The exhaust volume tracks the opening height exactly.
The velocity is held constant by design, and the volume is what moves. So the naive answer — "raise the sash and lose your inflow" — is simply not what this cabinet does, and a student who believes it will be confused the first time they lift the sash and nothing about the airflow reading changes. What changes is the alarm.

Which brings back the real question. If the airflow is fine at 10 inches, why does the cabinet start complaining at 8¼?

Because what you lose off the mark is not velocity. It is certification. Every NSF/ANSI 49 test the cabinet has ever passed — inflow, downflow, the four smoke-pattern tests, containment — was performed at the marked opening. One inch higher, nothing has been measured by anybody. The cabinet cannot tell you whether the split has moved, whether the curtain still holds at the top corners, or whether the sash-retention pattern still keeps smoke inside. It can only tell you that you are somewhere nobody checked, and that is exactly what the red LED means.

Two more reasons the manufacturer is strict, both visible in the sim:

  • The compensation is a reserve, and it is spoken for. The SmartFlow indicator exists to show how much capacity the blower has left to absorb filter loading over a year of use. Spend it holding your inflow at an opening nobody certified and there is less of it for the thing it was for. Drag the sash past about 13 inches and you can watch it run out: the velocity finally starts to fall, and the airflow alarm joins the position alarm it has been ignoring for five inches.
  • The tolerance is deliberately lopsided: +0.25 in, −0.75 in. You may be three times further below the mark than above it. Too low costs you working room and eventually starts disturbing the barrier with your own forearms; too high costs containment, and containment is the one that hurts somebody.
Read it yourself Where these numbers come from. All of them are in the operating manual for the cabinet in our room — the airflow specification table, the Use of the Window section for the dimple and the tolerance, and the certification section for the alarm test. The interesting habit to build is not remembering the figures; it is knowing that a machine you use every day ships with a document that answers questions like this exactly, and going to look.
Check yourself

1. The blower is running and the sash is at the certified height, but you are working with your forearms resting on the front grille. Which of the two streams have you broken, and who is now unprotected?

Answer

The inflow. Your arms are sitting on the intake, so room air is no longer being drawn cleanly under the opening — and the exhaust path for anything aerosolised at the work surface is blocked. Both you and the product lose. It is also the single most common posture error in a tissue-culture room.

2. Why is there no useful protection from a cabinet that has been switched on for only thirty seconds?

Answer

The steady flow field has not established, and the particles already settled on the work surface have not been swept to the grille. The usual guidance is to run the cabinet for at least 15 minutes before use, or simply to leave it running through the work week.

3. Somebody pushes a rack of plates right up against the back wall, covering the rear grille. The front grille is clear and the sash is on the mark, so the inflow reading has not moved. What have they broken?

Answer

About half of the downflow's exit. That air still has to leave, and the only other way out is forwards — so the split moves backwards and air that should have gone straight down and out at the rear now travels across the work surface to reach the front grille instead. Nothing on the display changes, because the display watches the inflow and the downflow velocity, not where the downflow goes.

4. Your cabinet is alarming on sash position. A colleague measures the inflow with an anemometer, gets 0.53 m/s, and says it is fine. Are they right?

Answer

They measured the one thing that is fine. The velocity is held by the blower compensating; what has been lost is that nothing at this opening has ever been tested — not the smoke patterns, not containment, not the split. An in-specification number at an uncertified opening is not evidence of protection, and the cabinet is alarming precisely to say so.

2.2 · Why 0.3 µm is the hard one

Both streams end at a HEPA filter, and the specification everyone quotes — "99.99% at 0.3 µm" — sounds like the filter's limit: it catches things down to 0.3 µm and misses smaller ones. That reading is exactly backwards, and the sim will show you why before the prose explains it.

Sweep the diameter axis and find the worst particle size.

  1. Look at the penetration curve. Find its peak — the size the filter is worst at.
  2. Is that peak at the smallest particle on the axis? What happens to penetration as you go below it?
  3. Turn each of the three mechanisms off in turn and watch which half of the curve collapses.
  4. Push the face velocity up. Which mechanism gets worse, and why does a filter have a rated flow at all?

Controls

Mechanisms

Penetration vs particle diameter

Three mechanisms, pulling in opposite directions

A HEPA filter is not a sieve. Its fibres are microns apart and it stops things a thousand times smaller than the gaps. It works because a particle can be removed from an airstream three different ways, and the three of them have opposite size dependence:

  • Inertial impaction — a heavy particle cannot follow the air around a fibre and crashes into it. Gets better as particles get bigger.
  • Interception — a particle following a streamline that passes within one particle-radius of a fibre touches it and sticks. Also better with size.
  • Diffusion — a small particle is kicked around by air molecules and wanders off its streamline into a fibre. Gets better as particles get smaller.

Assuming they act independently, a particle escapes only if all three miss it:

eq. 1.2.3 $$\eta \;=\; 1-(1-\eta_D)(1-\eta_R)(1-\eta_I)$$

The wandering that drives the diffusion term is the same random walk that drives everything in solution, and its strength is set by the Stokes–Einstein relation — bigger particle, weaker wandering:

eq. 1.2.4 $$D \;=\; \frac{k_BT}{3\pi\eta d}$$
Two mechanisms improve with size and one improves with smallness, so somewhere in the middle every mechanism is bad at once. That worst size — the most-penetrating particle size, typically 0.1–0.3 µm — is where the standard is written. "99.99% at 0.3 µm" is a promise about the filter's hardest case, which means it does better than that on everything else, in both directions. A 13 µm skin flake is not a challenge for a HEPA filter; it is a rounding error.
Worth knowing The filter is not the fragile part. HEPA media is extremely good and rarely the reason a cabinet fails a certification. What fails is the seal around the filter, the blower's ability to hold flow as the filter loads, and — overwhelmingly — the person standing in front of it. Which is what the rest of this lesson is about.
  • The cabinet is a shape made of air: downflow protects the culture, inflow protects you.
  • Inflow velocity ∝ 1/sash height. The sash is a safety setting, not a comfort setting.
  • 0.3 µm is the filter's hardest size, not its limit — performance improves in both directions from there.
  • Filters almost never fail. The airflow in front of them is what fails.

3.1 · Block the grille and watch what happens

This is the question this lesson exists to answer: what does overcrowding actually do? Not "it is untidy" — what breaks, and at what point.

The sim below is §2.1 — the same cabinet, the same circuit, the same split — with things in it. Covering a grille is not modelled as a separate effect: it shrinks that grille's open area in the very equation §2.1 uses to place the split, so the whole loop reorganises in front of you. The panel on the right is the tray from above, because which span is covered is not something a side section can show.

Start at zero. Then start covering things.

  1. At 0% blocked, every orange tracer entering at the opening turns down into the front grille and is captured. That is the certified cabinet, and it is the picture you already know from §2.1.
  2. Cover 20% of the front grille. In the plan view the arrows over the covered span vanish — not shorten, vanish — while the rest speed up. Velocity goes up, not down. Is that good?
  3. Keep going to 50%. Red tracers now walk straight across the section and over the tray: over a covered span there is no inward velocity, so there is nothing to cross. Find the blockage where the verdict flips.

  5. Both grilles clear, now stack items high enough to reach the filter face (the tall-items chip). Watch the blue tracers divert around it and close in behind — a different failure, same destination.
  6. Now the one that will actually happen to you: your forearms on the front edge. Nothing has been left in the cabinet, nothing is untidy, and about a third of the intake is covered anyway. Compare it with the absorbent pad — the numbers are nearly the same.

Now name it: the same continuity equation, used against you

The blower still moves the same volume of air. If a fraction f of the grille is covered, all of that air has to go through the fraction (1 − f) that is left:

eq. 1.3.1 $$v_{\rm local} \;=\; \frac{Q}{A\,(1-f)} \;=\; \frac{v_{\rm in}}{1-f}$$

That is eq. 1.2.2 again — same law, different area — and it is doing something genuinely counter-intuitive. Blocking the grille makes the remaining air go faster, not slower. So why is the cabinet worse?

Because the protection was never about speed. It was about smoothness. A cabinet works when the air moves in flat, parallel sheets — laminar flow, which is where the old name "laminar flow hood" comes from. Sheets of air do not mix, so the boundary between room air and work-surface air holds. And the part of the face you covered has no inward velocity at all — so along that span there is simply no curtain to cross, and room air walks in unopposed. Push the blockage further and the remaining opening stops being a gentle sheet and becomes a jet, which separates from the edges and rolls up into vortices; the vortices mix. Once the air mixes, there is no boundary, and there is no cabinet.

The commonest thing covering the front grille is your own forearms. Not a pad, not a tip box — your arms, resting on the front edge because that is where arms go. Two forearms across the intake cover roughly a third of it, they are there for the whole procedure, and nobody counts them as clutter because they are not objects. The cabinet has a fitting for exactly this: the manual says the armrests exist to "minimize blockage of airflow at the front grille" — a sentence that only makes sense once you know where arms end up by default. Rest your arms on the armrest, keep your wrists forward of your work, and keep your forearms off the grille. It costs nothing and it is worth more than most of the rest of this tab.
And the remedy is a part you can order, not a habit you have to maintain. The 1300 Series takes two armrests — solid blocks that insert into the first perforated track of the work tray and sit 8 in (20 cm) apart, centred in the working area. They hold your forearms nearly horizontal and, more to the point, they hold them off the grille and behind it — which is something a fitting does all day and attention does not. Try the forearms on the armrests chip: coverage goes from about 30% to 4% with the same arms and the same person. The manual's own line is unusually direct: "Work safety is assured only if the armrests are used correctly!" If the cabinet in your room has not got a pair, they are a catalogue item.
Overcrowding does not weaken the airflow. It converts the airflow from a barrier into a mixer. That is why a half-blocked grille is worse than a slightly under-powered blower: an under-powered blower is a weak wall, and a blocked grille is a fan pointed at your sample.

The second failure has nothing to do with the grille. Anything tall enough interrupts the downflow before it reaches the work surface. A bottle standing 25 cm high in a cabinet with a 35 cm working depth is not "in the way" — it is a hole in the ceiling of sterile air, and everything downstream of it is being fed by whatever swirled in from the sides.

Where the published guidance lands "Do not use the hood as a storage area." The standard reference for this lesson gives the reason in one clause: storing boxes, bottles and cans in the hood, "besides adding to the bioburden, disrupts the airflow patterns." Both halves matter, and the second is the one people underestimate — a clean, sterile, autoclaved box left in a cabinet still breaks it.

3.2 · Everything in the cabinet has a wake

Even a single object that is nowhere near a grille casts a shadow. Air flowing past a bluff body separates behind it and recirculates — a region where the air is not coming from the filter any more, it is going round in a circle. Put your open dish in that region and the cabinet is, locally, off.

Move the bottle around and find where the shadow lands.

  1. Put a 20 cm bottle 5 cm behind the open dish — object at 37, dish at 32. In the section, watch the blue tracers divert around it and the amber ones stop falling and start going round. In the plan panel, read what fraction of the dish that costs you.
  2. Look at everything else in the section while you do it: the circuit, the split, both grilles. All perfect. That is the point — nothing on a real cabinet's display would report this.

3.3 · Move slower than the air

Last failure mode, and the one nobody is told: your hands can be faster than the cabinet. The air curtain moves at about half a metre per second. That is not fast. A relaxed reach across a bench is roughly 0.5 m/s; a quick grab is two or three times that.

Sweep your hand through the opening at different speeds and watch what comes in with it. The section is §2.1's cabinet, so the slug of room air your arm drags in has somewhere real to go — it is carried by the actual downflow, over the actual tray, toward the dish. The plan panel is the view that settles the argument: the air curtain is a line across the front of the cabinet, and a path either crosses it or it does not. Work done parallel to the sash is an arm moving into the page of the section, which is why it sits still there and drags nothing through.

  1. You arrive at 80 cm/s — an ordinary reach. Read the disturbance ratio, then drag down to 15 cm/s and watch the curtain hold.
  2. Raise it to 50 cm/s. That is the speed of the air itself. What is the disturbance ratio?
  3. Go to 120 cm/s — a normal, unthinking reach. Watch the red slug of room air appear in both views at the moment the arm crosses the line — it is one slug drawn twice — and follow it across the section to the open dish.
  4. Now switch to parallel to the sash and leave the speed exactly where it was. The ratio Π does not change at all — but the crossing count goes to zero, and so does everything that depended on it. That is the whole rule.

The same cabinet — now with an arm going through the opening

One caution before the equation, because this section is easy to over-read: everything here counts room air crossing the curtain, and nothing else. It is a complete account of that one route and it says nothing at all about the particles coming off you. §3.4 picks that up.

The whole thing is one ratio. Call it the disturbance ratio:

eq. 1.3.2 $$\Pi \;=\; \frac{v_{\rm hand}}{v_{\rm curtain}}$$

When Π < 1 the curtain re-forms behind your arm faster than your arm is moving, and the boundary survives. When Π > 1 your arm is outrunning the air that is supposed to close behind it, and it drags a slug of room air in with it. With vcurtain ≈ 0.5 m/s, the whole rule is:

Move your hands slower than about 50 cm/s — roughly a hand's width every third of a second — and move them parallel to the sash rather than in and out through it. That single sentence is most of what "good technique in the hood" means, and it is the one thing you can practise without any cells at all.

And it explains a detail from the published guidance that otherwise reads as fussiness: "Placing a dust-laden sleeve into a laminar flow hood generates a cloud of dust particles that can easily fall into and contaminate cultures." A sleeve is a large, rough, slow-moving surface loaded with exactly the 4–28 µm particles from §1.1, and it arrives on a fast-moving arm. The dustiness slider is that sentence, made into a number.

Check yourself

1. Two people are working. One keeps a very clean, empty cabinet but reaches in and out quickly a dozen times per procedure. The other keeps a cluttered cabinet but moves slowly and never leaves. Who contaminates more?

Answer

Genuinely a trick question — both are failing, in different ways, and neither is safe. But the fast reacher is usually worse in practice, because each crossing is a discrete injection of room air directly over the work, and twelve of them is twelve chances. The cluttered cabinet degrades the baseline; the fast hands add events. Fix the events first — they are free to fix.

2. Why does opening the door of the culture room matter, if the cabinet is three metres away?

Answer

A door sweeps a large volume of air and creates a back-draft across the room. That cross-draft hits the front opening sideways and peels the air curtain off, exactly like a fast hand but across the whole width. Published guidance is explicit: keep culture-room doors closed while a cabinet is in use, and minimise foot traffic behind it.

  • Blocking a grille speeds the air up and turns a barrier into a mixer. It is the worst failure because it looks like nothing.
  • Every object casts a wake. Tall things go at the back, never upstream of an open vessel.
  • The curtain moves at ~0.5 m/s. Move slower than that, and parallel to the sash.
  • Clutter degrades the baseline; fast hands and open doors add discrete events. Both matter; the events are free to fix.

Why height helps the dish and hurts everything else

Raising your hand widens the plume, because the particles have further to fall and longer to disperse. That drops the fraction landing in any one vessel — which looks like an improvement and is not, for two reasons. The total shed is unchanged, so the same material is now distributed over a wider patch of bench; and if there is more than one open vessel down there, a wider plume reaches more of them. Dispersion is not removal. The only move that removes anything is putting the plume somewhere the cabinet can deal with it — which is to say, on the stainless steel, where the downflow sweeps it into a grille and through a HEPA filter within seconds.

Move parallel and keep your hands out from over it. §3.3 is about the path your hand takes; this is about the place your hand occupies. A slow, parallel, well-planned movement that parks your cuff directly over an open flask is a technically perfect execution of the wrong idea.
Nothing above an open vesselNot your hand, not your cuff, not a pipette tip you are not currently using, not the bottle you are holding. If it is above open medium, whatever it sheds is in the medium.
Approach from beside or in front, never from aboveCome at a flask or a plate from the side or from your own side of it rather than over it, and cap it the moment you are done. A cap turns this whole section off. The one place you cannot retreat to is the front grille — §3.1.
Sleeves are part of the problemThe cuff is further from your eyes than your fingers and it shed more than they do. Gowns with knit cuffs under gloves exist for this reason, and it is why the manual asks you to work with your forearms on the armrest rather than waving them.
Slow is two benefits, not oneA slow hand crosses the curtain gently (§3.3) and sheds less with a narrower plume (this section). It is the one habit that pays into both routes.
Honesty about the model What is solid and what is a scale. Solid: that gloves and sleeves shed continuously, that the downflow carries what they shed almost straight down, that the plume widens with fall height and with hand speed, and that the fraction reaching a small target therefore collapses very fast with lateral offset. Illustrative: the absolute particles per minute, which depends on garments, skin, activity and how recently the gloves were changed, and which varies by more than an order of magnitude between people. Read the ratios here and the shape of the collapse; do not quote the counts.

4.1 · Why 70%, and why the wait is the whole thing

Everything so far has been about air. This tab is about the other way things get in, which is that you carry them in. The cabinet filters air; it has no opinion whatsoever about the outside of the bottle in your hand.

The habit is universal and the reason for it usually is not: spray the outside with 70% ethanol, and then — the part people skip — leave it alone until it is dry. Two questions worth being able to answer, because the answers change what you do:

  • Why 70%, and not the 100% bottle on the shelf?
  • Why does waiting matter more than which bottle you picked?
  1. Start on spray and wipe — the usual. Read the survivor count. That is what goes into the cabinet on that item.
  2. Switch to spray, then actually wait. Nothing changed except that you did nothing for half a minute. Read it again.
  3. Now 100%, surely stronger. Same wait, stronger alcohol, worse result. Find D on the stats panel and compare it with 70%.
  4. Drag the concentration slider slowly from 30% to 100% and watch the blue curve. Where is the peak, and how flat is it either side? That flatness is why nobody argues about 68 versus 75.
  5. Try a light mist at a 30 s wait. The wait is long and it buys nothing. Why not — and what would you change?
  6. Set the load to 10⁷ (something genuinely filthy) and find the wait that still gets you under one survivor.

Controls

The concentration curve, and the clock that actually decides it

Water is not the diluent. It is a reagent.

Ethanol kills a vegetative cell two ways at once: it dissolves into the lipid membrane and makes it leak, and it denatures proteins — unfolds them so they stop working. The second one is the killing step, and it is the one that has a requirement people find surprising:

Protein denaturation needs water. A protein in a genuinely anhydrous solvent is remarkably stable — this is why enzymes can be freeze-dried and sit in a drawer for a year. Take the water away and you have not made the alcohol stronger; you have removed the thing it needs to work with.

And there is a second, more mechanical failure. Absolute alcohol pulls water out of the outermost layer of the cell so quickly that the surface proteins there coagulate into a fixed shell — and the alcohol then cannot get through it. The organism inside is dehydrated, wrapped, and alive. This is the same phenomenon as fixing a specimen for microscopy, which is a thing we do on purpose with alcohol, and it should be the clue.

So the two requirements pull opposite ways. More alcohol means more of the agent; more water means the agent can act, and can keep penetrating instead of sealing the surface. Something like

eq. 1.4.2 $$\frac{1}{D(C)} \;\propto\; \left(\frac{C}{0.70}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{1-C}{0.30}\right)^{9/7}$$

has the right shape, and a product of that form peaks at C = p/(p+q) — which is 0.70 for those two exponents. The number to take away is not 70. It is the shape: a broad plateau from roughly 55% to 85% where it hardly matters, a soft decline below that, and a collapse as you approach 100%.

One honest wrinkle, visible on the chart if you push the wait past about 20 seconds: the achieved reduction can peak slightly below 70%, because a more dilute film stays wet longer and buys back contact time. The green line marks where the rate peaks — eq. 1.4.2 — and that is what 70% is chosen for. The two effects are separate, they pull in the same broad direction, and neither of them is a reason to argue about a few percent.

The number on the bottle 70% is a middle, not a threshold. Published disinfection data put the effective range for ethanol at about 60–90%, with absolute alcohol markedly worse. 70% sits in the middle of the plateau, is what wholesalers stock, and is what your bottle says. If someone hands you 80% or 65%, use it. If someone hands you the 100% from the solvent cupboard, dilute it.

Now the part that actually decides the outcome

Disinfection is a rate, not an event. For a fixed organism on a fixed surface it is first-order in time: every D seconds of wet contact takes one factor of ten off the count. D is the decimal reduction time.

eq. 1.4.1 $$\log_{10}\!\frac{N_{0}}{N} \;=\; \frac{t_{\rm wet}}{D(C)}$$

Read what is on top of that fraction. Time. And not the time since you sprayed — the time the surface was wet, because a film that has evaporated has stopped working. So the contact time you actually get is

teffective = min( how long you waited , how long it stayed wet )

which is two separate ways to lose. Wipe it dry at five seconds and you took the time away yourself. Mist it so lightly that it evaporates in four seconds and no amount of patience helps. The alcohol-rich films are the ones that dry soonest, so 100% ethanol loses on both terms at once — slower kill and less time to do it in.

Spray it, put it down, and go and do something else. The single highest-value habit on this page costs you thirty seconds of not touching a bottle. Nothing else in this lesson is that cheap.
Where ethanol simply does not work Three limits worth knowing before you rely on it. Spores. Ethanol is not sporicidal at any concentration — a Bacillus spore will sit in 70% ethanol indefinitely. That is what autoclaving is for, and it is why a spore-forming contamination is a different conversation. Soil. Organic matter protects organisms and consumes the alcohol; a visibly dirty surface must be cleaned first and then disinfected, in that order, and the model above assumes a clean surface. Porous things — and a distinction worth getting right. Cardboard and corrugate cannot be surface-disinfected in any meaningful sense: break the box down at the bench and bring in the inner packs. But do not extend that to the paper-backed peel pouch a serological pipette comes in. That paper is a sterile barrier, and it is porous on purpose — porous is how the sterilant got in when it was made. You do not disinfect a peel pouch, and spraying one can wick liquid straight through it and defeat it. You peel it, and you peel it at the cabinet, so that what comes out of it is presented into clean air. Lesson 3 §3.3 has the technique.
And two safety notes Ethanol is flammable and the cabinet recirculates. Spray sparingly, never toward the supply filter face, and do not use an open flame in a cabinet — a Bunsen burner in a Class II cabinet disrupts the airflow pattern it depends on and puts alcohol vapour next to a flame. Use sterile disposable loops or a microincinerator. And 70% ethanol left standing on stainless steel is fine, but the manual asks for the chamber to be rinsed after disinfection, because residues are what actually damage the finish.

The loading routine, in the order it happens

None of this is difficult and all of it is skippable, which is why it is worth writing down once.

1 · Run it firstTurn the blower on and wait for the airflow indicator to go green before anything goes in — the manual's own criterion. In practice a few minutes: the chamber has to sweep itself out, and until it has you are loading into room air.
2 · Wipe the empty cabinet70% ethanol on the work tray and the sides while it is empty, and let that stay wet too. Then rinse if your protocol calls for it. Doing this after you have loaded is doing it around your own stuff.
3 · Decide what actually goes inOnly what the next twenty minutes needs. Every extra item is bioburden plus a wake (§3.2) plus something your sleeve now has to travel over (§3.4).
4 · Spray, and then leave itWet, not misted. Set it down outside and start the next one; by the time you have sprayed the fourth item the first is ready. This is the step that has a number attached to it — eq. 1.4.1 — and it is the one that gets dropped.
5 · Bring the barrier in; leave the shipping outTwo different things get called "packaging". A peel pouch is a sterile barrier and is designed to be opened at the cabinet — peel it here, not at the bench, or the contents meet room air on the way in. Corrugate and cardboard are shipping, not a barrier: break the box down outside and bring in the inner packs. And the wrapper never stays — peel, take, wrapper straight into the discard.
6 · Load in one trip, slowlyOne deliberate pass at under 50 cm/s beats six quick ones — the crossings are what cost you (§3.3), so make fewer of them. Carry with both hands and keep your forearms off the intake.
7 · Place it where it is going to liveBehind the work-zone limit, clear of both grilles, tall things at the back and downstream of anything open, clean on one side and waste on the other so no reach ever crosses an open vessel (§3.2, §3.4). Placing it twice is two more chances to knock something.
8 · Let it settleGive the cabinet a minute after loading before you open the first vessel. You have just put six objects and two arms through the curtain; the downflow needs a moment to clear what that stirred up.
"Do not bring packaging into the cabinet" is one of those rules that is half right and gets taught whole. The question is never whether something is wrapped. It is where the sterile barrier gets broken, and what is exposed at the moment it does. A peel pouch is a barrier: break it inside the cabinet and the sterile contents are immediately in filtered air, which is the entire reason the pouch is shaped the way it is. A cardboard carton is not a barrier at all — it is a box that a barrier arrived in — so opening it inside buys nothing and costs you fibres you cannot disinfect off. Same rule, opposite answers, and the thing that decides it is what is underneath the layer you are about to open.
The order is not arbitrary — it is the order that stops you undoing your own work. Wipe before you load, or you are wiping around your stuff. Spray before you need the item, or you will not wait. Place it once, or you will be reaching over things you have already opened. Every one of these is a step that costs nothing when it is in the right place and costs the whole procedure when it is not.

5.3 · What one contaminated flask actually did

Everything above is a model. This is a measurement, and it is the reason this lesson leads the whole track.

McGarrity deliberately infected a culture with mycoplasma and used it to study how mycoplasma spreads during ordinary subculturing inside a laminar flow hood. After a single trypsinisation of that infected culture, live mycoplasma were recovered from:

  • the technician
  • the outside of the flask
  • the haemocytometer
  • the pipettor
  • the outside of the pipette discard pan
  • and the surface of the laminar flow hood itself — four to six days later

A clean culture, subcultured once a week in that same hood afterwards, tested positive after six weeks.

Read that list again as a map of where your hands went. Not one of those surfaces is exotic. The pipettor. The discard pan. The outside of the flask — the part you hold. The contamination did not travel through the air to those places; it travelled on the same trajectories your hands make a hundred times a week, and then it sat there for most of a working week waiting.

This is also the honest answer to "why does my whole lab go down at once?" The reference this lesson is built on states it plainly: if one culture in a laboratory is mycoplasma-contaminated then usually most, if not all, of the others will be too. And the leading source is not the air, or the media, or the cabinet — it is an infected culture brought in from another lab.

Quarantine incoming linesA cell line from another lab is untested until you test it. It does not go in the shared incubator first.
One line at a timeOne cell line, one bottle of medium, in the cabinet at a time — then disinfect the surface before the next line.
Disinfect after, not just before70% ethanol before and after every use. The four-to-six-day number is why "after" is not optional.
Waste leaves with youEmpty pipette trays and waste daily. A discard pan sitting in a cabinet overnight is a reservoir.
Worked example — the cabinet is running; how long until a settled particle is swept away?
  1. Write what you know. Downflow vdown = 0.32 m/s over a work surface 0.35 m deep (front to back). A particle released at the sash line, 0.4 m above the surface.
  2. Assemble. Two clocks race: gravity (eq. 1.1.1) and the downflow carrying it to a grille. Time to be carried down the 0.4 m by the airflow:
    $$t_{\rm air}=\frac{h}{v_{\rm down}}=\frac{0.40}{0.32}\ \mathrm{s}$$
  3. Compare with gravity alone for a 13 µm particle, using vs = 5.08 × 10−3 m/s from §1.1:
    $$t_{\rm grav}=\frac{0.40}{5.08\times10^{-3}}\ \mathrm{s}$$
  4. Read the ratio. tair = 1.3 s, tgrav = 79 s — the downflow is about 60 times stronger than gravity for this particle.
In a working cabinet, gravity is irrelevant — the air decides where every particle goes, and it takes about a second to do it. That is the good news and the bad news in one number: the cabinet clears your mistakes in about a second, and it delivers them just as fast if the flow is pointed the wrong way.
  • Lay out for the task: front strip empty, open vessels in the middle, waste downstream, tall things at the back.
  • Fewer objects is not tidiness — it is fewer wakes and less bioburden.
  • Contamination travels on your hands and your tools, not mysteriously through the air.
  • In a running cabinet the airflow beats gravity ~60:1. The air decides. Your job is to decide what the air does.

6.1 · Next lesson's headline, measured today

You have just spent twenty minutes protecting the air above one plate. Then you pick the plate up, walk three metres, and put it inside a box that is 37 °C, 95% humidity, has an open tray of water in the bottom, a fan, and eight other people's cultures in it.

Before you leave this lesson, measure one thing about that box: what actually happens when someone opens the door.

  1. Open the door for 10 seconds. How long does the temperature take to come back?
  2. Now for 60 seconds — someone hunting for their flask. Compare the recovery times.
  3. Switch the readout to CO₂. Which recovers more slowly, heat or gas? Why might that be?
  4. Set door openings to 12 per day and watch what fraction of the day the box actually spends at setpoint.

You are seeing it here without earning it. In Lesson 2 you will build it from the physics, find out why τ is so different for heat and for CO₂, and discover what that difference does to the pH of the medium your cells are sitting in. This equation keeps the number 1.5.1 when it comes back.

An assumption from this lesson that expires

Every sim in Tabs 1–4 treated a particle as a thing that arrives from outside and lands on your culture. That is the right model for a cabinet. It is the wrong model for an incubator, where the most damaging things that happen to a culture are not deliveries at all — they are the medium slowly evaporating, the pH sliding, and the cells eating everything. Nothing lands. The culture destroys itself.
