1.1 · Push the plunger and watch what actually moves

Almost everyone who uses a pipettor for the first time believes it sucks up liquid. It does not touch the liquid at all. There is a column of air between the piston and your sample, and everything that will go wrong in the next two tabs is something that happens to that air.

Drive the plunger by hand. Find the two stops.

  1. Watch one full stroke without touching anything. Press, dip, release, wait, withdraw. Note which part of it involves liquid at all — it is less than half.
  2. During the push, read what is leaving the tip. It is air, and that is why you press before the tip goes in rather than after.
  3. Now drag the plunger slider yourself. Find where it gets noticeably harder — the first stop — then push past it to the second stop. Note how much extra air that moves. Let go and the stroke resumes on its own.
  4. Try each pill in turn. A gel, rushed and the same gel, waited out differ by nothing except the wait slider — compare what each one actually took.
  5. Switch to aspirated from the second stop and watch the same stroke. How much comes up, and is that what your protocol asked for?
  6. Now change the liquid to Matrigel on ice and watch the release again. A dashed line appears: that is where the air says the liquid should be. Why is the red level behind it?
  7. Look at the right-hand panel. The top plot is the whole round trip against time — dashed is the piston's demand, solid is what is actually in the tip. The bottom plot is the same trip with the demand on the x-axis, which turns a lag into a loop. Read the widest gap.
  8. With Matrigel still selected, wind wait before withdrawing from 0 to 2 s and watch the loop's right-hand corner. The strokes stay bowed — that never goes away — but the corner closes, and the corner is the volume you wrote down.
  9. Read "actually taken" at 0 s and at 1 s. It should go from about half of what you asked for to within 3%. One second.
  10. Now put the liquid back to PBS with the wait at 0. The loop collapses onto the diagonal on its own. Nothing about your technique changed — so what did?
  11. Set the wait so that "wait ÷ τ" reads about 3τ for each liquid in turn, and note how different those waits are in seconds. That number is where "wait one second" comes from, and §2.5 derives it.

Name it: two stops, two jobs, and one that is a trap

An air-displacement pipettor is a syringe with air in it. Move the piston a distance x through a bore of area A and you displace

eq. 3.1.1 $$V \;=\; A\,x$$

The first stop is calibrated: Ax equals the volume on the dial. The second stop pushes further, and its job is to clear the tip on dispense — to blow out the last drop. It is not calibrated for anything.

Aspirate from the first stop. Dispense to the second. Aspirating from the second stop pulls up more than you set and is the single most common way a new user is systematically wrong. The exception is deliberate — reverse pipetting, in §2.5 — where you aspirate from the second stop on purpose and never dispense the extra.

The air between piston and liquid is not a rigid rod. It is a gas, so its volume responds to pressure and temperature:

eq. 3.1.2 $$\frac{\Delta V_{\rm cushion}}{V_{\rm cushion}} \;=\; \frac{\Delta T}{T} - \frac{\Delta P}{P}$$

That is the whole reason a warm hand, a big tip on a small volume, or a volatile solvent changes your answer. §1.2 puts a number on it before the next tab spends it.

Worth knowing The other kind of pipettor. A positive-displacement device has a piston inside a disposable tip, in direct contact with the liquid. No air cushion, so none of eq. 3.1.2 applies — which is why it is the right tool for viscous, volatile or dense liquids where the cushion is the problem. It costs more per point, so it is used where accuracy matters, not everywhere.
  • The pipettor moves air. The liquid follows.
  • First stop aspirates and is calibrated. Second stop dispenses and is not.
  • The air column is compressible and expandable — that is where the errors live.
  • The liquid lags the piston, and plotted against it that lag is a hysteresis loop. Its width is the viscosity; you cannot pipette it away.
  • What you can close is the corner — and only by waiting. Matrigel with no wait takes half of what the dial says; one second brings it inside 3%.
  • The moment the tip leaves the liquid the shortfall is locked in. After that the cushion is pulling on air, and no amount of patience helps.

1.2 · The cushion moves when you are not touching it

You have stopped pressing. The plunger is at rest, your sample is in the tip, and nothing about the device is moving. Between the piston and that sample there is still a sealed column of air — for a 1000 µL pipettor taking 100 µL, about 900 µL of it, nine times the volume you are trying to measure.

Sealed does not mean fixed. eq. 3.1.2 says that column responds to temperature and pressure whether or not you are doing anything, and there is a heat source clamped around it: your hand. A palm is about 33 °C. The room is about 22 °C. The air inside is going to move.

Why it is slow, and why that matters more than the size

The air does not jump to 33 °C. It is inside a plastic body that conducts heat poorly, and only the part of the column your fingers actually wrap gets warmed at all — call that fraction φ, and it is about a quarter. What is left approaches your hand's temperature the way anything warming up does, exponentially, with a time constant τ of roughly 45 seconds:

eq. 3.1.3 $$\Delta V(t) \;=\; \varphi\,V_{\rm cushion}\,\frac{T_{\rm hand}-T_{\rm room}}{T_0} \left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$

Three consequences fall straight out of that, and each is a habit:

Time is a variable you controlAt t = 0 the error is zero. At t = τ it is 63% of the way to its worst. Put the pipettor down between cycles and you are permanently near t = 0.
The cushion, not the pipettor, is the leverVcushion is set by how much of the device is empty. Take 100 µL with a 100 µL pipettor and the cushion is tiny. Take it with a 1000 µL one and you have nine times the air and nine times this error.
Two mistakes multiply, they do not addWrong pipettor and a warm hand is not two small errors. The second term is proportional to the first one's cushion, so picking the wrong device is what makes hand warmth matter at all.

One more thing, because it is the reason this error survives: watch what does not happen. A pendant drop only falls when its weight beats the surface tension holding it to the orifice — Tate's law, \(V_{\rm drop} \approx 2\pi a\gamma/\rho g\), which is 14 µL on a small tip and about 24 µL on a large one. The expansion almost never reaches that. So nothing drips, nothing is visible, and the sample simply moves to the outside of your tip.

  1. Start on left on the stand. Let the clock run a full cycle. How much moves? Why?
  2. Switch to held in your hand. Same 100 µL, same 100 µL pipettor. Watch the meniscus and read the loss at 45 seconds — that is one τ.
  3. Now switch to held, and the wrong pipettor: same hand, same volume, but taken with the 1000 µL device. Compare the two curves on the chart.
  4. Drag the volume down to 20 µL with the 1000 µL pipettor still selected. The cushion grows as the sample shrinks — the error gets worse in both directions at once.
  5. Find a setting where the bead at the tip reaches the drop threshold. Can you, with a hand?

Worked example — the same 100 µL, taken two ways, held for half a minute
  1. Set the scene. You need 100 µL. It is a 22 °C room, your hand is 33 °C, and you hold the pipettor for 30 s while you find the right well. φ ≈ 0.25, τ ≈ 45 s, T0 = 295 K.
  2. Find each cushion. The cushion is the shaft's dead volume plus whatever of the tip is not full — roughly
    $$V_{\rm cushion} \approx 0.15\,R + 0.85\,(R - V_{\rm set})$$
    For the 100 µL device, R = 100 and Vset = 100, so Vcushion = 15 µL. For the 1000 µL device, R = 1000, so Vcushion = 150 + 765 = 915 µL — sixty-one times more air for exactly the same job.
  3. Assemble eq. 3.1.3 before putting numbers in it.
    $$\Delta V(30\,\mathrm{s}) = 0.25\,V_{\rm cushion}\, \frac{11\ \mathrm{K}}{295\ \mathrm{K}}\left(1-e^{-30/45}\right) = 0.25\,V_{\rm cushion}\,(0.0373)(0.487)$$
    which is just 0.00454 × Vcushion. The whole difference between the two devices is already visible: everything except the cushion is identical.
  4. Evaluate.
    $$\Delta V_{100} = 0.0045 \times 15 = 0.068\ \mu\mathrm{L} \qquad \Delta V_{1000} = 0.0045 \times 915 = 4.2\ \mu\mathrm{L}$$
  5. Put it in the units that matter. 0.07% against 4.2% of the volume you asked for.
The right pipettor makes hand warmth disappear — not shrink, disappear, to seven hundredths of a percent. The wrong one turns it into the largest single term in your error budget, which is exactly what you will see happen to the warm hand bar in §2.1. This is why the two mistakes are one mistake: hand warmth is only ever a symptom of having picked up a device with too much air in it.
  • The air column keeps working after you stop. eq. 3.1.2 does not care that you have let go.
  • The error grows with time held, 63% of the way there in one τ ≈ 45 s. Put it on the stand.
  • Vcushion is the multiplier on everything thermal — and it is set by which pipettor you picked up.
  • Nothing drips. The expansion never reaches Tate's threshold, so the sample moves to the outside of the tip and you never see it go.

2.1 · Why liquid sticks to plastic at all

Shake a drop of water onto a fresh pipette tip and it beads up and rolls off. Shake the same drop onto a clean glass slide and it spreads into a film you have to wipe. Same water, same gravity, same air. The solid decided.

Everything else in this tab — the film inside the tip, the liquid you drag in on the outside, the drop that will not leave the end — is the same decision being made over and over in different geometries. So it is worth spending one section on the decision itself.

Three surfaces meet at the edge of a drop

Look at the rim of the drop, the circle where liquid, solid and air all touch. That circle is called the contact line, and three different interfaces end on it:

  • solid–vapour (γsv) — dry solid meeting air
  • solid–liquid (γsl) — wetted solid meeting liquid
  • liquid–vapour (γlv) — the drop's own free surface, which is the γ you have already been using since §2.2

Each of those costs energy per unit area to make. That is what surface tension is — and it is worth noticing that J/m² and N/m are the same units, so the same number is simultaneously an energy per area and a pull per length. That is why an energy argument turns into a force balance without any work.

Spreading the drop by one square millimetre destroys a square millimetre of dry solid, creates a square millimetre of wetted solid, and adds a square millimetre of free liquid surface. The drop spreads if that trade is downhill. Balancing the three pulls along the solid gives Young's equation:

eq. 3.4.1 $$\gamma_{sv} \;=\; \gamma_{sl} + \gamma_{lv}\cos\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \cos\theta \;=\; \frac{\gamma_{sv}-\gamma_{sl}}{\gamma_{lv}}$$

θ is the contact angle — the angle the liquid surface makes with the solid, measured through the liquid — and it is the single number that says how a given liquid feels about a given solid.

θ < 90° — wettingA high-energy solid. The drop spreads, climbs walls, and clings. Glass, clean metal, tissue-culture-treated plastic.
θ > 90° — non-wettingA low-energy solid. The drop beads and lets go easily. PTFE, polyethylene, and — deliberately — polypropylene.

Your tips are polypropylene on purpose

Pipette tips are not made of polypropylene because it is cheap, or not only because it is cheap. Its surface energy is about 30 mN/m, which puts water at θ ≈ 93° — just on the non-wetting side of the line. A glass pipette of the same shape sits at θ ≈ 25°, and every liquid you put in it clings correspondingly harder.

The vessels are the opposite, and also on purpose. Untreated polystyrene is a low-energy plastic like polypropylene; a tissue-culture-treated flask has been plasma-oxidised to raise its surface energy to about 57 mN/m, which is what makes medium spread on it and cells attach to it. Two plastics, engineered in opposite directions, and you will use both in the same minute.

The one that changes under you A used tip is not a fresh tip. Serum proteins adsorb onto polypropylene within seconds and present their own, much more hydrophilic, surface. The effective energy climbs from ~30 to ~45 mN/m and θ falls from 93° to about 72°. Everything in §2.3 and §2.4 gets worse by that much, which is the real reason serum-containing medium is harder to pipette accurately than PBS — it is not just the viscosity, it is that the medium changes the tip.

The number the rest of the tab actually uses

θ is a shape. What the next two sections need is an energy: how much it costs, per unit area, to peel the liquid back off the solid. Combine Young with the definition of that work and the γsl you cannot measure cancels out:

eq. 3.4.2 $$W_{\rm adh} \;=\; \gamma_{lv}\left(1 + \cos\theta\right)$$

Young–Dupré. Everything from here to the end of the tab is a comparison of two of these: does the liquid stay on the tip, or go to the wall? Which surface wins is which surface has the larger W — and by how much.

Read one number off the simulation and keep it. Water on polypropylene is W ≈ 68 mJ/m². Water on a treated flask wall is W ≈ 115 mJ/m². And a liquid meeting itself — θ = 0, cos θ = 1 — is W = 2γ ≈ 144 mJ/m², which is the largest value eq. 3.4.2 can return. Nothing solid gets near it. §2.4 spends that fact: the drop on the end of your tip should be given back to the medium, which is both the only surface a sterile tip is allowed to touch and the strongest pull available.
  1. Start on a fresh tip. Read θ. Which side of 90° is it on, and by how little?
  2. Drag the surface energy up to a treated flask wall. Watch the drop go flat and W climb.
  3. Switch to a tip after serum. Nothing about the plastic changed — proteins landed on it. How far did θ move, and what did that do to W?
  4. Put it back on fresh polypropylene and change the liquid instead, from PBS to medium + 10% serum. Both γlv and θ move. Which one dominates W?
  5. On the chart, find the surface energy at which θ crosses 90° for your liquid. Everything to the left of it beads; everything to the right clings.

Where the model is a fit and where it is physics

eq. 3.4.1 and eq. 3.4.2 are exact. They are a force balance and a definition, and they hold whatever the materials are.

Getting θ from γs is the part that is not. The simulation uses the Good–Girifalco form, γsl = γs + γl − 2Φ√(γsγl), which reduces Young to

$$\cos\theta \;=\; 2\Phi\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_s}{\gamma_l}} \;-\; 1$$

with the interaction parameter Φ taken as an empirical fit, Φ = 0.66 + 0.006(γs − 18), rather than as a constant. Φ rises with surface energy because high-energy solids interact with water through polar bonds that a purely dispersive theory does not contain. 2.2 · Everything comes out wet

Pull a spoon out of a glass of water. It comes out wet, and within a second most of what clung to it has run back off. Pull the same spoon out of a jar of honey and it comes out coated — and the coat stays. Same spoon, same movement, same gravity. The liquid decided.

Now do the part that is less obvious. Put the spoon back in the water and pull it out fast. It comes out wetter than when you pulled it slowly. Nothing about the water changed.

So the film a surface carries away is not a property of the liquid. It is a property of the liquid and of how fast the surface moved through it — and since how fast is entirely up to you, this is one of the very few error terms at the bench you can shrink for free, with no equipment and no extra step.

The tug-of-war at the waterline

Put the spoon down and take the cleanest possible version of the problem: a flat plate standing vertically in a bath, pulled straight up at a steady speed U. That is the geometry this was first solved in, and everything you will actually touch — a pipette tip, a serological pipette, the inside of a bottle neck — is a variation on it.

Look closely at the waterline. Two things are fighting over the same liquid:

  • The plate drags liquid up with it. Liquid touching a solid does not slip — it moves with the solid. Viscosity carries that motion sideways into the fluid, so a layer near the plate gets hauled upward with it. The strength of that haul goes as viscosity times speed, μU.
  • Surface tension pulls the liquid back down. Where the film leaves the bath the free surface is curved, and curvature costs energy. Surface tension γ works to flatten that curve, which means pulling liquid back into the bath.

Neither term decides on its own. What decides is the ratio — how far viscosity is winning against surface tension. That ratio has no units and it has a name, the capillary number:

definition $$\mathrm{Ca} \;=\; \frac{\mu U}{\gamma}$$

Small Ca — slow, thin, high surface tension — and the plate comes out very nearly dry. Large Ca — fast, viscous, low surface tension — and it comes out coated. Every liquid in this lab sits somewhere on that scale, and the same liquid slides along the scale as you change how fast you move.

How thick the film actually is

Landau and Levich solved the plate problem in 1942, by matching the flat part of the film to the curved meniscus underneath it. The answer is short:

eq. 3.2.1 $$t_{\rm film} \;\approx\; 0.94\,\ell\,\mathrm{Ca}^{2/3} \,,\qquad \mathrm{Ca} \;=\; \frac{\mu U}{\gamma}$$

The one symbol that needs explaining is ℓ, the length that sets how sharply the meniscus curves. For a flat plate it is the capillary length

$$\ell_c \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\rho g}}\;\approx\; 2.4\ \mathrm{mm} \quad\text{for medium}$$

— the scale over which surface tension can still hold liquid up against gravity. For a tube — a pipette tip, a serological pipette — the wall's own radius r sets the curvature instead, so ℓ = r. That single substitution is the whole reason a result derived for a plate in a bath tells you what a pipette keeps.

Two things are worth carrying away from that expression, in order of usefulness:

The exponent is ⅔, not 1Speed matters, but less than proportionally. Double the withdrawal speed and the film grows by 22/3 = 59%, not 100%. Halve it and the film drops 37%. Viscosity enters through Ca in exactly the same way, so the same two numbers apply to it.
The film scales with the geometry, not your volumeA wider bore keeps a thicker film, whatever volume you happen to be moving. The residue in a 25 mL serological pipette is a different-sized problem from the residue in a 20 µL tip even at the same speed, in the same liquid.

From a thickness to a volume — the number that costs you

A thickness in micrometres is not yet an error. What you lose is the volume of that film: the thickness spread over every square millimetre of wall the liquid wetted. For a bore of diameter d wetted over a length L, the wetted perimeter is πd and

eq. 3.2.2 $$V_{\rm film} \;=\; \pi\,d\,L\,t_{\rm film}$$

This is the bridge from a physics result to a bench consequence, and it is where the surprises live: L is set by how far up the bore you drew the liquid, and a tall thin column of liquid has a great deal of wall.

Now go and measure it. The simulation below is the plate and the bath, nothing else — pull the plate out and watch the film it carries. The chart beside it is the same result on log axes, where a power law is a straight line and you can read the ⅔ off the slope with a ruler.

  2. Take the speed from 5 to 10 cm/s. The film should grow by 59%, not double — check it against the readout.
  3. Take the speed from 5 down to 0.5 cm/s, a factor of ten. On the chart, the point should fall by a factor of 102/3 ≈ 4.6. Does it?
  4. Now change the liquid instead and leave the speed alone. The point jumps to a different line — but the lines are all parallel. Why must they be?
  5. Switch to a 200 µL tip bore. The physics is identical; only ℓ changed, from 2.4 mm to 0.75 mm. Watch the film get thinner and the volume readout appear.
  6. Switch to a 25 mL serological pipette at 20 cm/s with serum-containing medium, and read the volume it keeps. That is a real number, and it is not small.
  7. Push Matrigel on ice up to 40 cm/s and read the verdict. The equation stops being allowed to answer — find out why.

Where the equation stops being allowed to answer

Landau–Levich is derived on two assumptions: that the film is thin compared with ℓ, and that the liquid is a clean Newtonian one. Both are worth knowing because both fail on things you will actually pipette.

The thinness condition is easy to check, because eq. 3.2.1 is the check: t/ℓ = 0.94·Ca2/3, so the film stops being thin once Ca climbs past roughly 0.03. Matrigel on ice at 20 cm/s puts a 380 µm film inside a 750 µm-radius tip bore — half the bore. At that point the equation is not slightly wrong, it is answering a different question, and the sim says so instead of printing a number.

The Newtonian condition fails more quietly. Serum-containing medium and anything with a gel in it are shear-thinning: their viscosity drops as you move them faster, so the real film grows a little more slowly with speed than Ca2/3 promises. Treat the absolute thickness as indicative and the scaling as the lesson — which is exactly the way you will use it, because the decision at the bench is never "what is t" but "should I have moved slower".

Worked example — how much medium a serological pipette keeps, fast versus slow
  1. Write down what you have. A 25 mL serological pipette, bore diameter d = 4.6 mm so r = 2.3 mm, drawn full so the wetted length is L = 25 cm. The liquid is medium with 10% serum: μ = 4 mPa·s, γ = 0.050 N/m. You lift it out of the bottle at 20 cm/s — a normal, unhurried movement — or at 2 cm/s, which feels slow.
  2. Assemble the two equations symbolically first. Thickness from eq. 3.2.1 with ℓ = r, volume from eq. 3.2.2:
    $$V_{\rm film} \;=\; \pi d L \cdot 0.94\,r \left(\frac{\mu U}{\gamma}\right)^{2/3}$$
    Everything but U is fixed by the pipette and the medium, so the whole thing is a constant times U2/3.
  3. Capillary number at each speed.
    $$\mathrm{Ca}_{20} = \frac{0.004 \times 0.20}{0.050} = 0.0160 \qquad \mathrm{Ca}_{2} = \frac{0.004 \times 0.02}{0.050} = 0.00160$$
  4. Thickness.
    $$t_{20} = 0.94(2.3\,\mathrm{mm})(0.0160)^{2/3} = 137\ \mu\mathrm{m} \qquad t_{2} = 0.94(2.3\,\mathrm{mm})(0.00160)^{2/3} = 29.6\ \mu\mathrm{m}$$
  5. Volume.
    $$V_{20} = \pi(4.6\,\mathrm{mm})(250\,\mathrm{mm})(137\,\mu\mathrm{m}) = 496\ \mu\mathrm{L} \qquad V_{2} = 107\ \mu\mathrm{L}$$
  6. Check the ratio against the exponent. 496/107 = 4.6, and 102/3 = 4.64. The two-thirds power is not decoration — it predicted the answer before you computed it.
Roughly 0.5 mL of medium stays in the pipette if you lift it out at 20 cm/s, and 0.1 mL if you lift it out at 2 cm/s. Four hundred microlitres, for nothing but slowing your arm down. On serum-containing medium, repeated every media change, that is a real cost as well as a real error. (Ca = 0.016 here, comfortably inside the thin-film range, so the equation is entitled to answer.)

  • Ca = μU/γ is the whole story: viscous drag versus surface tension.
  • t ≈ 0.94 ℓ Ca2/3. Double the speed = +59%. Halve it = −37%.
  • ℓ is the capillary length (~2.4 mm) for a plate and the bore radius for a tube — that substitution is what makes this a pipetting result.
  • Thickness only becomes an error through eq. 3.2.2: V = πdLt. Wide bore, long draw, big loss.
  • Above Ca ≈ 0.03 the film is no longer thin and the equation is out of its range. That is not a rounding error, it is a different problem.

2.3 · Going in: how deep, and at what angle

§2.2 was about a surface leaving a liquid. A pipette tip has two such surfaces, and only one of them has been accounted for.

Inside — the boreThe film there is liquid you aspirated and will not deliver. It makes you short. That is §2.2, and pre-wetting is the fix.
Outside — the wallThe film there is liquid you never asked for, lifted out of the source and carried to wherever the tip goes next. It makes you long — and it contaminates. This section.

Both obey exactly the same two equations. What is different is the area, and the area outside is set by one decision: how far in you pushed the tip.

A tip is a cone, so depth costs more than you would guess

If the tip were a straight rod, wetting twice as deep would wet twice the area. It is not — it is a cone, roughly 0.8 mm across at the very end and widening by about 0.15 mm of diameter for every millimetre you go up. Integrating the circumference along the immersed length s:

$$A_{\rm out} \;=\; \int_0^{s}\!\pi\,d(z)\,\mathrm{d}z \;=\; \pi\left(d_0 s + k s^{2}\right)$$

The s² is the whole story. And it is worse than that, because the local radius also sets ℓ in eq. 3.2.1 — a fatter part of the cone carries a thicker film as well as more of it. (The tip's outer radius, 0.4–1.6 mm, is smaller than the capillary length 2.4 mm, so ℓ is the local radius rather than ℓc — the fibre-coating limit, not the plate limit.)

ImmersionWetted areaFilmCarried outOn a 100 µL delivery
2 mm — just below the surface5.97 mm²4.6 µm0.027 µL+0.03%
15 mm — buried90.7 mm²9.3 µm0.84 µL+0.84%

Seven and a half times the depth, thirty-one times the liquid. And unlike the inside film, no habit removes this one — there is no such thing as pre-wetting the outside. You only get to not do it.

Two ways to get it wrong in the other direction

Too shallowThe level falls as you aspirate. Take 2 mL from a reservoir 45 mm across and the surface drops 1.3 mm; start 1 mm down and the tip is in air before you finish, and you get a partial volume with a bubble in it.
On the bottomThe vessel floor throttles the orifice, the flow rate collapses, and the plunger reaches the stop long before the liquid does. That one is not a film problem and the simulation below does not model it — §2.5 gives it its mechanism, and it is a savage one: the settling time goes as 1/r⁴.

And now the angle — where the obvious answer is wrong

"Hold the pipettor vertical" is on every guidance sheet, and the obvious mechanism is the half of eq. 3.1.2 that §1.2 never used. Tilt the device by φ and the liquid column in the tip no longer hangs straight down, so its vertical height falls from L to Lcos φ, and the hydrostatic pull it exerts on the sealed cushion above it changes:

eq. 3.1.4 $$\Delta P \;=\; \rho g L\left(1-\cos\varphi\right), \qquad \frac{\Delta V}{V_{\rm cushion}} \;=\; -\frac{\Delta P}{P_0}$$

So put numbers in it. A 20 mm column, tilted 30°, on a 115 µL cushion:

$$\Delta P = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.020 \times 0.134 = 26\ \mathrm{Pa} \qquad \Delta V = 115\,\frac{26}{101325} = 0.03\ \mu\mathrm{L}$$

Three hundredths of a microlitre — 0.03%. The manufacturers measure the angle error at something like 0.5 to 0.7%. The obvious mechanism is real, and it is twenty times too small to be the answer.

So "hold it vertical" is not a pressure rule. It is an immersion-depth rule wearing a disguise. A tip held at φ and pushed to a vertical depth h is wetted along its own axis for s = h/cos φ — 15% further at 30°, 41% further at 45° — and the area went as s². Tilting does not change the pressure in any way that matters. It changes how much of the tip is under the surface, which is the term that was already the expensive one.

That is worth more than the rule it explains. Two guidance sheets can give you the same instruction for different reasons, and only one of them tells you what to do when the situation changes. If the angle error were hydrostatic you would fix it by using a shorter column. It is not, so you fix it by watching the tip rather than the pipettor.

  1. Start on just below the surface. Read what the outside carries out — it is a rounding error.
  2. Drag the depth to 15 mm. The area readout should go up about fifteen-fold and the volume about thirty. Why the difference?
  3. Now tilt to 30° without touching the depth. The vertical depth is the same; the wetted path is not.
  4. Set the depth to 1 mm and the volume to 2000 µL. Watch the level fall past the tip.
  5. Go back to 15 mm and change the liquid to medium + 10% serum. Both this section and §2.1 are acting at once: more viscous and a tip the proteins have made wettable.

2.4 · Coming out: the drop that stays

You have the volume, the tip is over the destination, you press to the second stop and the blow-out clears the bore. §2.2 is dealt with. And there is still something on the end of the tip.

It is not a mistake and it is not avoidable. It is what §2.1 said would happen: liquid adheres to the plastic with W = γ(1 + cos θ), and no amount of pushing air past it changes that. The only questions are how much, and what can take it away from you.

What is actually hanging there — two things, not one

The residual is a bead pinned on the rim of the orifice, plus a film that has crept up the outside of the tip. They are governed by different physics and — this is the part worth slowing down for — they respond to wettability in opposite directions.

The bead, at the rimA cap pinned at the orifice edge, standing at the contact angle. As θ falls the liquid flattens out instead of bulging, so the bead gets smaller. It cannot exceed Tate's limit or it simply falls off.
The creep, up the wallA static meniscus climbing the outside against gravity, h ≈ ℓc√(2(1 − sin θ)). At θ near 90° it is essentially zero. As θ falls it grows fast — and it is spread over the whole circumference.

Tate's law sets the ceiling on the bead, and it is the same expression §1.2 used to show that a warm hand never makes a pipettor drip:

eq. 3.4.3 $$V_{\rm drop} \;=\; \frac{2\pi a\gamma}{\rho g}$$

For a 200 µL tip, a = 0.4 mm, that ceiling is about 15 µL — far more than anything ever hangs there. The bead is nowhere near falling off; it is just stuck.

Put the two together for a real tip and the crossover is the whole lesson. In medium, on a 200 µL tip:

The one number to take to the bench A fresh tip holds 0.14 µL. The same tip, after it has been in serum-containing medium, holds 1.12 µL. Eight times as much, and nothing about the plastic changed — proteins landed on it and moved θ from 87° to 63° (§2.1). This is why touching off is a footnote when you are moving PBS around and a real term when you are moving medium, and why the people who insist on it are usually the people who work with serum.

Where you put it — and the rule that decides

McGhee Lab practice — and it overrides the pipetting literature The tip touches nothing but the medium. Ever. General-purpose pipetting guidance — including the Corning sheet this lesson cites for the other five habits — tells you to touch off against the vessel wall. In an analytical lab that is fine. In cell culture it is wrong, and it is the kind of wrong that costs you a flask a fortnight later: the wall of an open vessel has been sitting in room air, and a tip that touches it is a tip that goes on to touch your stock. Touch the drop off into the medium already in the vessel.

So ask eq. 3.4.2 what the medium is worth as a receiving surface. The drop and the bulk are the same liquid. A liquid meets itself at a contact angle of zero — there is nothing for it to bead up against — so put θ = 0 into W = γ(1 + cos θ) and you get the work of cohesion:

$$W_{\rm medium} \;=\; \gamma\,(1+\cos 0) \;=\; 2\gamma$$

That is the ceiling of eq. 3.4.2. No solid can beat it, because every solid has θ > 0 and therefore offers less than 2γ. For medium:

2.5 · When forward pipetting simply fails

Everything so far has assumed the liquid does what the plunger says, when the plunger says it. Four sections of surfaces and films, and every one of them treated the liquid as arriving instantly. For water that is fine. For the things this lab actually moves — serum-heavy medium, gels, anything on ice — it is not, and two separate instructions on every guidance sheet exist because of it.

"Wait one second" — the last rule without a reason

Release the plunger and the liquid does not jump into the tip. It has to be pushed there by the pressure difference, through a narrow bore, against its own viscosity — and the thing doing the pushing is a compressible gas. That is an RC circuit with the parts renamed. Poiseuille gives the resistance of the bore, and the trapped air gives the compliance:

eq. 3.2.3 $$\tau_{\rm flow} \;=\; R\,C \;=\; \frac{8\mu L_{\rm bore}}{\pi r^{4}} \cdot \frac{V_{\rm cushion}}{P_0}$$

Both halves are things you have already met: the μ is §2.2's viscosity, and the Vcushion/P₀ is the same compressibility that made §1.2's cushion expand when your hand warmed it. Put a 200 µL tip's numbers in and the answer is not what the rule implies:

Liquidμτ3τ — practically settled

Medium + 10% serum4 mPa·s14 ms0.04 s
Matrigel on ice100 mPa·s339 ms1.02 s
"Wait one second" is three time constants of the most viscous thing you handle. For PBS the rule is wasting your time by a factor of a hundred — and it is still correct to teach, because the alternative is asking a new student to estimate a viscosity before every aspiration. What matters is knowing which case it was written for: the one-second wait is a Matrigel rule that everyone applies to everything, and if you ever find yourself rushing it, rush it on the PBS.

Note that τ goes as 1/r⁴. Halve the orifice — a finer tip, a partially blocked one, a tip pressed against the bottom of the tube as in §2.3 — and the settling time goes up sixteen-fold. That is why "never rest the tip on the vessel floor" and "wait one second" are the same instruction arriving from two directions.

And when waiting is not enough

Viscous liquids also do not leave when you tell them to — they cling, and foaming ones trap air on the way out. For those there is a different stroke, and it is worth learning properly because the lab uses it on every gel and every serum-heavy reagent.

3.1 · Don't leave it open

There are only two rules in this tab. Don't leave anything open longer than you need to, and don't let the tip touch anything but the medium. Everything else people tell you about sterile technique is one of those two wearing a costume.

First, why a count is the only thing worth talking about

Both rules are about the same quantity: how many organisms get in. Not a risk score, not a feeling — a count. And there is one fact that makes the count the whole story. A flask of medium at 37 °C is a device for growing things, and it does not care how many it started with:

eq. 3.3.1 $$N(t) \;=\; N_{0}\,2^{\,t/t_{d}}$$

A bacterial contaminant doubles about every half hour. Ten millilitres of medium goes visibly cloudy at roughly 107 cells/mL, so 108 cells. From a single organism that is 27 doublings — 13 hours. From ten thousand organisms it is 13 doublings — 6.6 hours.

A ten-thousand-fold difference in dose buys you six and a half hours. Both flasks are cloudy when you come in tomorrow. So "how much got in" is very nearly not a question; "did anything get in at all" is the entire question. That is why everything below is a count of founders and a probability that the count reached one, and never a severity score.

This section is the first rule: open something, point it up, and it starts collecting. Two different things arrive, and they are worth doing together because the interesting answer is which one wins.

An open vessel drains a column of air

Air holds particles and particles fall. Lesson 1 did both halves — a terminal velocity where drag balances weight:

eq. 1.1.1 from Lesson 1 $$v_s \;=\; \frac{(\rho_p-\rho_f)\,g\,d^{2}}{18\,\eta}$$

About 3 mm/s for a 10 µm carrier particle — note the d², the only strong dependence in it. Then the number arriving in an opening of area A over a time t, from air at concentration C:

eq. 1.1.4 from Lesson 1 $$N_{\rm air} \;=\; C\,v_s\,A\,t \;=\; C \cdot \underbrace{(A\,v_s\,t)}_{\text{a volume}}$$

Grouped that way it stops being an abstraction. Avst is a volume of air — a column standing on the opening, growing upward at vs, and everything in it is going to land inside. That is what the simulation draws. A 60 mm dish sweeps out 60 cm of height in three and a half minutes and drains 2.5 litres in five, and the count is simply how many organisms were in those 2.5 litres.

Which is where C arrives, and it is the largest single number in this lesson. The box drawn beside the dish holds 80 litres of air. On the open bench that box contains about three viable organisms. In a running cabinet it contains 0.008 — the box is, on average, empty. Same column, same vs, same dish: the cabinet does not change how much air you drain, it changes what is in it.

You, at ten centimetres

Now put a hand over the opening. A hand is not part of the room average — it is a local source at short range, and the geometry is completely different. Take a source shedding s particles per second, spreading over a hemisphere of radius H; the opening's share is just its share of that hemisphere:

eq. 3.3.2 $$N_{\rm source} \;=\; s\,t_{\rm over}\,\frac{A}{2\pi H^{2}}$$

The H² is the control, and it is quadratic: double the clearance and the dose quarters, for free, without slowing down or changing anything else you were doing. That is a far more useful rule than "never", because it tells you what to do when you genuinely cannot avoid passing over something.

Turning a count into a decision

Both terms are fractions of one organism, and a fraction of an organism never arrives. What a fractional count means is a probability, and for rare independent arrivals with expectation N that probability is Poisson:

eq. 3.3.3 $$P(\text{at least one}) \;=\; 1-e^{-N}$$

Two things follow, and the whole shape of the first rule is in them. When N ≪ 1, P ≈ N: the risk is linear in the clock, so halving the open time halves it, and there is no threshold below which you are safe. And when N ≫ 1, P saturates at one and the count stops mattering at all — which is exactly the regime §3.2 lives in.

Here is why the two arrival terms belong in one picture. They do not just differ in size — they swap places depending on where you are standing. On the open bench the air dominates and your hand is a rounding error on top of it. Inside a running cabinet the air term all but vanishes, and the same three-second reach-over becomes eighty times everything else combined. The cabinet does not make you safe. It removes your competition and promotes you to the main source. Every rule about how you move your hands exists because of that promotion.
  1. Start with nothing overhead, in the cabinet, and just watch one play. Act one time-lapses the five minutes; the blue column is the air the mouth has drained. Read the litres.
  2. Count the purple dots in the box. There are none — that is 0.008 organisms in 80 litres. Now untick the cabinet: three appear, and the odds move by a factor of four hundred.
  3. Back into the cabinet, and put a fresh gloved hand over it at 10 cm for three seconds. Act two runs at real time — watch how short it is against the act before it, then read which bar is longer.
  4. Raise the hand to 20 cm. The dose should fall by four, not two — that is the H² in eq. 3.3.2.
  5. Now untick the cabinet again with the hand still there. The ratio inverts. Explain to yourself why the same hand matters less on the bench.
  6. Switch to a street-clothes cuff at 10 cm. It is the only setting where one three-second event is worth more than one whole organism, and the pip grid fills up.
  7. Drag the particle size from 10 to 20 µm. The column grows four times faster (d² in eq. 1.1.1) and the air term follows; the source term does not move. Why not?

3.2 · Don't let the tip touch anything

§3.1 spent five minutes of open time and 2.5 litres of drained air to reach a few ten-thousandths of one founder. Here is what a contact lasting a tenth of a second is worth against that.

Every surface that is not sterile carries organisms at some areal density σ, in CFU/cm². Touch it with a wet tip over a contact patch of area Ac, and some fraction f of what that patch covered comes with you:

eq. 3.3.4 $$N_{0} \;=\; f\,\sigma\,A_{c}$$

There is no dispersion in it, no distance, no time — it is an area times a density, which is why the simulation can draw it directly: a patch of surface speckled at σ, and a circle of area Ac landing on it. What the circle covers is what goes in.

The number you cannot know

Now the part that decides the shape of the rule. σ is not one number, it is a range, and the published ranges are wide:

The wiped deck of the cabinet≈ 1 CFU/cm², and anywhere from 0.03 to 30 depending on whether anyone has set something down on it since it was wiped.
The neck of the bottle≈ 10 CFU/cm², range 1 to 100. It has been handled, and it lives in a fridge.
Your own glove, mid-session≈ 50 CFU/cm², range 5 to 500. A glove is only as clean as the last thing it touched.
Your sleeve, or the bench≈ 700 CFU/cm², range 100 to 5000. Fabric and open benchtop are in a different league.

Read the extremes of that table together. A one-square-millimetre brush against a freshly wiped deck delivers about 0.003 founders — twelve times the whole of §3.1's five open minutes, but still small. The same brush against a sleeve delivers two, and at the top of that range fifteen. So one gesture spans from "almost certainly nothing" to "certain", and the two surfaces look identical. You cannot tell which one you touched by looking at it, and you cannot tell afterwards either.

That is why the simulation plots a count and not a probability. A probability axis saturates: everything from two founders upward reads as "100%" and the whole right-hand end of the picture collapses into one point. The count keeps going, and it has the only threshold that matters marked on it — one.

That is why this rule is a geometry and not a number. §3.1 gave you a quantity you can measure and trade against being in a hurry: the clock. Here the quantity is a surface density you cannot measure, spanning three decades, on a surface that gives you no clue which decade it is in. A rule that has to work without that information can only be the flat one: the tip touches nothing but the medium (§2.4). And note what it costs to follow — nothing. The open-time rule has a floor, because you have to open the vessel to do the work. This one has a floor of zero: every contact is optional. That is the whole reason one rule is "minimise" and the other is "never".

And then the exponential, which does not care

Whatever got in now grows by eq. 3.3.1, so the moment it becomes something you can see is set by how far it has to climb:

eq. 3.3.5 $$t_{\rm vis} \;=\; t_{d}\,\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{c_{\rm vis}V}{N_{0}}\right)$$

It is a logarithm of the dose, which is the second half of why the count barely matters. The chart draws your contact against a single founder, and the two curves are parallel lines a few hours apart. What the dose changed was not the outcome, only the hour you find out about it — and if N0 is below one the two curves land on top of each other exactly, because a contamination that happens at all starts from one organism however it arrived.

Which leaves one organism that breaks the picture. Mycoplasma never becomes visible at all — no turbidity, no pH crash, no cells you can spot down the microscope. eq. 3.3.5 has no solution for it, so the horizontal line disappears from the chart and nothing bounds the spread except the interval between tests.

  2. Step through the four surfaces without touching a slider. Watch the marker walk along the founders axis. How many decades did it travel, and which surfaces cross the line at one?
  3. Stay on your sleeve and read the band, not the dot. Where does the bottom of it sit? Is there any reading of that surface on which nothing got in?
  4. Now drag the transfer fraction from 100% down to 5%. It is the most uncertain number in eq. 3.3.4 — and watch how little the verdict cares. Why not?
  5. Set the patch to 20 mm² — a tip laid against something rather than brushing it. Compare the founders with a 0.2 mm² touch: it is a straight ratio of areas.
  6. On the growth chart, compare the two curves. Read both times, then work out how many hours ten thousand founders bought over one.
  7. Switch the organism to mycoplasma. Nothing about your technique changed. What disappeared from the chart, and what replaced it?

Worked example — the 10 µL sample, and why the manufacturer keeps mentioning it
  1. Write what you know. You need 10 µL. On the bench are a 0.5–10 µL pipettor and a 10–100 µL one. Accuracy degrades away from nominal roughly as the square root of the range ratio.
  2. Assemble the systematic term.
    $$V_{\rm sys} \;\approx\; k\sqrt{R\,V_{\rm set}}\,,\qquad k \approx 0.008$$
  3. Evaluate both devices.
    $$V_{\rm sys}^{(10)} = 0.008\sqrt{10\times10}=0.08\ \mu\mathrm{L} \qquad V_{\rm sys}^{(100)} = 0.008\sqrt{100\times10}=0.25\ \mu\mathrm{L}$$
  4. Convert to what you care about. 0.8% versus 2.5% of the volume you wanted.
Three times the error, for free, from picking up the wrong one of two devices sitting side by side. Corning's guidance is exactly this case: "when you want to pipet a 10 µL sample, use a pipettor with the 0.5 to 10 µL volume range rather than a pipettor with the 10 to 100 µL range." The habit is: look at the number on the plunger before you pick it up.

An assumption from this lesson that expires

Everything in Tabs 1–4 treated contamination as something that happens to a vessel you are working on. That is the right frame for a media change. It is the wrong frame for a stock: a stock bottle is not one vessel, it is every experiment that will ever be run from it, and the question stops being "did I contaminate this?" and becomes "how much did I put at risk?"
