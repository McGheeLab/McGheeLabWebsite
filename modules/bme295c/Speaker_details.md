# Prosthetic, Bionic, and Beyond. Engineering and Controlling the Next Generation of Robotic Limbs

**Mario I. Romero-Ortega, Ph.D.**
*Department Head and Professor, Biomedical Engineering, University of Arizona*

Robotic limbs have advanced from passive mechanical replacements to devices integrated with the human nervous system, yet the terms *prosthetic* and *bionic* are often conflated. This talk draws a clear architectural distinction. Prosthetics restore form and gross function, while bionic limbs are bidirectionally coupled to the user, driven by neural intent and capable of returning touch, force, and proprioception. I will survey the spectrum of control strategies, from surface EMG and targeted muscle reinnervation to intrafascicular arrays, microchannel devices, and our laboratory's regenerative peripheral neural interfaces that allow axons to grow through the electrode itself. I will then contrast upper-limb engineering, where dexterity and sensory feedback dominate, with lower-limb engineering, where dynamics, load-bearing, and gait control take precedence. The talk closes with the remaining barriers, including chronic stability, wireless power, and regulatory pathways that continue to gate clinical translation.

# Imperceptible by Design. Next-Generation Wearables for Continuous Diagnostics and Closed-Loop Therapy

**Philipp Gutruf, Ph.D.**
*Associate Professor and Associate Department Head, Biomedical Engineering, University of Arizona*

Wearables are finally crossing the threshold from consumer gadgets to clinical-grade diagnostic and therapeutic devices. This talk examines the convergence that made automated diagnosis possible, including soft skin-conformal materials, wireless power, on-device machine learning, and continuous longitudinal data streams, and argues that the deciding factor for long-term adoption is *imperceptibility*, meaning a device the user never has to think about. I will present our laboratory's biosymbiotic platform, soft 3D-printed mesh wearables that conform to the individual, harvest energy wirelessly, and transmit clinical-grade signals to clinicians miles away. I will then introduce osseosurface electronics, thin wireless devices that interface directly with bone to record the strain and mechanical loading history that drives fracture healing, giving orthopedic surgeons an objective, continuous readout in place of episodic radiographs. The talk closes with fully implanted, battery-free platforms for closed-loop neurostimulation, cardiac pacing, and functional electrical stimulation.

# From Biopsy to Bedside Microscopy. Making Cellular Diagnosis Available Everywhere

**Dongkyun "DK" Kang, Ph.D.**
*Associate Professor, Wyant College of Optical Sciences and Department of Biomedical Engineering, University of Arizona*

A definitive cancer diagnosis still depends on a pathologist viewing stained cells under a microscope, a workflow that requires biopsy, fixation, sectioning, and days of laboratory infrastructure that simply does not exist in most of the world. This talk first reviews the standard histopathology pipeline and its real costs, including diagnostic delay, sampling error, and the inability to monitor the same cells over time. I will then introduce in vivo microscopy, including confocal, multiphoton, and light-sheet techniques, that image living tissue at cellular resolution without excision, replacing physical sectioning with optical sectioning and enabling real-time, point-of-care diagnosis. Finally, I will present our laboratory's smartphone confocal microscope, which uses a slit aperture and diffraction grating on a consumer phone to perform confocal imaging without any beam-scanning hardware. I will share results from our NIH-funded program diagnosing Kaposi's sarcoma in Uganda and extensions to cervical, oral, and skin cancer screening.

# Convergence of Engineering in Medicine

**Frederic Zenhausern, Ph.D., MBA**
*Professor of Basic Medical Sciences and Director of the Center for Applied NanoBioscience and Medicine, University of Arizona College of Medicine – Phoenix*

This talk introduces students to the converging frontier where engineering, nanoscience, and molecular medicine meet to reshape clinical practice. I will first describe how biomedical research is conducted in the United States and outline its three main funding pillars, which include federal agencies (NIH, NSF, and DoD/DARPA), foundations and philanthropic organizations, and industry partnerships with venture capital. I will then identify three research priorities for the next 5 to 10 years, including precision and predictive diagnostics built on point-of-care molecular sensors, personalized therapeutics enabled by genomics and nanomedicine, and AI-augmented decision support that extends advanced care into resource-limited settings. Finally, I will walk through the translational pipeline, including discovery, preclinical validation, regulatory strategy, clinical trials, and commercialization, drawing on our Center for Applied NanoBioscience and Medicine's experience converting bench-top inventions into deployable diagnostic platforms. Convergence is the operative word, and medicine's future is being engineered now.

# Optical Imaging for Early Detection of Ovarian Cancer

**Jennifer Barton, Ph.D.**
*Professor, Biomedical Engineering and Wyant College of Optical Sciences, University of Arizona*

Making a decision whether to have a prophylactic (preventative) surgery is difficult. Would you choose to lose your ability to conceive children naturally, and be reliant on hormones pills and patches for health, to nearly eliminate a 15% chance of getting likely deadly ovarian cancer? # Modeling the Tumor Microenvironment with Organoids and Organs-on-Chip

**Jerome Lacombe, Ph.D.**
*Assistant Professor, Center for Applied NanoBioscience and Medicine, University of Arizona College of Medicine – Phoenix*

This lecture will present the biochemical and mechanical interactions that take place within the tumor microenvironment, and how they contribute to cancer development, progression, and response to therapy. It will then introduce emerging biological models, such as tumor organoids and organ-on-chip systems, designed to more faithfully reproduce these complex conditions than traditional cell culture methods. By better capturing the cellular diversity, spatial organization, and physical forces present in real tumors, these models provide more accurate insights into cancer biology and significantly improve how we study human tumor behavior and responses to therapy.

# Brain MRI for Neurodegenerative Disorders and the Grand Health Challenge of Detecting Neurodegeneration Before It's Too Late

**Elizabeth Hutchinson, Ph.D.**
*Associate Professor, Biomedical Engineering, University of Arizona*

Neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's Disease are devastating to patients, caregivers and families. They also present a large a growing economic burden to the healthcare system and individuals affected with these disorders. If diagnostic tools can be developed to sensitively and specifically detect AD pathology at the earliest stages, then there is the potential for preventative treatment and improved outcomes, but currently the majority of diagnosis for AD is made at later stages when cognition and daily life are already affected. In combination with other clinical diagnostic tools, brain imaging and especially magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can play an important role in the diagnosis and monitoring of AD. In this talk, the development of novel diffusion MRI approaches to improve non-invasive detection of AD pathology in the brain will be explored. Diffusion MRI uses the MRI signal to sensitively measure the magnitude and shape of water diffusion as a probe of cell and tissue features such as cell density, edema, white matter tract connections among others. We will discuss the basic principles that enable this technology, innovations that improve the capabilities of diffusion MRI and the role that it may play in clinical diagnosis of AD and other neurodegenerative disorders.

**Discussion Questions**

- How could different types of diagnostics (e.g. clinical exam, blood markers, imaging) be used together to best serve patients?
- If we had the ability to use diffusion MRI to accurately report any shape or size feature of cellular structures what would be important or interesting to target in neurodegenerative diseases?
- What are the ethics of performing diagnostics when there is no effective prevention, treatment or cure?

# Developing 3D In Vitro Models of Cancer via Cellular Assembly and 3D Bioprinting

**Alexander McGhee, Ph.D.**
*Assistant Professor, Biomedical Engineering, University of Arizona*

In vitro models give us something animal studies and patient trials cannot, namely a controllable, observable, repeatable system for asking precise mechanistic questions about cancer. The future of these models is one in which every patient's tumor is reconstructed in a dish to test therapy *before* it is given. I will confront a fundamental problem in targeted therapeutics. Drugs are typically developed against 2D cell monolayers that lack the architecture, stromal cells, and mechanical forces of real tumors, which is why so many compounds that look promising in flat culture fail in the body. The second half of the talk turns to 3D bioprinting and its persistent issues, including resolution limits, cell viability, and recapitulating native heterogeneity, and shows how harnessing the *collective self-assembly* behavior of cells lets us build tissue models the printer alone cannot.
