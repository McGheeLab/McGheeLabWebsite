A.1 · The question — two resistances in series

"Cell surface receptors, because they are restricted to the plasma membrane, are clustered in space. If diffusion can limit the rate of ligand-receptor interaction, theory predicts clustering to have the effect of reducing both the forward and reverse rate constants for ligand-receptor binding. The forward rate constant is reduced because nearby receptors compete for the same ligand and depress its concentration locally."

The paper's ligand is a hapten — a small molecule that an antibody can bind but that is too small to provoke an immune response on its own. Here it is a dye called DCT, dissolved in the bath around the cell. A hapten in the bath has two jobs before it counts as bound: reach the cell, then find an antibody site on it. Each job has its own rate. The first is Week 2's Smoluchowski rate for a sphere, and it does not care how many sites the cell carries. The second is the intrinsic rate of one site times the number of sites, and it cares about nothing else.

Which job limits the cell is the whole paper. Erickson and colleagues changed the number of IgE molecules on a rat basophilic leukemia (RBL) cell and watched the per-site rate constant respond. Before you see their data, build the picture: a sphere of radius a whose surface reflects hapten everywhere except on N small absorbing disks of radius s.

One convention before you touch anything. The paper counts N in Fab combining sites; each IgE carries two, so IgE = N/2. The cell radius the paper fits is a = 4 µm. Both conventions are printed wherever they are used below.

What the paper says — and the equation the sim just measured

Two steps, two rates

The paper describes binding as "a two-step process: (a) diffusion of ligand to the vicinity of the surface with forward rate constant 4πDa followed by (b) adsorption at the surface by a specific receptor with forward rate constant κon" (p. 657, citing Shoup and Szabo). Two steps in sequence add their times, and a rate constant is an inverse time. So the whole-cell rate constant obeys

eq. R1.A.1$$\frac{1}{k^{c}} = \frac{1}{4\pi D a} + \frac{1}{N\kappa}$$

The first term is the Smoluchowski rate you met in Week 2 — a fully absorbing sphere collects hapten at

eq. 1.5.1 again$$k_{\mathrm{S}} = 4\pi D a$$

and the second is N independent sites each catching at the intrinsic rate κ. Invert eq. R1.A.1 and divide by N: that is the paper's Eq. 1, written per site and per cell.

eq. R1.A.2$$k_{\mathrm{on}} = \frac{4\pi D a\,\kappa}{4\pi D a + N\kappa}, \qquad k^{c} = N k_{\mathrm{on}}$$

Everything the sim showed you is in one dimensionless ratio, the reaction step's capacity over the diffusion step's:

eq. R1.A.3$$x \equiv \frac{N\kappa}{4\pi D a}, \qquad k_{\mathrm{on}} = \frac{\kappa}{1+x}, \qquad k^{c} = 4\pi D a\,\frac{x}{1+x}$$

For \(x \ll 1\) the per-site rate is κ and nobody competes; the paper's words are "when 4πDa ≫ Nκon then kon → κon" (p. 657). For \(x \gg 1\) the whole cell binds like an absorbing sphere: "Nkon → 4πDa … increases in receptor density beyond this will have very little effect" (p. 658). The pinched iso-lines were the first regime; the smooth shell was the second.

Where Week 3's formula came from

The paper adds one more line. If each site is a perfectly absorbing disk of radius s on a plane, its intrinsic rate is Hill's 4Ds. Put κ = 4Ds into eq. R1.A.2, cancel 4D, and

eq. 3.3.12 again$$k^{c} = 4\pi D a\,\frac{Ns}{\pi a + Ns}$$

which is Berg and Purcell's receptor formula from Week 3, "recovered" (p. 658). The paper does not make that substitution in its fit; it lets κ float, so its κ is a measured chemical rate, not a geometric one. The natural scale of N is where the two resistances are equal, \(x = 1\):

eq. R1.A.4$$N_{1/2} = \frac{4\pi D a}{\kappa}, \qquad k^{c}(N_{1/2}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,4\pi D a$$

About 2.7 × 105 Fab, or 1.3 × 105 IgE, brings the cell to half the Smoluchowski rate — the paper's "half the maximum possible rate of binding DCT … by having ~105 IgE receptors" (p. 661). Those disks cover about 5 % of the surface. Half of the diffusion limit for a twentieth of the area: that is the point of Week 3's formula, and here it is measured.

A.2 · The measurement — the quench bench

"Under these conditions, kon is apparently a decreasing function of [Fab]bulk and also therefore of N. This trend is in qualitative agreement with the intuitive notion of adjacent receptors competing for available ligand at the cell surface and consequently reducing the observed kon per receptor."

The instrument is a fluorimeter. FITC-labelled IgE loses about a fifth of its fluorescence when a DNP hapten sits in the combining site, so a quench trace is an occupancy trace. Hapten is injected into a stirred cuvette of sensitized cells ("mixing time … <2 s", p. 658), the signal is averaged every 0.5 s, and the decay is fitted to one forward rate constant with the dissociation rate tied to the known equilibrium constant.

The paper's trick is in how N was varied: cells were loaded with different amounts of anti-DNP IgE, and two control series (IgE in solution over blocked cells; fixed loading, varied cell count) showed that nothing but the surface density moved the rate. What the paper says — and how far it got

The stopping point

The paper's inset "plots the same data points as in Fig. 3 where the kon values have been modified by multiplication by N … to illustrate graphically that in the limit of large N, kc approaches 4πDa" (p. 661). A.4 · Synthesis — the paper in our words

Erickson, Goldstein, Holowka and Baird showed that antibody sites on a cell compete for hapten exactly as a two-step, series-rate picture says: the per-site forward rate constant falls with the number of sites, the whole-cell rate heads toward the Smoluchowski value 4πDa, and the N → 0 limit of the cell data lands on the rate measured for the same IgE free in solution. Two control series rule out bulk concentration and cell number as the cause. The evidence is Fig. 3 — about two decades in N, points scattered 10–20 % about a two-parameter curve — and it reaches four fifths of the plateau, never the plateau, because an RBL cell carries at most about 106 IgE.

What they assumed rather than measured: a diffusion coefficient for DCT of 10−5 cm2/s, a spherical cell, sites spread evenly and acting independently, a reverse rate tied to the forward rate through an equilibrium constant measured earlier, and rate constants that do not drift as free sites are used up. The first assumption is the one that turns the fitted composite 4πDa into a radius; the paper calls the 4 µm it yields "reasonable". Your report redoes that step from your card — a temperature, a density, a hydration shell — and follows where the radius goes.

The one equation this tab has not yet written is the one the paper skipped. A small molecule's diffusion coefficient follows from its size through Week 1's mobility argument, and the fitted composite then hands back a radius:

eq. R1.A.7$$D = \frac{k_{B}T}{6\pi\eta\, r_H}\ \ (\text{Stokes–Einstein again}), \qquad a_{\mathrm{imp}} = \frac{(4\pi D a)_{\mathrm{fit}}}{4\pi D}$$

Halve D and the implied radius doubles; the fitted κ does not move at all, because it is the intercept of eq. R1.A.5 and D never enters it. The paper's inverse route is a check you can run in one line: its D = 10−5 cm2/s at 25 °C, where water's viscosity is 0.89 mPa·s (Notes table), corresponds through eq. R1.A.7 to rH = 4.11 × 10−21 J / (6π × 0.89 × 10−3 Pa·s × 10−9 m2/s) ≈ 0.25 nm — about the size of a single small ion. DCT is a 460 g/mol molecule with a hydration shell; you will find it larger, its D smaller, and the paper's 4 µm cell correspondingly larger. Whether that helps or hurts the paper's self-consistency depends on how big an RBL cell really is, which is your call to make and defend.

One consequence the paper states but does not measure: the same competition that lowers the forward rate lowers the reverse rate, because a hapten leaving one site has probability x/(1+x) of being caught by a neighbour before it escapes the cell — at the paper's furthest point, four chances in five. What the model cannot say

  • Pairs, not points. IgE is bivalent, so sites arrive two at a time. The paper notes that this "might be expected by itself to lower the intrinsic forward rate constant by a factor of <ln 2" (p. 660, citing Goldstein and Wiegel) and did not test it with Fab fragments. The fitted κ may be up to 1.4× below a true single-site value.
  • Independent disks. Berg–Purcell disks do not see each other's depletion zones. Zwanzig's effective-medium correction replaces κ by \(\kappa/(1-p_A)\) with \(p_A\) the covered fraction; at the paper's own 4 % coverage that is a 4 % lift in the reaction step and, through x/(1+x), roughly 2 % in the whole-cell rate — under Fig. 3's scatter. The [566] question asks you to carry this through with your card's numbers.
  • Constant rate constants. The paper's Eq. 5 "ignores the time dependence of the rate constants due to the decreasing free receptor density as binding progresses" (p. 659); occupancy was kept below 0.5 to blunt it, and an early-time analysis "did not change the values of kon significantly".
  • The reverse rate. koff was not fitted but set to kon/Ka, with Ka from an earlier study of the same cells. That is self-consistent only if diffusion scales both rates by the same factor — which the series picture says it does — and if Ka was measured under the same conditions.
  • A still bath, a spherical cell. The cuvette was stirred and RBL cells are not spheres. The paper's own defence is that Eq. 1 "should hold for any shaped cell" with 4πDa replaced by the right diffusion-limited constant (p. 658) — which is to say the fitted composite is a rate, and only becomes a radius through an assumption.
  • Two steps in series add their times: 1/kc = 1/(4πDa) + 1/(Nκ). Everything else is algebra on that line.
  • One ratio decides the regime: x = Nκ/4πDa. Below one, chemistry limits and kon ≈ κ; above one, diffusion limits and kc → 4πDa.
  • Half the diffusion limit costs about 5 % of the surface. That is why cells can afford many receptor systems at once.
  • The paper reached x ≈ 4, i.e. 80 % of the plateau; certifying a plateau needs x above ten, which the biology did not allow.
  • The paper's κ is measured; its cell radius is inferred through an assumed D. Count N in Fab and treat D as yours to defend.

B.1 · Two phases, two kinds of physics

"Traditionally, separate experiments estimate the mobility of actin monomer or the lifetime of actin filaments. A mathematical description of the dynamics of the actin cytoskeleton, however, predicts that the evolution of fluorescence in PAF and FRAP experiments depends simultaneously on the diffusion coefficient of actin monomer, D, the fraction of actin in filaments, FF, and the lifetime of actin filaments, τ" … "Control experiments confirm the modeling assumption that the evolution of fluorescence is dominated by the diffusion of actin monomer, and the cyclic turnover of actin filaments, but not by filament diffusion."

Two acronyms from that quotation, before anything else. FRAP is fluorescence recovery after photobleaching: you destroy the fluorescence in a stripe of the cell with a strong laser pulse, then watch brightness return to it. PAF is photoactivation of fluorescence, the same experiment upside down — you switch a stripe on and watch it fade. This module uses the FRAP version throughout.

So: bleach a band across a cell full of labelled actin and watch it refill. Two things refill it. Monomers walk in from both sides within seconds — that is Week 1's random walk, and its clock is set by the band width and D. The filaments inside the band cannot walk anywhere; their fluorescence comes back only as dark subunits fall off and bright ones from the monomer pool take their place, one exchange at a time, and that clock is the filament lifetime τ.

The paper's claim is that one recovery curve carries both clocks and the split between them, provided you write both species into the model at once. Older analyses did not: they fit one diffusing species and called the rest "immobile".

What the paper says — and the equation the sim just measured

Two species, one balance each

Call \(C_m(x,t)\) the labelled monomer and \(C_f(x,t)\) the labelled subunits sitting in filaments. Monomer diffuses (Fick's first law, eq. 1.3.5 again, \(J = -D\,\partial C_m/\partial x\)) and is drawn on whenever a filament swaps a subunit; filaments do not diffuse and change only by that swap. At steady state the polymer pool loses and gains a subunit every τ on average, so the exchange flux per unit volume is \((C_m - C_f)/\tau\) written in filament units — and since the monomer pool is γ times smaller than the polymer pool, the same flux is γτ-scaled in monomer units:

eq. R1.B.1$$\frac{\partial C_m}{\partial t} = D\,\frac{\partial^2 C_m}{\partial x^2} - \frac{C_m - C_f}{\gamma\,\tau}$$
eq. R1.B.2$$\frac{\partial C_f}{\partial t} = \frac{C_m - C_f}{\tau}$$
eq. R1.B.3$$\gamma \equiv \frac{\text{monomer}}{\text{polymer}} = \frac{1-FF}{FF}$$

These are Tardy's equations (Tardy et al. 1995) with no-flux ends at 0 and L. What the microscope integrates over the band is the total label, monomer plus polymer, each weighted by the share of actin it holds. Writing \(C^*\) for the bleach deficit in each species (so FRAP recovery is 1 − deficit):

eq. R1.B.4$$\frac{F}{F_0} = 1 - \big[(1-FF)\,C^*_m + FF\,C^*_f\big]$$

The paper's Eq. 1 reads \(F/F_0 = (C^*_m + \gamma C^*_f)/(1+\gamma)\) and defines γ as "the local ratio of actin monomer to polymer" (p. 2072). Before you use eq. R1.B.4, write down which phase the printed γ multiplies and which phase a monomer-to-polymer ratio ought to weight, then decide for yourself whether Eq. 1 as printed and its own definition of γ agree. The sim weights the phases by (1 − FF, FF); your written solutions say what you did with Eq. 1 and why.

The two clocks

Set τ → ∞ and eq. R1.B.1 is Week 1's diffusion equation on a band. Its centre recovers half its fast amplitude after a fixed multiple of the diffusion time \(w^2/D\); for a sharp band of width w that multiple is 0.275:

eq. R1.B.5$$t_{1/2} = 0.275\,\frac{w^2}{D}, \qquad t_{\mathrm{diff}} \equiv \frac{w^2}{D}$$

Set D → ∞ instead and eq. R1.B.2 says the filament deficit relaxes as \(e^{-t/\tau}\) toward a monomer pool that is already uniform. Nothing in that phase knows about w. So the band width is a knob on one clock only — which is exactly why a sweep of w can separate them.

The referee

Week 3's Damköhler number compares a reaction rate with a transport rate over the same distance (eq. 3.3.9 again). Here the "reaction" is subunit exchange at filament ends, rate 1/τ, and transport is monomer diffusion across the band, rate D/w². Their ratio is the paper's βw:

eq. R1.B.7$$\beta_w \equiv \frac{w^2}{\tau\,D} = \frac{1/\tau}{D/w^2} = \mathrm{Da}$$

βw ≪ 1: monomer crosses the band many times before a filament swaps a subunit, so exchange limits and the two phases sit far apart. βw ≫ 1: filaments swap faster than monomer can arrive, the phases merge, and the paper's words are "diffusion-limited filament turnover" (p. 2077). Because eq. R1.B.5 gives the fast clock and τ the slow one, any single kept row already carries βw without knowing D or τ separately:

eq. R1.B.8$$\frac{t_{1/2}}{t_s} = \frac{0.275\,w^2/D}{\tau} = 0.275\,\beta_w \quad\Rightarrow\quad \beta_w = \frac{t_{1/2}}{0.275\,t_s}$$

The paper's own reading of the keratocyte result is the second one: "the disparity between the PAF experiments of Theriot and Mitchison and other studies may indicate a difference in the ratio of sequestered to polymerized actin between the cell body and the lamellapodia" (p. 2077). That is a claim about FF, not about βw — and this slide's filament fraction is hidden.

Why the slow time you just read is not the lifetime

The two species are coupled, and that is what the two-phase fit ignores. Solve the pair of equations mode by mode and each spatial mode has two decay rates, not one; the slower of the two is not 1/τ but

eq. R1.B.9$$t_{\text{slow}} \;=\; \tau\left(1+\varepsilon\right),\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{1}{\tau\,(1-\mathrm{FF})\,D\,q^{2}},\qquad q=\frac{2\pi}{L}$$

ε is the exchange rate divided by the rate at which a monomer crosses the cell. In the cell body it is small and the fit is safe. In a lamellipodium it is not: with a 20 µm slide, a thirty-second lifetime, a high filament fraction and a slow monomer, ε approaches and exceeds one — so the reported time can be half again, or double, the true lifetime. The error has a sign. Coupling always drains the fast phase and slows the slow one, so the reported time is too long and the reported filament fraction too low, never the other way round. That is why a bound is defensible even when a value is not: whatever your slide reported, the true lifetime is shorter than that.

Notice what this does to the paper’s own argument. McGrath and colleagues read the keratocyte’s single-phase recovery as evidence about the filament fraction — too little free monomer to make a fast phase. But a merged recovery is also exactly what coupling produces when ε is of order one, with any filament fraction at all. The observation does not separate the two explanations, and neither does this bench. Say so in your written solutions rather than picking one.

B.4 · Synthesis — the paper in our words

McGrath and colleagues bleached or uncaged a band across the body of an endothelial cell and found the recovery biphasic every time: a fast phase over about ten seconds and a slow one over minutes. Fitting Tardy's two-species model — diffusing monomer exchanging subunits with a fixed polymer — to each curve returned D, FF and τ at once: for FRAP, 5.8 µm²/s, 0.50 and 4.8 min; for PAF, 3.1 µm²/s, 0.36 and 7.5 min. The drugs moved the parameters the way the model demands: jasplakinolide locked the monomer into filaments and the fast phase all but vanished; cytochalasin D capped barbed ends, lowered FF slightly and lengthened τ about fivefold — the direction expected of exchange at one end only (Wegner), and the opposite of what diffusing fragments would do.

Turnover is fast but not diffusion-limited: βw is a few hundredths. What the authors assumed rather than measured: filaments do not diffuse (tested only through the drugs); bleaching is first-order; the cell is a rectangle of length Leq; and a label on one percent of the actin behaves like the rest — yet their two labels disagree on all three parameters, which they attribute to profilin affinity.

What the model cannot say

  • Whether the slow phase hides short filaments that do diffuse. The model forbids it by construction; the only evidence against it is the direction of the cytochalasin D shift (shortened filaments would have sped the slow phase up, and it slowed down).
  • Whether the label is the technique.   • How much of τ is the bleach correction. The correction assumes first-order photobleaching and its error "accumulates" over exposures (p. 2072), so the long-time decay — τ — carries the bias, while D from the first seconds barely does.
  • What FF is in a lamellipodium. The single-phase keratocyte result is read as a low sequestered pool; this pane's slide can only tell you how close its phases are, not why.

C.1 · One post, one pull

“For small deformations, the deflection of a post is directly proportional to the lateral force applied on its top via a spring constant.” … “The accuracy on the displacement of the top of the pillars was ~40 nm.”

Everything in this paper rests on one substitution. A cell pulls on the top of a silicone post; nobody can see the force, but anyone can see the top move. If the post is a cantilever, the two are proportional, and the constant of proportionality is set entirely by the post's shape and the stiffness of the rubber it is moulded from. Measure a displacement, multiply by that constant, report a force.

That substitution has a price, and the second sentence above is it. Forty nanometres is the floor: any pull that moves the tip less than that is invisible. Notice what happens to that floor as the posts get stiffer — the same 40 nm of blur becomes a larger and larger force. Your own posts are µm in radius and µm tall, moulded from a batch whose modulus — the stiffness of the rubber itself, whatever shape you mould it into — is MPa.

What the paper says — and the equation the sim just measured
eq. R1.C.1$$F=\frac{3\pi E}{4}\,\frac{r^{4}}{L^{3}}\,\Delta x \;=\; k\,\Delta x$$

This is the paper's Eq. 1, and it is Euler–Bernoulli bending with the load at the tip: the fourth power of the radius comes from the second moment of area of a circle, and the cube of the height from integrating the curvature twice along the post. Doubling the radius stiffens the post sixteenfold; doubling the height softens it eightfold. Height is the cheaper knob, and the authors used both — “we varied the dimensions of the posts from 1 to 2 µm in diameter and from 1.6 to 6 µm in height leading to a wide range of spring constants, from 2 to 130 nN/µm” (p. L52).

Two things are worth noticing before you leave this equation. The modulus enters only once and only linearly, so a batch-to-batch error in the rubber shifts every stiffness in the array by the same factor — it moves the whole calibration, not the shape of any result. And the geometry enters to the fourth and third power, so the accuracy of the reported forces rests on how well the posts were measured, not on the force sensor at all.

An exponent of one says constant deflection; an exponent of zero would have said constant force; anything in between says the cell is doing something else and the single-number summary is hiding it.

Why a slope of one is a constant deflection
  1. Write the claim as a power law. Suppose the mean force goes as some power of the stiffness.
    \(\langle F\rangle = C\,k^{\,n}\)
  2. Turn it into a deflection. By eq. R1.C.1 the deflection is the force over the stiffness.
    \(\Delta x=\langle F\rangle/k = C\,k^{\,n-1}\)
  3. Ask when the deflection stops caring about k.
    \(n-1=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; n=1,\quad \Delta x = C\)
  4. Read the intercept as the deflection. On log–log axes the slope-1 line's offset is \(\log C\), and \(C\) is the constant deflection in whatever units force over stiffness carries — nN per nN/µm, which is µm.
So the exponent tests the form of the claim and the offset gives its one number. This is why the module's sweep prints them separately: a good offset with a bad exponent is not a confirmation, it is a coincidence at one stiffness.

C.3 · Where the line would bend

“By increasing the stiffness even more, one expects to reach a plateau but the force resolution on very rigid posts did not allow us to study this regime.”

This is the most honest sentence in the paper, and it is an invitation. The authors know that constant deflection cannot go on forever: a cell is not an infinitely strong displacement source, and on a stiff enough post it must run out of force. Somewhere the slope-1 line has to bend over into a plateau. They say plainly that they could not go there.

You can. This bench hands you a cell whose machinery is itself a spring — a contractile element pulling through its own compliance, in series with the post. A spring in series with a spring is a divider: on posts much softer than the cell, the cell wins and the deflection is whatever the cell can deliver; on posts much stiffer, the post wins and the force saturates at whatever the cell can generate. The stiffness where the two hand over is a property of the cell, and it is hidden from you. Your sweep is how you find it.

Run soft as well as stiff. The soft end fixes the deflection the cell would deliver if nothing resisted it; the stiff end fixes the force it can muster; the half-way point locates the cell's own stiffness. The strip asks for all three because any two of them can be fitted by the wrong model.

Commit now.
  • On posts far softer than the cell's own spring, what does the force do as stiffness rises — and what does the deflection do?
  • On posts far stiffer, which of the two goes flat?
  • The paper's stiffest post is 130 nN/µm. Guess whether a cell stiffness of that same order would have been visible in Fig. 2, and commit to a yes or a no.
  1. Start at the two softest settings, then jump to the two stiffest. You are bracketing, not scanning — and the soft end is not optional: it is what fixes the intercept of the line you are about to read.
  2. Keep going until the strip lights: four usable runs, three settings, a decade of span, and one run at 10 nN/µm or below.
  3. Switch the sweep to stiffness over force against stiffness. A spring in series with a spring is a straight line on those axes — read its slope and its intercept, and work out what each one is before you open the reveal.
  4. Now switch back to the log–log force axis with the paper’s slope-1 line beside your points. Find by eye where your curve first leaves that line by more than the scatter, and compare it with the stiffness your straightened line just gave you.

Two springs in series share a displacement, so the force is set by the smaller stiffness. When the post is soft, \(k\ll k_C\), the force rises in proportion to \(k\) and the deflection is constant at \(F_{\text{sat}}/k_C\) — indistinguishable from the paper's result. The whole of Fig. 2 lives in the first regime if the cell's own stiffness is well above 130 nN/µm.

The authors reach for the same idea in their closing line: these results “question the commonly accepted mechanism of an active feedback based on a probing of the surface stiffness by the cells” and suggest instead “an alternative possible model where a characteristic intracellular length controls the amount of deformation” (p.     \(\dfrac{F_{\text{sat}}\,k/(k+k_C)}{\Delta x_0\,k}=\dfrac{k_C}{k+k_C}=1-\varepsilon\)

    \(k=k_C\,\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\)

C.4 · Synthesis — the paper in our words

Saez and colleagues moulded dense arrays of silicone posts whose stiffness they could tune by shape alone, from 2 to 130 nN/µm, and dropped islands of 10–20 MDCK cells onto them. Tracking each post's top to about 40 nm, they collected every force under an island into a histogram and plotted its mean, and its tail, against stiffness across two decades. Both rose in proportion to stiffness — a slope of one on log–log axes — so the deflection the cells produced was the same everywhere, about 130 ± 20 nm. Post density between 10 and 40 % made no difference. Vinculin staining showed large, elongated focal adhesions only on the stiff posts, with diffuse contacts below an equivalent modulus of about 10 kPa.

What the paper assumes rather than shows: that a post is an isolated tip-loaded cantilever with a rigid base; that the modulus of the rubber is known; that the mean of a very broad histogram is the quantity a cell controls; and that a monolayer edge and its interior can be pooled. What it says plainly it cannot do is reach the stiffnesses where constant deflection must fail — and in that gap, “the cell controls a deformation” and “the cell is a spring stiffer than our stiffest post” are the same claim.

What the model cannot say

  • Which variable the cell holds fixed. Over the stiffness range measured, constant deflection and a cell-spring-in-series are indistinguishable. The paper's own sentence about the plateau is the honest boundary of its claim.
  • What the mean of that histogram means. The distribution is wide and skewed, the tail comes from a different structure (the monolayer edge), and no argument is given that a cell regulates the mean of it.
  • Whether adhesion size causes anything. Fig. 3 correlates big focal adhesions with stiff substrates. Reinforcement could be the cause of the larger force, its consequence, or a co-symptom.
  • Anything about time. Every number here is a steady value. A clutch that engages, loads and slips would produce the same average with a completely different mechanism, and the deflection-over-lifetime rate you compute in C5 is a comparison, not evidence.
  • What the equivalent modulus really is. It inherits the arbitrary contact size, so 1 to 100 kPa should be read as an order of magnitude for comparison with gels, not as a material property of the array.
  • A force sensor made of posts measures a displacement; the force is a calculation, and its accuracy rests on the geometry to the fourth power, not on the sensor.
  • A slope of one on log–log axes tests the form of a claim; the offset is its one number. A good number with a bad exponent is a coincidence.
  • Resolution that scales with the signal never fails visibly — an array of stiffer posts stops resolving differences long before it stops resolving forces.
  • Two springs in series are indistinguishable from one displacement source until the load approaches the hidden spring, so “what the cell controls” is only answerable past where this experiment could reach.

Two rates in series — delivery to a cell, then capture at one of N sites (eq. 1.5.1 again; eq. 3.3.12 again)

$$\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{on}}} = \frac{1}{\kappa} + \frac{N}{4\pi D a} \quad\text{(a straight line in } N\text{)} \qquad\qquad \kappa \to 4Ds \;\Longrightarrow\; k_{\mathrm{cell}} = 4\pi D a\cdot\frac{Ns}{Ns+\pi a}$$
  • Symbols\(4\pi D a\) = the Smoluchowski delivery rate to a perfectly absorbing sphere of radius \(a\), in volume per time per cell · \(\kappa\) = the rate at which one isolated site clears ligand from the volume around it (volume per time per site) · \(N\) = sites per cell · \(x\) = the ratio of the capture rate to the delivery rate, the surface Damköhler number (eq. 3.3.9) · \(4Ds\) = the diffusion-limited rate of an absorbing disk of radius \(s\) on a reflecting plane (Hill 1975; Berg & Purcell 1977).
  • What it saysResistances add. A ligand must first reach the cell (rate \(4\pi D a\)), then find a site (\(N\kappa\)); the slower step limits. Per site, the rate falls as \(N\) grows because neighbours compete for one depleted layer; per cell it rises and saturates. \(x \ll 1\): reaction-limited, \(k_{\mathrm{on}} \to \kappa\) and the cell rate is \(N\kappa\). \(x \gg 1\): diffusion-limited, \(N k_{\mathrm{on}} \to 4\pi D a\) — the whole cell binds like an absorbing sphere. The half-way point is \(x = 1\), i.e. \(N_{1/2} = 4\pi D a/\kappa\); reaching a fraction \(f\) of the plateau needs \(x = f/(1-f)\) — 90 % needs \(x = 9\), not 0.9. On the straightened axes \(1/k_{\mathrm{on}}\) vs \(N\), the intercept is \(1/\kappa\) and the slope is \(1/(4\pi D a)\). Substituting Hill's disk for \(\kappa\) returns the Berg–Purcell receptor formula (eq. 3.3.12): the correction is a ratio of lengths \(Ns\) to \(\pi a\), not of areas, which is why a few percent coverage already captures half of what a perfect absorber would.
  • When it appliesSteady state; a spherical cell; sites spread evenly and independent; the ligand dilute and monovalent; occupancy low enough that the number of free sites is effectively constant. The same argument run backwards gives the fate of a released ligand: it rebinds a neighbour with probability \(x/(1+x)\) and escapes to the bulk with probability \(1/(1+x)\) — the two add to one, and a report needs the right one.
  • Fails when\(N\) is counted in the wrong currency (a bivalent antibody carries two combining sites: sites and molecules differ by two, and a rate per site and a rate per cell differ by \(N\)); a value at one \(N\) is called \(\kappa\) (\(\kappa\) is the \(N \to 0\) intercept, and every read at finite \(N\) sits below it); \(x \approx 1\) is called a "limit" (it is the crossover — neither step dominates until \(x\) is well below 0.5 or well above 2); the units are mixed (rates here are volume per time, cm³/s or µm³/s — see the bridges below; a dropped \(10^{-14}\) is not a rounding error). Stokes–Einstein via mobility — from a molecular weight to a diffusion coefficient (eq. 1.3.2, 1.3.9 again)

$$D = \mu\,k_BT, \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{\zeta} = \frac{1}{6\pi\eta\,r_H} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad D = \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta\,r_H}$$
$$r_{\mathrm{bare}} = \left(\frac{3M}{4\pi\rho N_A}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad r_H = r_{\mathrm{bare}} + \delta_h \quad\text{(a hydration shell of one water layer is } \sim 0.1\text{–}0.3\text{ nm)}$$
  • Symbols\(\mu\) = mobility, velocity per unit force · \(\zeta\) = drag coefficient · \(\eta\) = viscosity of the medium the molecule swims in, at your temperature · \(r_H\) = hydrodynamic radius · \(M\) = molar mass (g/mol) · \(\rho\) = the density of the packed molecule (g/cm³; small organics 1.2–1.4, proteins ≈ 1.35) · \(N_A\) = Avogadro's number · \(\delta_h\) = the hydration shell you add to the bare packing radius.
  • What it saysThe same drag that resists pulling a particle sets how far thermal kicks scatter it: \(D\) is the mobility times the thermal energy. It runs forward (size → D) or backward (D → size, or D and a fitted composite → the size of whatever the composite hides). The packing route turns a molecular weight into a radius by treating the molecule as a sphere of its own bulk density; a hydration shell makes it larger and slower.
  • When it appliesLow Reynolds number, a roughly spherical object, a Newtonian medium, far from walls. In water, look \(\eta\) up at your temperature (table below) — never "10⁻³". A medium "N times as viscous as water" is N times water at your T. Compact folded proteins follow the packing route to within ~20 %; a non-spherical shape (an actin monomer is roughly 5.5 × 5.5 × 3.5 nm) has an \(r_H\) a little above its equal-volume sphere.
  • Fails whenA diameter is fed in for a radius (factor 2 in D); a viscosity at 20 °C is used at 37 °C (factor 1.45); the paper's assumed D is reported as though it were yours — a paper's fixed input is the paper's, your estimate is an independent number; the SI bridge is crossed badly (D in m²/s × 10⁴ = cm²/s; 10⁻⁸ cm²/s = 1 µm²/s); the cube root is forgotten (a factor of 10⁷ announces itself). When the medium is cytoplasm, the ratio Dwater/Dmeasured is a hindrance factor, not a correction to \(\eta\) — say what you think hinders.

A bleached band with two species — monomer that diffuses, filaments that exchange

$$\frac{\partial C_m}{\partial t} = D\,\frac{\partial^2 C_m}{\partial x^2} - \frac{C_m - C_f}{\gamma\,\tau}, \qquad\quad \frac{\partial C_f}{\partial t} = \frac{C_m - C_f}{\tau}, \qquad\quad \gamma \equiv \frac{\text{monomer}}{\text{polymer}} = \frac{1-FF}{FF}$$
$$\frac{F}{F_0} = (1-FF)\,C^*_m + FF\,C^*_f \qquad\text{(the fluorescence in the band is the pool-weighted sum of the two species' fluorescent fractions)}$$
  • Symbols\(C_m, C_f\) = fluorescent monomer and fluorescent-in-filament concentrations, each written as a Fourier series in the band width \(w\) and the cell length \(L\) with no-flux ends · \(D\) = the monomer's diffusion coefficient in cytoplasm · \(\tau\) = the filament lifetime, a 1/e time · \(FF\) = the fraction of total actin in filaments · \(\gamma\) = monomer : polymer, so \(FF = 1/(1+\gamma)\) and \(1-FF = \gamma/(1+\gamma)\) · \(C^*\) = each species' fluorescence normalized to its own pre-bleach value.
  • What it saysTwo clocks in one curve. Monomer diffuses back into the band over \(\sim w^2/D\) (seconds) — the fast phase, whose amplitude is the monomer share \(1-FF\). Filaments do not move; their fluorescence recovers only as subunits exchange with the monomer pool at rate \(1/\tau\) (minutes) — the slow phase, whose amplitude is \(FF\). The exchange term conserves actin between pools, which is why the monomer equation carries \(1/(\gamma\tau)\) where the filament equation carries \(1/\tau\). Fit both at once and one experiment returns \(D\), \(FF\) and \(\tau\).
  • When it appliesA one-dimensional cell (a rectangle, or an equivalent length \(L_{eq}\)) with a top-hat bleach; filaments immobile on the time scale of the experiment; first-order exchange with one lifetime; the labelled actin behaving like the rest of the pool. The two phases separate cleanly only when \(\tau \gg w^2/D\) — see the diffusion clocks below.
  • Fails whenThe fast share is reported as the filament fraction (it is the monomer share); \(\gamma\) is inverted (\(FF/(1-FF)\) is polymer : monomer); the slow phase is called an "immobile fraction" (a one-species model with an immobile fraction — Axelrod et al. 1976 — has no \(\tau\) at all: the lifetime is not mis-measured, it is absent); the paper's Eq. 1 is copied without reading it — check which term the paper's Eq. 1 weights by \(\gamma\), against the paper's own definition of \(\gamma\), before you write your version.

Diffusion clocks — the half-time of a band, the diffusion time, and the Damköhler referee (eq. 3.3.9 again)

$$t_{1/2} = 0.275\,\frac{w^2}{D}\ \ \text{(a 1-D top-hat band of width } w \text{ recovers half its fast amplitude at its centre)} \qquad\quad t_{\mathrm{diff}} \equiv \frac{w^2}{D}$$
$$\beta_w \equiv \frac{w^2}{\tau D} = \frac{t_{\mathrm{diff}}}{\tau} = \frac{\text{rate of exchange } (1/\tau)}{\text{rate of diffusive supply } (D/w^2)} \qquad\quad \tau \text{ is a 1/e time: } \ t_{\mathrm{half\text{-}life}} = \tau\ln 2 = 0.69\,\tau$$
  • What it saysDiffusion covers area, not distance: the time to refill a band grows as \(w^2\). Plot the fast half-time against \(w^2\) and the points fall on a line through the origin whose slope is \(0.275/D\). The diffusion time \(w^2/D\) is the convention the paper uses (no factor of 2 or 4 — state yours). \(\beta_w\) is a Damköhler number with "reaction" = subunit exchange at filament ends: \(\beta_w \ll 1\) means turnover is far slower than monomer transit and the exchange step limits; \(\beta_w \sim 1\) means diffusion begins to starve the filaments and the two phases merge into one.
  • When it appliesA one-dimensional band much narrower than the cell, monomer free to diffuse across it, a recovery curve whose fast phase is resolved (sampling faster than \(t_{1/2}\)). The 0.275 is a property of the top-hat geometry; a Gaussian bleach or a 2-D spot has a different prefactor.
  • Fails when\(w^2/D\) is called the half-time (it is 3.6× longer); \(w^2/4D\) and \(w^2/D\) are mixed without saying which; \(\tau\) is entered in minutes where a ratio wants seconds (a factor 60 in \(\beta_w\)); a half-life is reported as \(\tau\) (0.69×); \(D\) is taken in water when the monomer lives in cytoplasm.

A post is a cantilever — its spring constant, and the gel it stands in for

$$\delta = \frac{F L^3}{3EI}, \quad I = \frac{\pi r^4}{4} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k \equiv \frac{F}{\Delta x} = \frac{3\pi E\,r^4}{4L^3}$$
$$F = \frac{4}{9}\,\pi E_{\mathrm{eq}}\,a\,\Delta x \quad\text{(a rigid contact of size } a \text{ on an elastic half-space)} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad E_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{9k}{4\pi a}, \quad a = r \text{ (the post's radius)}$$
  • Symbols\(E\) = Young's modulus of the elastomer (Pa) · \(r\) = the post's radius · \(L\) = its height · \(I\) = the second moment of a circular section · \(\Delta x\) = the tip deflection · \(k\) = force per displacement (N/m; 1 N/m = 10³ nN/µm) · \(E_{\mathrm{eq}}\) = the modulus of the continuous gel that would give the same force for the same displacement through a contact of radius \(a\).
  • What it saysA slender post bends like a cantilever loaded at its tip: stiffness rises as the fourth power of radius and falls as the cube of height, so one rubber makes floors two decades apart in stiffness. The smallest force a post can report is its displacement resolution times \(k\). The equivalent-modulus map is the Boussinesq result for a half-space: \(E_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is proportional to \(k/a\), so two posts of equal \(k\) but different radius are not the same gel.
  • When it appliesSmall deflections (\(\Delta x \ll L\)), linear elasticity, a slender post (\(L\) several times \(r\)), a rigid base (no tilt or shear at the root), one \(E\) for the batch. \(E_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is a translation for comparing with gel studies, not a measured property of the array.
  • Fails whenA diameter is used as \(r\) (a factor 16 in \(k\), a factor 2 in \(E_{\mathrm{eq}}\)); \(I\) is taken as \(\pi r^4/2\) (that is the polar moment, for torsion); N/m is reported as nN/µm without the 10³; the 4π/9 is dropped; \(a\) is set to the diameter or to a focal-adhesion size when the paper's convention is the radius. A bending strain is not \(\Delta x/L\) or \(\Delta x/r\): at the root it is \(\sim 3 r\Delta x/L^2\).

A spring pulling on a spring — the two-spring law and the line it hides in

$$F = F_{\mathrm{sat}}\,\frac{k}{k + k_C} \qquad\xrightarrow{\;k \ll k_C\;}\qquad F \approx \frac{F_{\mathrm{sat}}}{k_C}\,k \quad\text{(a line of slope one on log–log axes: an apparent constant deflection } \Delta x_{\mathrm{cf}} = F_{\mathrm{sat}}/k_C\text{)}$$
$$\frac{k}{F} = \frac{k}{F_{\mathrm{sat}}} + \frac{k_C}{F_{\mathrm{sat}}} = \frac{k}{F_{\mathrm{sat}}} + \frac{1}{\Delta x_{\mathrm{cf}}} \quad\text{(straightened: slope } 1/F_{\mathrm{sat}}\text{, intercept } 1/\Delta x_{\mathrm{cf}}\text{)} \qquad\quad \frac{F}{F_{\mathrm{line}}} = \frac{k_C}{k + k_C}$$
  • Symbols\(k\) = the substrate's (post's) stiffness · \(k_C\) = the cell's internal stiffness, in series with it · \(F_{\mathrm{sat}}\) = the saturating active force the cell can exert on a rigid substrate · \(F_{\mathrm{line}} = (F_{\mathrm{sat}}/k_C)\,k\) = the low-\(k\) line extended (Marcq, Yoshinaga & Prost 2011).
  • What it saysA contractile element of finite stiffness pulling on a compliant post shares its deformation between the two springs. On soft posts the force is proportional to \(k\) and the post deflects by the same \(F_{\mathrm{sat}}/k_C\) every time — indistinguishable from "the cell holds its deflection fixed". On posts stiffer than \(k_C\) the force saturates at \(F_{\mathrm{sat}}\) and the deflection falls. The half-point of the plateau is at \(k = k_C\); the departure from the line at any \(k\) is the factor \(k_C/(k+k_C)\). The two hidden numbers are one plateau and one half-point — or one slope and one intercept on the straightened axes.
  • When it appliesA cell whose contractile machinery can be lumped into one spring and one force; a quasi-static pull; posts spanning both sides of \(k_C\) if you want to see the bend. Two models that coincide over the whole measured range cannot be told apart by that range — say so, and say where they would separate.
  • Fails whenA sweep that never reaches the plateau reports its highest point as \(F_{\mathrm{sat}}\) (at \(k = k_C\) you are at half); the half-point is confused with the 90 % point (\(k = 9k_C\)); \(k/(k+k_C)\) and \(k_C/(k+k_C)\) are swapped (they add to one); the "constant deflection" of the soft limit is mistaken for evidence against the two-spring picture — it is what the two-spring picture predicts there.

Dimensional analysis of a contact — the Boussinesq scaling, and a deflection as a speed

$$[E] = \mathrm{Pa} = \mathrm{N/m^2}, \quad [a] = [\Delta x] = \mathrm{m} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F \sim E\,a\,\Delta x \quad\text{(the only combination with the dimensions of force; the prefactor is } 4\pi/9 \text{ for a rigid disk, Landau \& Lifshitz)}$$
$$v_{\mathrm{tip}} = \frac{\Delta x}{t_{FA}} \qquad\quad \frac{v_{\mathrm{tip}}}{v_{\mathrm{retro}}} = \text{the fraction of the actin flow transmitted to the substrate} \qquad\quad \dot F = k\,v \ \ \text{(Week 5: a substrate spring loads a clutch at rate stiffness × speed)}$$
  • What it saysA load through a contact of size \(a\) on a half-space of modulus \(E\) can only produce a force proportional to \(E a \Delta x\) — so a stiffness \(k = F/\Delta x\) maps to a modulus only through a length, \(E \sim k/a\). Whether cells answer to \(k\) or to \(E_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is then a testable question: posts of different radius at the same \(k\) should differ if it is \(E_{\mathrm{eq}}\). A deflection that builds over the lifetime of an adhesion is a speed; compare it with the retrograde flow of the actin that drives it and you have the fraction of the flow that reaches the substrate — the rest slips (the motor–clutch's frictional slippage).
  • When it appliesLinear elasticity, a contact small compared with the substrate, a Poisson ratio hidden in the prefactor. For the speed, one adhesion lifetime (minutes) during which the post is loaded once; retrograde flow at the speeds Week 5 used (tens to ~100 nm/s).
  • Fails whenMPa is used where kPa was meant (10³); the lifetime is entered in minutes against a flow in nm/s (60); a fraction is reported as a percent (100); the transmitted fraction is compared with a loaded flow speed when the quoted one is unloaded, or the reverse — say which.

Unit bridges — cross them once, in writing

  • Diffusion\(10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm^2/s} = 1\ \mathrm{\mu m^2/s}\) exactly (1 cm² = 10⁸ µm²) · \(1\ \mathrm{m^2/s} = 10^4\ \mathrm{cm^2/s} = 10^{12}\ \mathrm{\mu m^2/s}\) · a small molecule in water is a few × 10⁻⁶ cm²/s, a protein in water ~10⁻⁶, the same protein in cytoplasm ~10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁸.
  • Rate constants\(1\ \mathrm{cm^3/s} = 10^{12}\ \mathrm{\mu m^3/s}\) · a rate in cm³ per site per second times a concentration in molecules per cm³ is a rate per site per second · to molar: \(1\ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}} \times N_A/1000 = 6.022\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}\), so \(10^{-13}\ \mathrm{cm^3/s} \approx 6\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}\) · per-site and per-cell rates differ by \(N\); a scaled unit like "10⁻¹⁴ cm³/s" is a currency — read the prefix printed beside the box.
  • Stiffness and stress\(1\ \mathrm{N/m} = 10^{3}\ \mathrm{nN/\mu m}\) (1 N = 10⁹ nN, 1 m = 10⁶ µm) · \(1\ \mathrm{nN/\mu m^2} = 1\ \mathrm{kPa}\) (a force per area is a stress; a force per length is a stiffness — nN/µm and Pa are different kinds of thing and no factor converts one into the other; a length \(a\) does) · \(1\ \mathrm{MPa} = 10^{3}\ \mathrm{kPa} = 10^{6}\ \mathrm{Pa}\) · \(1\ \mathrm{pN/nm} = 1\ \mathrm{nN/\mu m} = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{N/m}\).
  • Lengths and times\(1\ \text{Å} = 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}\); \(50\ \text{Å}\) is a radius of 5 nm · \(1\ \mathrm{\mu m} = 10^{3}\ \mathrm{nm} = 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm}\) · minutes × 60 = seconds, before any ratio · a 1/e time × 0.69 = a half-life.
  • Molar and molecular\(1\ \mathrm{\mu M} = 602.2\ \mathrm{molecules/\mu m^3}\) · \(1\ \mathrm{nM} = 0.6022\ \mathrm{molecules/\mu m^3}\) · \(M\) in g/mol ÷ \(N_A\) = grams per molecule; ÷ \(\rho\) in g/cm³ = cm³ per molecule; × 10²¹ = nm³.

Conventions the papers use — and this module keys to

  • Sites, not molecules\(N\) counts antibody combining sites (Fab); one IgE carries two, so IgE per cell = \(N/2\). Coverage and spacing are computed with IgE disks of radius \(s\) on the paper's cell radius \(a = 4\ \mathrm{\mu m}\): coverage = \(N_{\mathrm{IgE}}\,\pi s^2 / 4\pi a^2 = N_{\mathrm{IgE}}\, s^2/4a^2\). The paper's receptor radius is 50 Å = 5 nm.
  • The paper's D is the paper'sErickson et al. fix \(D = 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm^2/s}\) and solve for \(a\) — that is a self-consistency check, not a measurement of either. A Stokes–Einstein estimate from your own assumptions is a different number; report it as yours and carry it through your own chain.
  • Half-time, diffusion time, \(\beta_w\)The fast phase of a 1-D band reaches half its amplitude at \(t_{1/2} = 0.275\,w^2/D\); the paper's diffusion time is \(t_{\mathrm{diff}} \equiv w^2/D\) and \(\beta_w \equiv w^2/(\tau D)\), no factors of 2 or 4. Table 1's "10⁻⁸ cm²/s" is exactly µm²/s.
  • τ is a 1/e timeMcGrath et al. say so themselves: their turnover times "are not half-lives, but are more accurately thought of as 1/e times". Report \(\tau\) in seconds when it enters a ratio.
  • Eq. 1's γMcGrath et al. define \(\gamma\) as the local ratio of monomer to polymer. Read their Eq. 1 and decide for yourself which term it weights by \(\gamma\), and whether that agrees with the definition — this sheet does not answer that.
  • a = rIn Saez et al.'s Eq. 2 the characteristic length \(a\) "corresponds to the radius of the pillars" — the radius, not the diameter. Their quoted \(E \approx 2\ \mathrm{MPa}\) is a batch value from a reference; your batch has its own \(E\).
  • Mean vs maximumSaez et al. plot the island's mean force per post and its maximum separately (Fig. 2, two colours); the 130 nm deflection is the mean's. A maximum is a tail statistic — it grows with the number of posts you look at.

Statistics toolbox — how to treat your sweep

  • The sweep, not one runA rate constant, a diffusion coefficient or a stiffness is the slope, intercept, plateau or half-point of a sweep, never one run divided by its setting. Straighten first: choose the axes on which the law is a line, then read slope and intercept as the two numbers the law says they are.
  • Weight the lineRuns with larger error bars deserve less pull: a weighted line uses weights \(1/e^2\). On reciprocal axes the weights change too (the error of \(1/y\) is \(e/y^2\)). The sweep plot draws the weighted line; if your hand fit disagrees with it, ask which points you trusted more and why.
  • A plateau is two settings that agreeCall it a plateau only when two settings well apart (a factor of three or more) return the same reading within their error bars. One high point is a point, not a plateau — and a curve that is still rising at your highest setting has a plateau you have not reached. Report the plateau as the mean of the runs on it.
  • A half-point is bracketed, then interpolatedYou need usable runs on both sides of half the plateau; interpolate between the nearest two on the straightened axis (log of the setting, if the dial is logarithmic). A half-point read from one side is an extrapolation.
  • Replicates at one settingAverage the raw readings first, then push the average through the law (\(\langle f(x)\rangle \neq f(\langle x\rangle)\)). The standard error of \(n\) replicates is the run's error bar over \(\sqrt{n}\).
  • Tag honestlyA run that barely left the noise, or ended before its plateau, is data you keep and mark, not data you delete. Only the runs you call usable feed the line — and the grader sees both.
  • Agreement is the answer keyThe same quantity computed two independent ways must agree: an intrinsic rate from an intercept and from a plateau divided by a half-point; a diffusion coefficient from a slope and from one wide band's half-time; a deflection from a slope-one fit and from one force divided by its stiffness; a cell stiffness from a half-point and from a saturating force divided by a soft-limit deflection. When they agree, trust both; when they do not, one chain has a slip — find it before you export.
