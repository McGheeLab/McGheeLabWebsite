The scenario

The neutrophil in the infected bite — the cell Weeks 2–4 followed. Before asking whether it can find the wound, your lab characterizes the three molecular parts it depends on, on one microscope. A TIRF rig sits on the bench with three slides in the box:

  • the receptor slide — the neutrophil’s chemokine receptor in a supported membrane, fed a vendor’s proprietary chemokine analogue whose binding rates are undisclosed;
  • the actin slide — a single filament growing from a seed, using actin from a proprietary expression line whose kinetics are undisclosed;
  • the myosin slide — filaments gliding over a lawn of the same lab’s myosin II, whose ATP dependence is undisclosed.

Characterize all three once, then spend those numbers on five questions about the neutrophil and the cells around it, and one challenge about its walk to the wound. Every number you use is one you measured or one printed in your dataset; every constant you look up, you cite.

Constants live in Notes (k_B, the nM and µM bridges, water’s viscosity by temperature, documented structural constants). Citing them from Notes is fine; citing the primary source is better.

The Microscope — one instrument, three slides [10 %]

Every slide is run the same way, with the same five words: Set one dial → Run → Keep the run (with your tag) → Repeat at a new setting → Answer. Two pictures, two names: This run (the trace the camera recorded, with its readouts) and Your sweep (every run you kept, plotted, with a straight line drawn through the ones you called usable). A protocol strip above the slide lights each step as you complete it and states the rule in its own words.

The rule, on every slide: keep at least five usable runs at at least four different settings, bracketing the feature — the feature is the thing each slide is about, and finding where it is by choosing settings is the inquiry. On the receptor slide you need kept runs on both sides of half coverage; on the actin slide at least one kept run in which the barbed end shrinks and one in which it grows; on the myosin slide your fastest kept speed must be at least three times your slowest. The strip tells you which side is missing.

Tagging is your judgment. Each run’s card asks you to call it usable or noisy before you keep it — a trace that never reached its plateau, or barely left the noise, is data you keep and mark, not data you delete. Only usable runs feed the line and count toward the rule.

What the microscope reports, and never reports. Per run it gives you raw readouts only: the receptor slide’s plateau occupancy and the times to reach 63 % of that plateau on the way up and fall to 37 % of it on the wash; the actin slide’s elongation rate of each end in subunits per second (signed); the myosin slide’s gliding speed in µm/s. The line through your sweep prints its slope, intercept and axis crossing as numbers with units. It never reports a rate constant, a K_D, a C_c, a K_M or a maximum speed — those are what you answer.

The six things it asks you to answer (typed, one box each, gated by that slide’s rule):

slide report field unit
receptor the rate at which a free receptor captures this ligand, per molecule per µm³ per second b1_kon µm³/s
receptor the rate at which a bound ligand lets go b1_koff s⁻¹
actin how fast this actin’s barbed end adds subunits per µM of free monomer b2_konB µM⁻¹s⁻¹

myosin the gliding speed this motor reaches when ATP is not limiting b3_vmax µm/s

The number of receptor sites in the field of view is printed on the receptor slide. In your written solutions, for each slide: (a) which axes you drew your line on and why that choice makes the slope and intercept mean what you report; (b) the second route to at least one of your two numbers (the Map & Export tab prints the agreement); (c) your uncertainty from the spread across defensible choices of which runs to call usable.

Problem 1 — Two readers, one ligand [15 %]

Your chemokine, in the bite: a bacterium of radius a_B and a neutrophil of radius a_N (both printed), each carrying N receptors (printed) whose capture radius is a_s (printed); the ligand’s molecular radius r_L (printed) in tissue fluid that is η_rel times as viscous as water (printed), at your T. (a) The ligand concentration at which half of a cell’s receptors are occupied at equilibrium — from your binding rates (p1_Chalf, nM). (b) How many times more ligand per second the neutrophil intercepts than the bacterium, if each were a perfect adsorber — every molecule that touched its surface stuck (p1_ratio, recorded). (c) For each cell, the fraction of that perfect-adsorber capture rate its N receptors of capture radius a_s actually achieve, if the receptors are spread evenly over its surface (p1_fracN, p1_fracB). (d) The recorded verdict: a cell is at its ceiling when its receptors capture within 10 % of what a perfectly adsorbing sphere of the same radius would. Which statement do your two fractions support (p1_verdict)? Written solutions: the relation for (a) and why equilibrium applies; the unit bridge, shown; what (c) assumes about the receptors’ spacing; a defence of (d) that says what it would take for the other cell to get there.

Problem 2 — How long must the T cell pause? [15 %]

A T cell of radius a_T (printed) pauses on the vessel wall to decide whether the local chemokine has stepped up by a fraction ε (printed) over a background C₀ (printed). Its receptors are yours from the receptor slide, N of them (the count printed in Problem 1). (a) The shortest averaging time that lets it resolve that step — an error in its concentration estimate equal to the step itself — with its N receptors (p2_tmin, s); the occupancy enters through what your own rates say it is at the background concentration. (b) The recorded verdict: does it decide within the pause time t_p (printed)? (p2_verdict). Written solutions: the relation you used and what it assumes; why occupancy enters through θ(1 − θ); what changes if the cell were a perfect adsorber instead of N receptors.

Problem 3 — Fuel for the motor [10 %]

In the neutrophil’s rear, myosin II runs on ATP at a concentration your dataset prints ([ATP]) — below saturation. In a separate optical-trap experiment on this myosin — a single head pulling on a bead, the way Finer, Simmons and Spudich did it in 1994 — the lab measured the head’s turnover at saturating ATP (k_cyc), its unitary force (F_step) and its working stroke (d_step); your dataset prints all three. From your motility curve: (a) the fraction of its maximum cycling rate the motor reaches at that ATP (p3_frac); (b) the power one head delivers there (p3_P, aW; 1 aW = 10⁻¹⁸ W); (c) the ATP concentration at which the motor would reach 90 % of its maximum (p3_C90, µM). Written solutions: why the gliding speed reports the cycle rate; the energy bookkeeping per stroke against the free energy of ATP hydrolysis (cite it).

Problem 4 — The belt [20 %]

At the neutrophil’s front, actin filaments grow at the leading edge while their far ends are taken apart behind it, so the network runs like a conveyor belt. Your actin slide measured how fast each end of a filament adds and loses subunits — those are the numbers this problem runs on. Your dataset prints: the factor by which cofilin makes the pointed end lose subunits faster (f_cof); the speed at which the leading edge advances (v_edge); the radius of a free actin monomer (r_G); and how many times more viscous than water the cytoplasm is (η_cyt), at temperature T.

(a) At what free-monomer concentration does a filament keep a constant length while subunits still join at one end and leave at the other? Use both of your lines from the actin slide (p4_Css, µM). (b) At that concentration, how many subunits per second pass through the filament (p4_J, s⁻¹), and what speed does that correspond to (p4_v, nm/s)? Each subunit adds a fixed length to a filament — a documented number you find and cite. (c) Now let cofilin make the pointed end lose subunits faster by f_cof, everything else unchanged: how many subunits per second pass through now (p4_Jcof, s⁻¹)? (d) For the leading edge to advance at v_edge, assuming the network is not being dragged backward, what free-monomer concentration must the front have (p4_Cneed, µM)? (e) Compare your measured barbed-end capture rate with the fastest rate diffusion alone could bring monomers of radius r_G to a filament tip in this cytoplasm. Report the ratio — your rate divided by that limit — which is the fraction of arriving monomers that actually stick (p4_stick; every unit conversion matters), and record the verdict: is the barbed end limited by how fast monomers arrive, or by how often an arrival sticks? (p4_verdict).

Written solutions: the adding-and-losing balance for both ends in symbols before any numbers; why cofilin’s effect enters only through the pointed end; the assumption behind (d); every unit conversion in (e).

Problem 5 — Road, or random walk? [10 %]

Two transport decisions inside the same cell. (a) A vesicle of radius r_v (printed) must cross L (printed) of cytoplasm that is η_cyt times as viscous as water (printed); a kinesin can carry it at v_kin (printed). Report the dimensionless ratio that decides whether the road pays (p5_Pe), and the recorded verdict (p5_verdict). (b) The whole cell walks at v_cell (printed, in µm/min) and forgets its direction every τ_p (printed); how far from its start, root-mean-square, is it after t_obs (printed, in minutes) (p5_rrms, µm) — and how far would a straight walk have taken it (p5_straight, µm)? Written solutions: the two transit times behind (a); what regime (b) is in at t_obs and how you know.

Challenge — The walk to the wound [20 %; graded for 566, attempted by all]

The situation. A splinter has carried bacteria into the dermis, and they have founded a colony — a patch of dividing cells whose number doubles every t_double (printed). The nearest capillary runs a distance L_c (printed) away. Neutrophils leave the capillary there and set off through the tissue. Watch one of them. It crawls at a steady speed v_cell (printed, in µm/min), but it does not hold a heading: averaged over many such cells the velocity relaxes toward zero, d⟨v⟩/dt = −⟨v⟩/τ_p, so a cell remembers its direction for about τ_p (printed) and then wanders. There is no gradient to steer it in this problem.

The question: how long does a wandering neutrophil take to reach the colony — and does it get there before the colony has doubled?

It is solved entirely by hand and graded from your written solutions; the module records nothing for it. Let G(t) = ⟨r²⟩ be the cell’s mean-squared displacement from the point where it left the capillary, and let H(t) = ⟨r·v⟩. Your written solution must do three things:

  1. Set up the differential equation that G obeys. A way in: write dG/dt in terms of H; then write dH/dt with the product rule, using ⟨v·v⟩ = v_cell² and the relaxation law above (the random part of the cell’s turning is uncorrelated with where it is); eliminate H. You should arrive at a second-order linear equation for G with constant coefficients. Then decide, and justify from the physics, the conditions at t = 0 that pin down its solution.
  2. Solve it, and show what G becomes for t ≪ τ_p and for t ≫ τ_p. Name the quantity that multiplies t in the second limit.
  3. Estimate the time at which the cell’s root-mean-square displacement first equals L_c. Report it in minutes, check which limit it falls in, and compare it with the doubling time and with the time a straight crawl at v_cell would take.
