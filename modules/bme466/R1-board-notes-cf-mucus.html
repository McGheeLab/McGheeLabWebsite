0 · The case — open with the paradox 4 min0 → 4

A nineteen-year-old with cystic fibrosis has grown Pseudomonas from every sputum culture for six years. Twice a day she inhales tobramycin — sprayed directly onto the infection, reaching sputum levels about a thousand times what kills this organism in a test tube. She gets fewer exacerbations. The cultures never turn negative. Today we find out why, and the answer is entirely transport.
figure 0 · the airway wall
  1. On the healthy side write L ≈ 7 µm beside the mucus band and v ≈ 50 µm/s on the arrow.
  2. On the CF side write L ≈ 50–500 µm, cross out the arrow, and put 10⁸–10⁹ /mL by the bacteria.
  3. Across both, write the one number that never changes: the airway is ~5 mm wide.
Losing one chloride channel gives you a chronic bacterial infection. What is the mechanical chain? You want: no anion secretion → no water → dehydrated mucus → cilia buried → mucus stops → nothing is removed. Write that chain along the top; everything today hangs off it.
Two lengths and one velocity are the whole case: how thick the film is, how wide the airway is, and how fast the blanket moves.

1 · How long to cross? — and is 1D even legal 3 min4 → 7

The drug lands on top of the film. The bacteria are at the bottom. First question in any transport problem: how long does it take to get across — and am I allowed to do this in one dimension?
figure 1 · the slab
  1. Mark the top boundary reflecting (nothing goes back into the air) and the bottom absorbing.
  2. Write the licence in the empty box: L/W ≈ 10⁻², so (L/W)² ≈ 10⁻⁴ — sideways transport is ten thousand times slower.
  3. Under the arrow write the answer: t = L²/2D = 2500/1100 ≈ 2.3 s.
eq. 1.2.8 $$t \approx \frac{L^2}{2dD} \;\xrightarrow{\ d=1\ }\; \frac{L^2}{2D}$$
D = 550 µm²/s for tobramycin in water (Stokes–Einstein, next block earns it). L = 50 µm. t = 2500/1100 ≈ 2.3 s against a fifteen-minute nebulization. Then invert it: \(L^\star=\sqrt{2Dt_{\text{dose}}}\) — the thickness that just fails to fill in one dose is ~300 µm.
Arrival is not the bottleneck. Cross out “the drug can’t penetrate the mucus” on the board — except in plugs thicker than a third of a millimetre, which is exactly what CF airways are full of.

2 · Two cargoes, one gravitational field 3 min7 → 10

The dose does not arrive as molecules. It arrives as three-micron droplets, each carrying millions of molecules. Two cargoes, a factor of 2500 in radius, same gravity. One of them falls. Which?
figure 2 · the settling column
  1. In the left column sketch the molecule’s profile — flat, all the way up.
  2. In the right column sketch the droplet’s — a spike on the floor.
  3. Write ℓg ≈ 1 km and ℓg ≈ 30 nm under them. Eight orders of magnitude, same field.
eq. 1.2.2 $$\ell_g=\frac{k_BT}{\Delta\rho\,Vg}\qquad\text{and, equivalently,}\qquad \ell_g=\frac{D}{v_{\text{sed}}}$$
Why does the droplet’s answer contain \(a^3\) and the molecule’s not care? Volume is in the denominator: ℓg falls like a³ while thermal energy stays put. Size sorts cargo into diffusers and projectiles far more brutally than anything else in the course.
Gravity delivers the dose and diffusion distributes it. That handoff is where patchy deposition is born — and it is why we can leave gravity out of Fick’s laws for the rest of the hour.

3 · “CF mucus is 1000× more viscous.” So? 4 min10 → 14

Here is the sentence in every review of this disease: CF mucus is up to a thousand times more viscous than normal. And you own an equation with η in the denominator. It is extremely tempting.
Put η = 1 Pa·s into Stokes–Einstein for a 0.6-nm molecule. What crossing time does block 1 now predict? D drops from 550 to 0.38 µm²/s, so t = 2500/0.76 ≈ 3300 s ≈ 55 minutes. Measurement says the film fills in seconds. We are wrong by a thousand — let them sit in it before you rescue them.
figure 3 · D versus cargo size
  1. Draw the water line: a straight line of slope −1 on these log–log axes (D ∝ 1/a).
  2. Draw the naive bulk-viscosity line: the same line, three decades lower. Point at where it puts tobramycin.
  3. Now the truth: water’s line for everything below ξ, falling off a cliff at a ≈ ξ = 140 nm.
eq. 1.3.9 $$D=\frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta a}\qquad\text{— whose }\eta\text{ is it?}$$
A 0.6-nm molecule in a 140-nm mesh swims through water-filled holes and never touches the net. Its η is water’s. Bulk rheology measures shearing the whole network — a different experiment. What actually costs this drug a factor of ten is binding: tobramycin is a polycation, sputum is anionic DNA and mucin, so \(D_{\text{eff}} \approx 55\) µm²/s and only a tenth of the drug is free (eq. R.2.2).
In eq. 1.3.9, use the viscosity the particle feels. Mucus is not thick — it is selectively thick, and the selector is size. That is why dornase alfa reaches its substrate and a 100-nm vector does not.

4 · Fick I — on the molecule that is not the drug 4 min14 → 18

Change molecules. This layer is not just thick, it is inhabited: a billion bacteria per millilitre, each burning about a million oxygen molecules a second, and oxygen only arrives from the air side. Supply from one direction, demand everywhere. That has a definite answer.
figure 4 · oxygen with depth
Straight line or curve — and which way does it bend? Curve, bending down: every slice eats some, so there is less flux left to carry deeper, so the gradient gets shallower with depth. It reaches zero with zero slope — you cannot deliver past the point where nothing is left.
  1. Sketch the curve from the surface point down to zero, flattening as it lands.
  2. Mark where it hits zero and label it δ ≈ 28 µm. Shade everything below it.
  3. In the shaded zone write two words: no respiration.
eq. R.2.3 $$D\frac{d^2C}{dx^2}=Q \;\Longrightarrow\; C(x)=C_s\Big(1-\frac{x}{\delta}\Big)^{2},\qquad \delta=\sqrt{\frac{2DC_s}{Q}}$$
Do the crude version first, out loud, in one division: supply \(DC_s/L\) = 4000 against demand \(QL\) = 25 000 per µm² per second — demand wins sixfold. Then the exact depth: \(\delta=\sqrt{2(2000)(100)/500}=\sqrt{800}\approx 28\) µm. In a 500-µm plug that is 94 % anoxic, and quartering the bacterial load only doubles δ — it is a square root.
Now the sting: aminoglycoside uptake is energy-dependent and needs a proton-motive force, which needs respiration, which needs oxygen. Fick’s first law, applied to a molecule that isn’t the drug, has just predicted antibiotic tolerance with no genetics in the story.

5 · Fick II — fifteen minutes on, twelve hours off 4 min18 → 22

Everything so far was steady state. Therapy is not: she nebulizes for fifteen minutes, then there is no source for twelve hours. So the clinical quantity is not the peak — it is how long the concentration down there stays above the killing threshold. And there are two thresholds.
figure 5 · free drug at the biofilm, one interval
  1. Draw the rise: it fills in 23 seconds — on a twelve-hour axis that is a vertical line.
  2. Draw the decay: exponential, two-hour lifetime. Mark where it crosses each threshold.
  3. Shade the time above each line and write the two clocks: ~6 h and ~15 min.
eq. 2.2.1 $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}=D\frac{\partial^2C}{\partial x^2}\;-\;\frac{C}{\tau_{\text{clr}}} \qquad(\text{source on for }t_{\text{dose}})$$
Sputum total peaks near 10³ µg/mL; binding leaves 10² µg/mL free — which is exactly the biofilm threshold. Zero margin. Time above a threshold \(c^\star\) is \(t_{\text{dose}}+\tau_{\text{clr}}\ln(c_{\text{peak}}/c^\star)\): for the planktonic MIC of 4 that is \(900+7200\ln 25\approx\) 6.7 h; for the biofilm’s 100 it is \(\ln 1 = 0\) — just the dose window, 2 % of the interval.
Above the planktonic MIC half the day, above the biofilm threshold for a quarter of an hour. That is not a failure of the drug — it is the arithmetic of her regimen, and it predicts both halves of her chart: real benefit, no cure.

6 · Smoluchowski — how often does it even hit a bacterium? 3 min22 → 25

A concentration doesn’t kill anything. A molecule arriving at a cell does. So: one immobile bacterium, half a micron of radius, sitting in the drug we just delivered. How many hits per second?
figure 6 · the hungry sphere
  1. On the right axes sketch the concentration around the cell: zero at contact, recovering to c∞ — the 1 − a/r halo.
  2. Beside the cell write the two collapses: D₂ ≈ 0 (it is stuck in a biofilm) and a₂ ≪ a₁ (0.6 nm against 0.5 µm).
  3. Write the number big: 4.5 × 10⁷ hits per second.
eq. 1.5.1 $$k_s=4\pi(D_1+D_2)(a_1+a_2)\;\longrightarrow\;4\pi D_{\text{eff}}a_{\text{cell}}=345\ \mu\mathrm{m^3/s}$$
100 µg/mL free is \(1.3\times10^{5}\) molecules/µm³ (the nM → per-µm³ conversion they keep dropping). \(345\times1.3\times10^{5}\approx\) 4.5 × 10⁷ per second, on one cell — about 4000 hits per ribosome per second. Even at the MIC it is \(1.8\times10^{6}\)/s. But killing takes 10³–10⁴ s.
Delivery is a hundred-million a second and killing takes an hour. Where did seven orders of magnitude go? Not into diffusion — the physics says arrival is free. Into the membrane: uptake needs the proton-motive force, which needs oxygen, which block 4 says is gone. Two blocks meeting from opposite directions is the moment to pause.
When the physics says a step is free, stop modelling it and go find the gate. Here the gate is uptake, and block 4 already told you it is shut.

7 · Péclet — three arenas, and the exit that closed 3 min25 → 28

Last tool, and it is the one that explains why she has a chronic infection at all. A healthy airway is not a still pond: cilia move the whole mucus blanket at about 50 µm/s and you swallow it. In CF that stops.
figure 7 · three arenas
  1. Fill the three Pe boxes: 0.05 across the film, 9 × 10³ along the airway, 10⁹ for a cough.
  2. Under each, the clock that matters: seconds · 3 min · 2 ms, and beside them diffusion’s answer for a centimetre: 10 days.
  3. In the CF row write Pe = 10⁴ and next to it 12 days to clear. Circle both.
eq. 2.3.8 $$\mathrm{Pe}=\frac{Lv}{D}\qquad\text{— and }L\text{ is whatever distance you are asking about}$$
Across the 50-µm film Pe ≈ 0.05, which retroactively licenses treating blocks 4 and 5 as pure diffusion. Along a centimetre of airway at 50 µm/s, Pe ≈ 9000: flow clears in 3 minutes what diffusion needs 10 days for. And a 0.5-µm bacterium in a 140-nm mesh has essentially no diffusion coefficient at all — the escalator is not its best exit, it is its only one.
CF: Pe is still ten thousand. Is the airway fine? No — and this is the trap worth three minutes. Pe = 10⁴ with v = 0.01 µm/s still takes twelve days to clear a centimetre. A dimensionless number ranks two mechanisms; it never tells you whether the winner is fast enough. For that you need L/v and L²/2D themselves.
Cystic fibrosis is a transport disease: it zeroes the one clearance mechanism that has no backup. Every physical therapy in the disease — the vest, the huff cough, exercise — is prosthetic convection.

8 · The verdict — losses multiply 2 min28 → 30

Seven questions, seven equations, and — this is the part worth remembering — four answers that say “not the bottleneck.” Knowing which of your tools does not explain a pathology is the same skill as knowing which one does.
figure 8 · the delivery chain and its gates
  1. Fill the four boxes: 0.5 × 0.1 × 1 × 10, and under the arrow the product Θ = 0.5.
  2. Put a ✗ on the uptake gate (block 4) and a ✗ on the removal gate (block 7).
  3. Last line on the board: the lever is L, K and v — never the antibiotic.
eq. R.4.1 $$\Theta=\underbrace{f_{\text{dep}}}_{\text{where it lands}}\times \underbrace{\frac{1}{1+K}}_{\text{how much is free}}\times \underbrace{\min\!\Big(1,\tfrac{2D_{\text{eff}}t_{\text{dose}}}{L^2}\Big)}_{\text{how deep}}\times \frac{c_{\text{neb}}}{\mathrm{MIC}_{\text{biofilm}}}$$
\(0.5\times0.1\times1\times10=\) 0.5 — a factor of two short of the biofilm threshold before either gate is considered, and both gates are shut. Then name what modern therapy actually moves: dornase alfa (L ↓ and K ↓, because the DNA it digests is also the drug’s main binder), hypertonic saline (L ↓, v ↑), a CFTR modulator (v ↑↑, L ↓, load ↓). Nothing on that list makes the antibiotic better.
One sentence each — why is doubling the dose worse value than halving the layer? Dose multiplies one factor that is already saturated; L enters the penetration term as L² and moves δ and the removal clock too. Send them to the module’s intervention dashboard to argue with it.
Suppression without cure is not a mystery and not a resistance story. It is three multiplicative losses and two shut gates — and every one of them is a transport number you can compute in a lecture.
