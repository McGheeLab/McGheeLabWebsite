The minute before the gun. A sprinter walks to the line on a warm afternoon. Their heart is pounding. Their hands are cold. Ten minutes ago they sat on the track and held a long calf stretch, because that is what they were taught. Two muscles are at work in this minute, and they are made of the same motors: the rings of smooth muscle that have just moved the blood out of the skin, and the calf that is about to push. This module asks two questions and answers both with numbers. Why are the hands cold? And did that stretch help? Tab 1 is the ring. Tab 2 is the calf. Tab 3 is the tendon between the calf and the bone. Tab 4 puts the minute together.

1.1 · A tube that wants to split

Start with a sausage in a pan

A sausage that bursts in the pan splits along its length, never round its middle. A wooden barrel is held together by hoops that run round it. Both say the same thing about a tube with pressure inside: the pressure tries to open the tube along its length, and what holds it shut is a stress that runs round the wall. It is called the hoop stress.

A blood vessel is such a tube. Module 7 gave every vessel in the body a radius and a pressure, and Module 8 ended at the arteriole, whose wall is wrapped in muscle. Before asking what that muscle does, ask what any vessel wall has to hold. Do what Week 6 did to the gel: cut, and balance the forces on one piece.

The balance on half a tube

Three decisions first. Cut the tube along a diameter and keep the top half, a length \(L\) of it. Nothing is moving, so the forces on that half balance. And \(P\) is the pressure inside counted from the pressure outside, while \(\sigma_\theta\) is the average stress across the wall's thickness.

  1. The push. The pressure pushes the half away from the other half. Every piece of the arch is pushed along its own radius, but the sideways parts cancel in pairs and the upward parts add up to the pressure times the flat floor of the cut, which is \(2R\) wide and \(L\) long. That sum is what the bench does, one piece at a time.
    $$F_{apart} = P \cdot 2R \cdot L$$
  2. The hold. The wall pulls the half back at its two cut faces. Week 5 wrote stress as force over area (eq. 5.5.2), so a force is a stress times an area, and each face is \(h\) wide and \(L\) long.
    $$F_{held} = \sigma_\theta \cdot 2 \cdot h \cdot L$$
  3. Nothing moves. Set the two equal.
    $$P \cdot 2R \cdot L = \sigma_\theta \cdot 2 \cdot h \cdot L$$
  4. Divide both sides by \(2hL\).
eq. 9.1.1 — the hoop stress (Laplace) $$\sigma_\theta = \frac{P\,R}{h}$$

\(\sigma_\theta\) the hoop stress in the wall; \(P\) the pressure across the wall; \(R\) the lumen's radius (Module 7's letter); \(h\) the wall's thickness. The length cancelled. A wide tube is hard to hold, a thick wall makes it easy, and only the ratio \(R/h\) matters.

Check yourself

1. An aneurysm is a stretch of artery that has ballooned to twice its radius, with a wall that has thinned to half. By how much has the stress in its wall changed, at the same blood pressure?

Answer

By eq. 9.1.1 the stress goes as \(R/h\). The radius doubled and the thickness halved, so the stress is four times what it was. A bulge raises the stress in its own wall, which is why an aneurysm tends to grow.

2. Why did the length \(L\) drop out of the balance?

Answer

Both forces grow with the length in the same way: a longer piece has more floor for the pressure to push on and more cut face to hold with. The stress in the wall is the same for one millimetre of vessel as for one metre.

1.2 · What is really in the wall, and the ring we draw instead

The real wall first

Before any mathematics, what is there. Cut a real arteriole across and its wall is three coats, each a different kind of cell.

  • The lining. A single layer of flat endothelial cells, their nuclei bulging into the lumen. It holds almost nothing. Its job is to sense the flow and to make the nitric oxide that tells the muscle to relax.
  • An elastic sheet, in the larger arterioles; in the smallest it is usually absent.
  • The muscle. One or two layers of smooth-muscle cells. Each cell is a long spindle wrapped round the vessel and overlapping its neighbours, like the turns of a bandage.
  • The outer coat. Loose collagen with a few fibroblasts. It carries the nerve endings that release norepinephrine onto the muscle, and it goes taut if the vessel is stretched wide.

Let's simplify

Only one of those coats pulls. So for the force balance, draw the wall as one ring of muscle of thickness \(h\), and leave the lining, the elastic sheet and the outer coat out. Cut the ring into twelve wedge-shaped cells so that you can watch it thicken when it squeezes. Real cells are not wedges. The wedges are for counting.

A cell built to hold

Each cell of the ring is Week 4's actin and myosin, laid obliquely across the cell between anchors. There are no stripes, because the filaments are not in register. When the cells shorten the ring tightens and the lumen narrows. When they relax, the blood's pressure opens it. The cell is built to pull slowly and then hold: once it has contracted, many of its heads stay attached without cycling. That is the latch state, "an attached, noncycling cross-bridge (latch-bridge) … responsible for the high economy of force maintenance" (Dillon 1981). A ring can hold for hours on a hundredth or less of the ATP a skeletal muscle would spend holding the same tension (Paul 1990).

How much of its strength is the ring using?

One decision: the simplified ring is all muscle, so the hoop stress of §1.1 is carried by the muscle.

  1. What the ring must hold at rest, from §1.1.
    $$\sigma_\theta = 5.75\ \text{kPa}$$
  2. The most arterial smooth muscle can pull, measured on strips of artery wall (Murphy 1974).
    $$\sigma_{max} \approx 220\ \text{kPa}$$
  3. The share in use.
    $$\frac{\sigma_\theta}{\sigma_{max}} = \frac{5.75}{220} = 2.6\ \%$$

The ring holds the vessel with under three per cent of what it has. The other ninety-seven per cent is why it can close the vessel against the pressure whenever it is told to.

How many heads is that?

Week 4 added up a team of myosin heads. The force of the team is the number of heads, times the share of them holding on at any instant, times the force of one.

eq. 4.2.6 again — the force of a team, read for the heads holding on $$F_{tot} = N\,r\,F_{step}$$

\(N\) the heads; \(r\) Week 4's duty ratio, the share attached (a small \(r\); a vessel's radius is always the capital \(R\)); \(F_{step}\) the force of one attached head, 3.5 pN. The product \(N\,r\) is the number holding on, and it is what a force can count.

  1. Take one square micron of wall. The force across it is the stress times that area, and 1 kPa is 1 000 pN per µm².
    $$F_{tot} = 5.75\ \text{kPa} \times 1\ \mu\text{m}^2 = 5\,750\ \text{pN}$$
  2. Divide by the force of one attached head.
    $$N\,r = \frac{F_{tot}}{F_{step}} = \frac{5\,750}{3.5} \approx 1\,640\ \text{heads on every square micron of wall}$$

In Week 4's free-running myosin \(r\) was 0.05. In the latch state a head stays on far longer, which is how smooth muscle makes as much stress as skeletal muscle with a fifth of the myosin (Murphy 1974).

What the simplification costs. The 220 kPa is a large artery's muscle, used here for an arteriole. The muscle is only part of the wall's thickness, so its own stress is higher than 5.75 kPa by the ratio of the wall to the muscle coat: about twice if the muscle is half the wall, which is still a few per cent of 220 kPa. And once the vessel is stretched wide the collagen of the outer coat carries some of the load, which §1.3 needs.

The ring is holding with 2.6 % of its strength. Now let it squeeze. To hold the lumen at half its radius, at the same blood pressure, must the muscle pull harder, or less hard? Decide before you read §1.3. →

1.3 · Squeeze it, let it go, and what stops it

Start with a ring of clay

Roll a ring of clay and press it inward all round. The hole gets smaller, and the ring gets fatter: the clay has to go somewhere. An arteriole's wall is the same. It is made of cells full of water, so its volume does not change. For a fixed length of vessel that means the area of the wall, seen end-on, stays the same. When the lumen narrows, the same area has to fit round a smaller hole, so the wall gets thicker.

The stress at any radius: letters first

Write the resting lumen radius and wall thickness as \(R_0\) and \(h_0\), and find the thickness \(h\) at any other lumen radius \(R\).

  1. Name the outside. Seen end-on, the wall is a big circle with the lumen cut out of it. Call the outer radius \(R_{out}\); it is the lumen's radius plus the wall.
    $$R_{out} = R + h$$
  2. The wall's area is the outer circle minus the lumen.
    $$A_{wall} = \pi R_{out}^2 - \pi R^2$$
  3. The same line at rest. The lumen is \(R_0\) and the outer radius is \(R_0 + h_0\).
    $$A_{wall} = \pi\,(R_0 + h_0)^2 - \pi R_0^2$$
  4. The area is kept, whatever the lumen does. Set the two equal.
    $$\pi R_{out}^2 - \pi R^2 = \pi\,(R_0 + h_0)^2 - \pi R_0^2$$
  5. Divide both sides by \(\pi\).
    $$R_{out}^2 - R^2 = (R_0 + h_0)^2 - R_0^2$$
  6. Move \(R^2\) across to get the outer radius alone.
    $$R_{out}^2 = R^2 + (R_0 + h_0)^2 - R_0^2$$
  7. Take the square root.
    $$R_{out} = \sqrt{R^2 + (R_0 + h_0)^2 - R_0^2}$$
  8. The wall is what lies between the lumen and the outside, \(h = R_{out} - R\).
    $$h = \sqrt{R^2 + (R_0 + h_0)^2 - R_0^2} - R$$
  9. Put that thickness into eq. 9.1.1.
eq. 9.1.1 again — the hoop stress at any radius, with the wall's volume kept $$\sigma_\theta = \frac{P\,R}{\sqrt{R^2 + (R_0 + h_0)^2 - R_0^2} - R}$$

\(R_0\) and \(h_0\) the lumen radius and wall thickness at rest; \(R\) the lumen radius now; \(R_{out}\) the outer radius; \(h\) the wall thickness now. That is the hoop stress at any radius, from the pressure and the resting vessel alone.

Now the numbers

  1. The part that never changes. The resting arteriole is \(R_0 = 15\) µm and \(h_0 = 20\) µm.
    $$(R_0 + h_0)^2 - R_0^2 = 35^2 - 15^2 = 1\,225 - 225 = 1\,000\ \mu\text{m}^2$$
  2. Squeeze the lumen to \(R = 7.5\) µm. The thickness, then the stress at the same 7.67 kPa.
    $$h = \sqrt{7.5^2 + 1\,000} - 7.5 = 32.5 - 7.5 = 25.0\ \mu\text{m} \qquad\quad \sigma_\theta = 7.67 \times \frac{7.5}{25.0} = 2.30\ \text{kPa}$$
  3. Let the ring go to twice its resting radius instead, \(R = 30\) µm.
    $$h = \sqrt{30^2 + 1\,000} - 30 = 43.6 - 30 = 13.6\ \mu\text{m} \qquad\quad \sigma_\theta = 7.67 \times \frac{30}{13.6} = 16.9\ \text{kPa}$$

Squeezed, the lumen lost 7.5 µm of radius and the outside came in by only 2.5 µm, so the wall went from 20 to 25 µm thick. Let go, the same wall is stretched round a bigger circle and thins to 13.6 µm. So the answer to §1.2's question is: less hard. Squeezed to half the radius, the ring holds less than half the stress it held at rest.

Why the let-go ring carries three times the stress

The muscle has relaxed, the pressure has not changed, and the stress in the wall has tripled. Take eq. 9.1.1 apart again into its force and its area, for one metre of vessel, to see where the factor of three comes from.

  1. The force. The pressure pushes on the floor of the cut, which is \(2R\) wide. At rest, and let go:
    $$P \cdot 2R = 7.67\ \text{kPa} \times 30\ \mu\text{m} = 0.230\ \text{N/m} \qquad\quad P \cdot 2R = 7.67\ \text{kPa} \times 60\ \mu\text{m} = 0.460\ \text{N/m}$$
    The floor is twice as wide, so the same pressure pushes twice as hard.
  2. The area that holds it. The two cut faces are \(2h\) wide together. At rest, and let go:
    $$2h = 40\ \mu\text{m} \qquad\quad 2h = 27.2\ \mu\text{m}$$
    The same amount of wall is spread round a bigger circle, so there is less wall in the cut.
  3. Stress is force over area.
    $$\sigma_\theta = \frac{0.460\ \text{N/m}}{27.2\ \mu\text{m}} = 16.9\ \text{kPa} \qquad\quad \frac{16.9}{5.75} = 2 \times 1.47 = 2.9$$

Twice the force on two thirds of the wall: three times the stress. Squeezing is the mirror image, half the force on a wall a quarter thicker. And notice what the hoop stress is. It is not what the muscle decides to make. It is what the pressure demands of whatever is in the wall. When the muscle lets go it stops supplying that demand, the pressure stretches the vessel wider, and the demand grows as it widens. So the balance alone is not stable: once a ring starts to close, closing gets easier, and once it starts to open, the pressure gains on it.

Then what stops it?

You may object: the muscle contracts and the wall gets thicker, so is a squeezed ring not stronger? No, and that is the point. Keep two things apart. Contracting always means the same thing: the cells shorten round the vessel, the lumen narrows, and the wall, which keeps its volume, gets thicker. A thicker wall is not a stronger muscle. It is the same cells with the same filaments, shorter and fatter. Thickening adds no motors. Stress hides this, because it divides by the thickness. So for one metre of vessel compare two forces: what the pressure asks of each side of the wall, and what the ring's cells can pull.

  1. The load. The push \(P\cdot2R\) is shared by the two cut faces, so each side of the wall carries half of it, \(P\,R\). At rest, squeezed to half the radius, and let go to double:
    $$7.67 \times 15 = 0.115\ \text{N/m} \qquad 7.67 \times 7.5 = 0.0575\ \text{N/m} \qquad 7.67 \times 30 = 0.230\ \text{N/m}$$
  2. What the muscle can pull. At its resting length the ring's best is its strength times its thickness.
    $$\sigma_{max}\,h_0 = 220\ \text{kPa} \times 20\ \mu\text{m} = 4.4\ \text{N/m}$$
  3. The share in use at rest is §1.2's 2.6 %, now as a ratio of forces.
    $$\frac{0.115}{4.4} = 2.6\ \%$$

On the way shut. The load falls in step with the radius: half the radius, half the load. That is why holding gets easier. But what the muscle can pull falls too, and in the end faster: a cell that has shortened a long way has its filaments overrunning each other and makes less and less force. Measured on small arteries, the active force is greatest at one length and falls below it along a line that reaches zero at 0.38 of that length (Mulvany & Warshaw 1979). It is the short side of the curve Tab 2 builds. So for a given order from the nerves there is one radius at which what the cells can pull has come down to what the pressure asks, and the ring stops there. More order, a smaller radius.

On the way open. If the pressure rises it stretches the vessel wider, and the load \(PR\) rises with both. Two things answer. The muscle reads the stretch and contracts harder, which pulls the vessel back in: Module 8's myogenic response (Davis & Hill 1999). It is the muscle resisting the opening, so the lumen narrows and the wall thickens again as it comes back. And if the muscle is switched off altogether, the vessel opens until the outer coat's collagen goes taut and carries the load with no muscle at all. At wide radii that passive pull is several times the most the muscle makes: 4.4 times, at 1.6 of the muscle's best length, in the same small arteries.

A tap, not a switch. Every order from the nerves has its own radius, because the muscle's pull and the pressure's load both change with the radius and cross at one place. Everything on the stress–radius curve is far below the 220 kPa the muscle can make. The ring is never short of strength. What it needs is to be told.

Mulvany & Warshaw's vessels were rat mesenteric small arteries about 0.2 mm across, much larger than this arteriole, so their curve is used for its shape and no force from it is printed. The 4.4 N/m is the resting ring's best and is not carried to other lengths.

1.4 · One pump, many taps: who tells the ring

Start with a thumb on a hose

Put your thumb over the end of a garden hose and a small movement of the thumb changes the stream a great deal. Module 7 said why: at a given pressure the flow through a tube goes as the fourth power of its radius. The body has tens of millions of such thumbs, the rings of its arterioles, and one pump. Every organ hangs off the same arterial pressure, each behind its own rings.

Three kinds of signal reach a ring

  • Stretch. Raise the pressure and the muscle contracts against it (§1.3).
  • The tissue's own products. A working muscle's CO₂, acid, potassium and adenosine relax the rings beside it.
  • The body's general orders. The sympathetic nerves release norepinephrine onto the rings, and the adrenal glands put epinephrine into the blood.

What a ring does with a general order depends on the receptor it carries. Skin, gut and kidney carry α₁ receptors, and their rings squeeze. Muscle and liver carry β₂ receptors, and at ordinary levels of epinephrine their rings let go. So one alarm moves blood out of the skin and the gut and into the muscles. Muscle takes about a fifth of the heart's output at rest and more than four fifths in hard exercise.

Who does what. The skin is shut mostly by the nerves' norepinephrine acting on α receptors (Cooke 1997). Epinephrine at low to moderate levels binds preferentially to β₂ receptors and widens the vessels of muscle and liver; it constricts through α receptors only at high levels (Klabunde). Infused at ordinary levels it clearly opened the calf, and the fall in hand flow was not significant (Fellows 1985). The lists of organs are the textbook's. Other constrictors, if you meet them: angiotensin II, endothelin. Other dilators: nitric oxide, adenosine, low oxygen.

And the rings have to choose. Module 8 §3.4 opened a working muscle with the skin warm and watched the artery's pressure fall; when the nerves shut the skin the pressure came back. The heart cannot feed every bed at once.

Deeper: the order also travels along the wall

Module 8's last tab watched a dilation travel. In a hamster's muscle, acetylcholine put on one small arteriole about a millimetre from where it branches widened it on the spot by 13 µm, and the vessel's origin, its parent and its sister branch widened too, by 4–6 µm each; the flow into the branches rose 25–80 %. Nitroprusside on the same spot widened it just as much and nothing else moved, and the flow did not change (Kurjiaka & Segal 1995). The cells of the wall are joined to one another by gap junctions, and "electrical signals initiated on microvascular smooth muscle and endothelial cells can travel rapidly for millimeters" (Segal 2005).

So the rings of one vessel do not act alone: what one cell hears, its neighbours along the wall hear a little later and a little fainter. This module does not write a law for how the signal fades with distance. It is the reason a working muscle can open its whole path, from the feed artery down, and not just the ring beside the working fibre.

1.5 · Why the hand goes cold

Two words first

Everyday speech mixes them up. Temperature is how hot something is, in degrees. Heat is energy, in joules, and it moves on its own from a warmer thing to a cooler one. Your hand is warmer than the room, so it loses heat to the air all the time. It loses it fast, because a hand is mostly surface: four to five times as much skin for its mass as the body as a whole (Taylor 2014). And it has little muscle to make heat of its own. What keeps a hand warm is the warm blood coming in. So count the heat the blood brings, the way Module 8 counted the oxygen.

Module 8's bookkeeping was this. Blood goes through a tissue and comes out carrying less oxygen, and the tissue has the difference: the flow, times what each volume of blood gives up on the way through, \(\dot V_{O_2} = Q\,(C_a - C_v)\). Do the same with heat. Blood goes through the skin and comes out cooler, and the skin has the difference as heat, which it loses to the air.

The heat a flow of blood leaves behind

  1. What one piece of blood gives up when it cools. From first-year chemistry: to warm a mass \(m\) by \(\Delta T\) degrees takes heat in proportion to both, and when it cools by \(\Delta T\) it gives the same heat back.
    $$q = m\,c\,\Delta T$$
  2. Blood is measured by volume, not by mass. A volume \(V\) of it has the mass \(\rho V\).
    $$q = \rho\,V\,c\,\Delta T$$
  3. In the skin the blood arrives at \(T_a\) and leaves at \(T_v\), so it has cooled by \(T_a - T_v\). The heat that volume left behind:
    $$q = \rho c\,V\,(T_a - T_v)$$
  4. The blood keeps coming. Every second a volume \(Q\) of it passes through, so the heat left behind every second is the same line with \(Q\) in place of \(V\).
eq. 8.3.2 again — the Fick principle, counting heat in place of oxygen $$\dot q = \rho c\,Q\,(T_a - T_v)$$

\(q\) heat, in joules; \(m\) mass; \(c\) the specific heat, which is the heat that warms one kilogram by one degree (a small \(c\); Module 8's capital \(C\) was oxygen content); \(\rho\) density; \(V\) volume; \(T_a\) and \(T_v\) the blood's temperature arriving and leaving; \(Q\) the flow; \(\dot q\) the heat left in the skin per second. The dot is the dot on \(\dot V_{O_2}\): a rate. Both lines are the flow times what each millilitre gives up: there oxygen, \(C_a - C_v\); here heat, \(\rho c\,(T_a - T_v)\). Heat per second is power, and one joule per second is one watt.

Now the numbers

  1. Blood's density and specific heat. 1 060 kg/m³, and 3 600 J/(kg·°C), a little below water's 4 180 because blood is mostly water. Multiply them and the kilograms cancel.
    $$\rho c = 1\,060\ \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \times 3\,600\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}} = 3.8 \times 10^6\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{m}^3\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}}$$
  2. A cubic metre is a million millilitres, so divide by \(10^6\).
    $$\rho c = 3.8\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{mL}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}}$$
    In words: a millilitre of blood that cools by one degree leaves 3.8 J behind.
  3. Comfortable. The skin's 0.3 L/min is 300 mL in 60 s, which is 5 mL/s. The blood arrives at 37 °C and leaves the skin at about 33 °C, four degrees cooler. Carry the units through: the millilitres cancel, the degrees cancel, and joules per second are left.
    $$\dot q = 3.8\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{mL}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}} \times 5\ \frac{\text{mL}}{\text{s}} \times 4\ {}^\circ\text{C} = 76\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{s}} = 76\ \text{W}$$
  4. Shut down. One decision: change only the flow, and keep the same four degrees, so that the only difference between the two cases is the rings. 0.05 L/min is 50 mL in 60 s, which is 0.83 mL/s.
    $$\dot q = 3.8 \times 0.83 \times 4 = 13\ \text{W}$$

Where the heat goes, and why the hand cools

Once it is in the skin the heat goes out to the air. The warmer the skin is than the air, the faster it loses heat. A cup of coffee does the same: it cools fast while it is hot and slowly once it is nearly at room temperature. So the skin settles at the temperature where it loses exactly what the blood brings. A resting body makes 80 to 100 W, and it has to lose all of it or it warms up. Comfortable skin is handed most of that to lose: 76 W.

Now shut the rings. The skin gets 13 W, and the body keeps more than 60 W in its core. Look at the hand's side of it. It is still losing heat to the air as fast as before, and the blood now brings a sixth of what it did. More heat is leaving than arriving, so the hand's temperature falls. As it falls the hand loses heat more slowly, and it stops falling when the loss has come down to what the blood still brings, which is not far above the air's own temperature. That is the cold hand.

Nothing is wrong with the body's heat. A cold hand on a frightened sprinter is the body choosing the core and the muscles over the skin, with a 36 % turn of its smallest taps. In the clinic, a cold hand that stays cold is read as a sign that blood is not reaching it.

The 4 °C drop, the 33 °C skin and the resting 80–100 W are declared textbook values. A hand that has really gone cold sends its blood back cooler than 33 °C, so each millilitre gives up more: 13 W is the simplest case, not a measurement. Blood's 3.8 J per millilitre per degree is confirmed (the IT'IS tissue database gives 3 617 J/(kg·°C) and 1 050 kg/m³, the same product). Tables print specific heat per kelvin; a kelvin and a degree Celsius are the same size. How fast skin loses heat to air is not written here as a law, because this course has not built one.

2.1 · From a calf to one head

The blood the rings took from the skin has gone to the muscles. A skeletal muscle uses the same two filaments as the ring's cell and arranges them the opposite way. In the ring they lie obliquely and are anchored to the cell, good for squeezing a long way slowly. In a muscle fibre they are in register, in a repeating unit about 2.7 µm long called the sarcomere. Go down through the sizes one at a time, with the real picture at each.

  1. The muscle is fibres lying side by side.
  2. A fibre is one cell, about 50 µm across and, in the calf, 5.1 cm long. It is packed with myofibrils.
  3. A myofibril, about a micron across, is sarcomeres joined end to end.
  4. A sarcomere has thick filaments of myosin in its middle and thin filaments of actin coming in from the Z lines at its two ends. The heads pull the thin filaments toward the middle and the sarcomere shortens.
  5. A thick filament is not one molecule. It is about three hundred myosin molecules. Each molecule is a long tail with two heads at one end. The tails lie side by side and make the shaft, and the heads stick out of it in rings of six, a ring every 14.3 nm, forty-nine rings on each half. That is 294 heads a side. The molecules of the two halves point opposite ways, tails toward the middle, so the middle has no heads (the bare zone) and the two halves pull the two Z lines toward each other.
  6. A thin filament is two strands of actin beads, 5.5 nm a bead, twisted round each other, about a micron long and fixed in the Z line. Along it lie tropomyosin and troponin, a troponin every 38.5 nm, which cover the places a head can grab. When the nerve fires the cell releases calcium, the calcium moves them aside, and the heads can reach. That is the muscle's switch.
  7. One myosin molecule is two heavy chains. Their long ends twist together into the tail, about 150 nm long, and their other ends are the two heads.
  8. One head has two parts. The motor domain grips actin and holds the pocket where ATP is split. The lever arm, about 9 nm long, joins it to the tail. The cycle is Week 4's: the head grabs the thin filament; its lever swings, and because the tail is held in the thick filament, the thin filament is dragged about 11 nm toward the middle; a new ATP binds, the head lets go, and the lever is cocked again.

One head, one stroke: 11 nm, about 3.5 pN, one ATP. From a fibre 5 cm long down to a stroke of 11 nm is a factor of about five million, and everything a muscle does is that one stroke added up.

Start with a tug-of-war

    $$F = 220\ \text{kPa} \times 20\ \mu\text{m}^2 = 4.4\ \mu\text{N}$$
  3. The calf's three muscles together, 82.6 cm² of cross-section.
    $$F = 22.5 \times 82.6 = 1\,860\ \text{N}$$
A muscle is strong because it is thick. The ring's cell and the calf differ by a factor of four hundred million in force and hardly at all in stress. The motors are the same. Roughly 2.3 kg for every square centimetre of cross-section, whatever the muscle.

That calf is from cadavers aged about 83 (Ward 2009), and the authors warn its areas are small for a young adult. In five young men the calf's measured maximum is 3 170 N (Magnusson 2001), which is the number the rest of this module uses. The two do not agree through 225 kPa (3 170 N would need 141 cm²): muscle area in the old and the stress in a living human are both uncertain, so the line and the 3 170 N are kept apart on purpose. The fibre's 50 µm, the myofibril's 1 µm and the 80 % fill are declared textbook values.

2.3 · The lever: how hard the calf pulls to hold you

Try it on a stair

Stand on one leg and rise onto your toes. On tiptoe the foot is a lever, and the ankle is its pivot. The ground pushes up on the ball of the foot with your whole weight. The calf, through the Achilles tendon, pulls up on the heel on the other side of the pivot. The heel is much closer to the ankle than the ball of the foot is, so the calf is on the short end of the lever.

Moments about the ankle

Three decisions. You are standing still on one leg, so the ground carries your whole weight, \(W\). Take moments about the ankle, so that the shin's push, which acts there, drops out. And measure the two lever arms across the pull: \(d\) from the ankle to the tendon and \(L_f\) from the ankle to the ball of the foot.

  1. Nothing is turning, so the tendon's moment balances the ground's.
    $$F_t\,d = W\,L_f$$
  2. Divide by \(d\).
    $$F_t = W\,\frac{L_f}{d}$$
  3. Numbers. A 70 kg person weighs \(W = 70 \times 9.81 = 687\) N; \(L_f = 12\) cm and \(d = 5\) cm.
    $$F_t = 687 \times \frac{12}{5} = 1\,650\ \text{N}$$

\(F_t\) the force in the Achilles tendon; \(W\) your weight; \(L_f\) and \(d\) the two lever arms. This is the moment balance of statics, used once, so it takes no equation number here.

The calf pulls with 2.4 times your weight to hold up one times your weight. Muscles almost always sit on the short end: they give up force to gain travel and speed at the far end of the bone. And the muscle is in series with its tendon, so by eq. 6.2.1 the muscle's force is the same 1 650 N.

That many heads are attached at every instant, in every cross-section along the muscle: the sarcomeres are in series, so each one carries the whole 1 650 N.

You are standing still. Why do you tire?

The weight is not moving, so you are doing no work on it: work is force times distance, and the distance is zero. Yet your calf burns. The reason is that no head holds on for long. Each one attaches, pulls, lets go and is replaced by another, and every one of those cycles burns an ATP whether or not anything moves. Holding still costs fuel. The ring's latch state (§1.2) is the muscle that avoids this. Your calf cannot.

The lever multiplies whatever stands on the foot by 2.4. Both legs, 824 N in each tendon. One leg, 1 650 N. One leg with a 20 kg pack, 2 120 N. On one leg the calf is at half of its best, so there is room, at its best length.

The 12 cm and 5 cm are declared round values. Measured, the ankle is 13.7 to 13.9 cm from the ball of the foot (Lee & Piazza 2009), and the tendon's arm is 4.4 to 5.5 cm at rest and grows about a fifth from toes-up to toes-down (Maganaris 1998; Rugg 1990), so the 2.4 is on the low side and is not constant through the raise. With 14 cm the lever would ask for 1 920 N. At 6 pN a head, which is what was measured in an intact fibre (Piazzesi 2007), the count is 2.7 × 10¹⁴. No number is given here for the ATP cost of holding.

2.4 · The overlap: why length sets the force

Where in the heel raise is the calf strongest?

Heel dropped below the stair, foot level, or the very top? If you tried it you know: the middle, and the top and the bottom are harder. The muscle is the same muscle at all three. What changes is how many of its heads can reach.

A head can only pull if there is a thin filament beside it. Stretch the sarcomere and the thin filaments are drawn out from between the thick ones, so fewer heads can reach. Shorten it and there comes a length where every head is covered. Shorten it further and things get in the way: the thin filaments from the two ends run into each other, and then the thick filament meets the Z lines. Measure it before you derive it.

Count the heads that can reach

The bench answered the question the plain way: the force a sarcomere can hold is the number of heads holding on, times the force of one. Halve the heads that can reach and you halve the force. Now write down what the bench was counting. Two decisions. In \(F_{tot} = N\,r\,F_{step}\) (eq. 4.2.6) the heads are all the same, so \(r\) and \(F_{step}\) do not change with length; only \(N\), the number with a thin filament beside them, does. And name the three lengths that matter: \(a\) the thick filament, \(b\) one thin filament, \(c\) the bare middle that carries no heads.

  1. Each half of a thick filament carries heads along half of what is left when the bare middle is taken out.
    $$\text{heads sit along}\quad \frac{a - c}{2}$$
  2. The overlap in each half. Lay the filaments end to end and they would span \(a + 2b\); the sarcomere is only \(S\) long, so the difference is the overlap, shared between the two halves.
    $$\text{overlap in each half} = \frac{a + 2b - S}{2}$$
  3. The share of heads that are covered is the overlap over the length that carries heads, and the force goes with it.

Too short

3.1 · A spring in series with the muscle

A muscle's fibres do not pull on the bone. They pull on a tendon, and the tendon pulls on the bone. The two are joined end to end, so Week 6's first rule applies (eq. 6.2.1): one force runs through both, and their stretches add. Whatever the calf makes, the Achilles tendon carries, and whatever the tendon does with that force changes the length the calf is left to work at. That is why the tendon belongs in a module about muscle.

A tendon is collagen, and to a first look it is a spring: pull on it and it stretches in proportion.

3.2 · Hold the pull: the tendon creeps

What got longer?

Most people say the muscle. The muscle belly, yes; and the tendon too, and the tendon's part is the one this course can calculate with a tool it already owns. A tendon is a spring with water and sliding fibres in it, so it also has a clock, like Week 6's gel. The simplest element with a spring's stiffness and a clock is Week 6's standard linear solid: a lone spring, and beside it a second spring in series with a damper. Pull quickly and both springs resist. Hold, and the damper slowly gives until the lone spring carries everything.

eq. 6.2.5 again — the standard linear solid, for a tendon $$F + \tau\,\dot F = k_\infty\,x + \tau\,k_0\,\dot x$$

\(F\) the force and \(x\) the stretch; a dot is a rate; \(k_0\) the stiffness the moment you pull, when both springs resist; \(k_\infty\) the stiffness after a long hold, when only the lone spring is left; \(\tau\) the element's clock.

Three decisions. Both heels are off a stair with the calves relaxed, so each tendon carries half your weight through the lever, §2.3's 824 N, and the pull is held: the force is fixed and the length is free. After a long hold the stiffness is a tenth lower, as measured after ten minutes of stretch (Kubo 2001): \(k_\infty = 0.9 \times 330 = 297\) N/mm. And the creep time \(\tau_\varepsilon\) is two minutes. That one is declared, not measured.

Under a held force \(\dot F = 0\), and Week 6 solved what is left: the stretch starts at \(F/k_0\) and creeps to \(F/k_\infty\) on the creep time \(\tau_\varepsilon = \tau\,k_0/k_\infty\).

eq. 6.2.5 again — solved for a held force $$x(t) = \frac{F}{k_\infty} - \left(\frac{F}{k_\infty} - \frac{F}{k_0}\right) e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}$$
  1. The moment you sink into the stretch.
    $$x(0) = \frac{F}{k_0} = \frac{824}{330} = 2.50\ \text{mm}$$
  2. Held for many minutes.
    $$x(\infty) = \frac{F}{k_\infty} = \frac{824}{297} = 2.77\ \text{mm}$$
  3. How much of that extra 0.28 mm a hold of length \(t\) collects is \(1 - e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\).
    $$30\ \text{s}: 22\ \% \qquad 1\ \text{min}: 39\ \% \qquad 5\ \text{min}: 92\ \% \qquad 10\ \text{min}: 99\ \%$$

Under a held pull the tendon lengthens by 11 %, most of it in the first few minutes. That creep is part of why a held stretch gives you range. Let go and it comes back on the same clock, which is why the range fades again. Week 6 §2.6's builder bench is this element: hold a force on it and watch it creep, then let go.

Most of the range is not the tendon. A quarter of a millimetre of tendon is about a third of a degree at the ankle. Most of the range gained in a stretch is the muscle belly giving the same way, and the stretcher tolerating more pull. The tendon's share is small, and it is the share that costs force, which is §3.3.

The two-minute creep time is declared so that a 30 s hold does a fifth of the creep and ten minutes does all of it. Excised human tendons relax 13–17 % in 100 s (Marchiori 2021) and 46 % in 15 min (Johnson 1994), so a clock of minutes is the right size, and the tenth taken from Kubo is on the small side. For the muscle's own give: the whole calf's passive torque fell 17 % in a 60 s hold (Gajdosik 2006), and most of the gain comes in the first four stretches (Taylor 1990).

So after a long stretch the tendon is longer and softer. Go straight to a maximal jump. Are you stronger, the same, or weaker? §3.3 →

3.3 · Stronger or weaker afterwards?

Most athletes have been told the answer. Ask why.

Does a held stretch make you stronger, or does stretching the tendon shorten the sarcomeres and make you weaker? Keep two moments apart.

During the stretch the muscle is relaxed and the joint is pushed to the end of its range. The muscle belly and the tendon are both being pulled longer, and the sarcomeres are long.

Afterwards you stand at some ankle angle and make a full effort. The joint angle fixes the distance from the heel to the knee, and the muscle and the tendon have to share it. A tendon that the hold has left softer stretches further under the same pull, so it takes more of that fixed distance, and the fibres are left with less. Every fibre is shorter, and every sarcomere in it. The motors have not changed. The sarcomere has moved along §2.4's curve, and what that does depends on where it started. Measure it.

    $$S' = S\left(1 - \frac{\Delta x}{L_m}\right)$$
  5. Read the force at \(S'\) off §2.4's curve: eq. 9.2.1 on the long side, 100 % on the flat top, the measured line on the short side.

Now the numbers, for a long hold

  1. Before. In a full effort the calf pulls 3 170 N.
    $$x = \frac{3\,170}{330} = 9.6\ \text{mm}$$
  2. After a long hold the stiffness is \(k_\infty = 297\) N/mm.
    $$x' = \frac{3\,170}{297} = 10.7\ \text{mm}$$
  3. The extra 1.07 mm comes out of fibres 51 mm long.
    $$\frac{\Delta x}{L_m} = \frac{1.07}{51} = 2.1\ \%$$
  4. At the top of the raise the sarcomere goes from 1.6 to 1.567 µm, on the steep, measured side of the curve.
    $$\frac{F}{F_0} = 0.84 \times \frac{1.567 - 1.27}{1.67 - 1.27} = 62\ \%$$

At the top of the raise the calf had 69 % of its best and now has 62 %: seven points gone, a tenth of what it had. Weaker. Level, on the flat top, the sarcomere goes from 2.1 to 2.056 µm and nothing changes. The same. With the heel dropped, on the long side, the shorter sarcomere overlaps more and the calf gains: 59 % becomes 63 %. A little stronger.

eq. 9.2.1 again — the overlap, with every sarcomere a share shorter $$\frac{F}{F_0} = \frac{a + 2b - S'}{a - c} = \frac{3.65 - 2.741}{1.45} = 63\ \% \qquad\text{(heel dropped, after a long hold)}$$

The measurements agree in size and in sign. Across many studies, static holds of a minute or more cost 4.6 % of strength or power, and holds under a minute 1.1 %; tested with the muscle long, the same stretching gave a gain of 2.2 % (Behm 2016). A 30 s hold collects only a fifth of the creep, so it costs about a fifth as much: at the top, 69 % becomes 68 %.

Nothing happened to the motors. The tendon got longer, so the muscle works further to the left on the same curve. Whether that is a loss or a gain depends on which side of the curve it was on. At short muscle lengths, which is where a jump or a sprint finishes its push, it is a loss.

Three things this line simplifies, and what each is worth. First, it joins a measurement to a clock: the tenth is what was measured after a ten-minute stretch, in the contraction that followed (Kubo 2001, who found no significant change in maximal force at the one angle tested), and a shorter hold is given the share of it that the creep's clock collects. Week 6's element on its own would do less: pulled quickly it answers with \(k_0\) again, and only the 0.28 mm already crept is carried into the effort, which would make 69 % into 67 %. Second, it puts the calf's best, 3 170 N, through the tendon at every position; with the force the calf really makes at the top the result is 69 → 64 %, the same sign and about two thirds the size. Third, the very large losses after very long stretching are mostly the nervous system: 33 minutes of stretching cost 28 % of maximal force, with the muscle's activation depressed for the first quarter of an hour (Fowles 2000). The tendon is the part of the loss this module can calculate.

3.4 · Speed: the fan and the loop

Start with a ball that does not bounce back

Drop a ball and it never returns to the height it fell from. Some of the work that squashed it stayed in it as heat. Pull a tendon and let it go, and plot stress against strain: the way back runs below the way up. The area under the way up is the work you put in; the area under the way back is the work the tendon returns; the loop between them is work that stayed in the tendon as heat. A spring would have no loop. The loop is the damper, and a damper cares how fast you pull.

A question to decide first. The same tendon is stretched to the same length, once over four seconds, once in four hundredths of a second. In which is the force higher? And which one tears?

Pull at a steady rate

Three decisions. Divide the geometry out, as Week 5 did (eq. 5.5.2): stress \(\sigma = F/A_t\), strain \(\varepsilon = x/L_t\), modulus \(E = kL_t/A_t\). The creep of §3.2 takes minutes and a movement is over in a second, so in a movement the slow damper has no time to give, and the tendon shows §3.1's \(k_0\): a modulus \(E_{slow} = 0.99\) GPa. And real tendon has more than one clock, which Week 6 §2.6 said how to add: another arm. Give it a fast arm, with a clock \(\tau_f = 0.05\) s, that makes a very fast pull 15 % stiffer: \(E_{fast} = 1.15 \times 0.99 = 1.14\) GPa. Both numbers are declared, to match two measurements below.

eq. 6.2.5 again — in stress and strain, with the fast arm's clock $$\sigma + \tau_f\,\dot\sigma = E_{slow}\,\varepsilon + \tau_f\,E_{fast}\,\dot\varepsilon$$

Pull at a steady rate \(\dot\varepsilon\) from rest. The solution is a spring line plus a term from the damper.

eq. 6.2.5 again — solved for a pull at a steady rate $$\sigma = E_{slow}\,\varepsilon + (E_{fast} - E_{slow})\,\dot\varepsilon\,\tau_f \left(1 - e^{-\varepsilon / (\dot\varepsilon\,\tau_f)}\right)$$

\(E_{slow}\) and \(E_{fast}\) the tendon's modulus pulled slowly and pulled faster than its fast clock; \(\dot\varepsilon\) the strain rate; \(\tau_f\) the fast clock.

  1. Pull slowly, so that reaching \(\varepsilon\) takes much longer than \(\tau_f\). The damper's term stays small.
    $$\sigma \to E_{slow}\,\varepsilon$$
  2. Pull fast, so that it takes much less than \(\tau_f\). The exponential opens up to \(\varepsilon/(\dot\varepsilon\tau_f)\) and the damper's arm acts as a spring.
    $$\sigma \to E_{fast}\,\varepsilon$$
  3. Take the tendon to 4 % strain at three speeds: 1 % a second (a slow stretch, four seconds), 10 % a second, and 100 % a second (the speed of a hop: the whole stretch in 0.04 s).
    $$39.7\ \text{MPa} \qquad\quad 40.3\ \text{MPa}\ \ (+1.7\ \%) \qquad\quad 43.7\ \text{MPa}\ \ (+10\ \%)$$

A hundred times faster, and the stress at the same stretch is up by a tenth. Measured on excised human Achilles tendons, ten times faster changed the modulus from 816 to 822 MPa, which is no change (Wren 2001). A tendon barely notices speed. So the force is higher in the fast pull, by less than you would think.

Let go: the loop

Call the work put in on the way up \(U_{pull}\) and the work returned on the way back \(U_{back}\). Each is an area under a line on the stress–strain plot.

eq. 9.3.1 — the loop's lost share (hysteresis) $$\text{share lost} = \frac{U_{pull} - U_{back}}{U_{pull}}$$

\(U_{pull}\) the work done on the tendon as it is stretched; \(U_{back}\) the work it does as it shortens again. Their difference is the area of the loop, left in the tendon as heat.

For this tendon, to 4 % and back at the speed of a hop, the model gives 8 %. Measured: 7 % in an excised tendon (Ker 1981), 18 % in a living calf (Maganaris & Paul 2002). So a tendon gives back more than four fifths of what a stride stores in it, which is why running is cheap, and keeps the rest as heat, which is part of what "warming up" is.

One fast clock gives a loop only near one speed: the model loses 1 % at 1 % a second, 6 % at 10 % a second and 8 % at 100 % a second, while measured loops are about the same from 0.22 to 11 Hz (Ker 1981). A real tendon has many clocks, and the page's one is a stand-in chosen to match Wren's two slopes and Ker's loop. Hopping loads the living tendon to a peak strain of 8.3 %, with 17–35 % lost (Lichtwark & Wilson 2005).

3.5 · What tears, and three ways to stretch

Pull one until it breaks

The excised tendons of §3.4 were pulled until they broke. At 1 % a second, and at 10 % a second:

$$71\ \text{MPa at } 7.5\ \% \text{ strain} \qquad\qquad 86\ \text{MPa at } 9.9\ \% \text{ strain}$$

Against that, the stress in the tendon on a one-leg tiptoe, and in the calf's full effort:

$$\frac{1\,650\ \text{N}}{73\ \text{mm}^2} = 23\ \text{MPa} \qquad\qquad \frac{3\,170\ \text{N}}{73\ \text{mm}^2} = 43\ \text{MPa}$$

Pulled faster, the tendon broke later and at a higher stress. Speed does not weaken a tendon. But the tendon is in series with the muscle (eq. 6.2.1), so whatever force the stretch raises runs through the fibres and through the junction where the fibres join the tendon, and that is the weak link: muscle strains happen there, when a muscle that is switched on is being lengthened, at forces well above the most it can make holding still (Garrett 1990). In the whole muscle–tendon unit a faster stretch does give a higher peak force and more energy to absorb (Taylor 1990). So the risk in a stretch is its force, and its force goes up with how far, how fast, and how hard the muscle is working against it.

A bounce's higher peak on the bench is the extra stretch at the end of the range. The part that matters most in life is not in the tendon at all: the stretch switches the muscle on, and a muscle that is switched on and being lengthened pulls harder than its best (Tab 5 measures that).

The first few cycles also condition the unit: the tendon's stretch in a living calf grows by about 5 mm over the first five contractions and then stops changing (Maganaris 2003), and each cycle leaves its loop behind as heat. Moving through the range is what the literature calls dynamic stretching, and that is the mechanics of a warm-up.

What the evidence supports, and no more. That moving through the range protects you is a mechanical argument with animal support: in rabbits, muscle warmed up by contracting took more force and more length to tear, and tore at the junction (Safran 1988). In people the reviews say that static stretching has "no clear effect on all-cause or overuse injuries" and that there are "no data" for dynamic stretching (Behm 2016); "there is evidence that pre-participation stretching reduces the incidence of muscle strains but there is clearly a need for further work" (McHugh & Cosgrave 2010). For performance the two differ in sign: dynamic +1.3 %, static −3.7 % (Behm 2016).

The Achilles tendon works close to its limit. Peak force measured in running is up to 9 kN, 12.5 body weights, 111 MPa by that study's own area (Komi 1990), which is more than the excised tendons' breaking stress; those came from older donors. Most tendons work at about 13 MPa against a strength of about 100 MPa, and the Achilles is the exception, with a safety factor near 1.5 (Ker, Alexander & Bennett 1988). Words: in the literature "active static" stretching means the muscle on the other side takes the joint to the end of its range and holds it there; "dynamic" stretching means controlled movement through the active range.

4.2 · What this model cannot say, and the module on one card

Every number in §4.1 came from a simplification that was named where it was made. Here they are in one place.

  • The wall is drawn as one ring. A real arteriole has three coats and the muscle is only part of its thickness; the 220 kPa is a large artery's muscle; the four walls' thicknesses are a textbook table whose source could not be read, and a living arteriole's wall is thinner for its lumen.
  • The receptor lists are the textbook's. Which beds squeeze and which open, and how far each tap turns for a given alarm, are not measurements made here. The reflexes that set the nerves' tone (the baroreceptor loop) and the heart's own mechanics are not in the module.
  • The hand's temperature is not computed. The module counts the watts arriving. How fast skin loses heat to the air is said in words, because this course has not built that law.
  • The lever's arms are held fixed. The tendon's arm grows about a fifth from toes-up to toes-down, and the ball of the foot is nearer 14 cm from the ankle than 12, so the calf's load is on the low side and is not the same at every angle.
  • The three sarcomere lengths of the raise are declared, and placed on a frog's curve. A person's filaments are longer, and where a living calf sits on its own curve was not looked up. The stretched muscle's passive tension is left out.
  • The tendon's slow clock is declared. The softening after a hold is a measurement joined to that clock; the calf's best force is put through the tendon at every position. With the force the calf really makes at the top, a long hold costs 69 → 64 % and not 62 %.
  • Most of a stretch's range is not the tendon, and the largest strength losses after very long stretching are the nervous system's. Neither is modelled.
  • One fast clock stands in for many. It gives a loop only near one speed; real tendon's loop is about the same over a fifty-fold range of speeds.
  • Injury. The module shows where the force goes and which link is weakest. It does not show that any way of stretching changes how often people are hurt, and the reviews say that is not known.

The lever's 2.4 would not, nor the shares 59, 100 and 69 %, nor anything about the ring or the loop. The tendon's force on one foot would rise to \(883\times2.4 = 2\,120\) N, 67 % of the same calf's best, so the reserve with the heel dropped would be gone.

The module in four lines

  • A ring of muscle holds a vessel with \(\sigma_\theta = PR/h\) and under 3 % of its strength, and by squeezing a third it sends the blood elsewhere: that is the cold hand.
  • The same motors in a calf pull 2.4 times your weight through a lever; how hard they can pull is how many heads overlap: 59 % long, 100 % in the middle, 69 % short.
  • The tendon in series is a spring with two clocks: held for minutes it creeps and the muscle slides along its curve; pulled fast it is a tenth stiffer and leaves 8 % as heat.
  • What tears is the junction, when the force is high.

5.1 · Next module's headline, measured today — the muscle that moves

Everything in this module was held still. The ring held a vessel at one radius. The calf held you on tiptoe at one length, and its force was the number of heads in reach, times the share holding on, times the force of one. A sprinter's calf is not held still. It shortens while it pulls, and in the step before that it is stretched while it pulls.

Hang a light load on a muscle that is switched on and it lifts the load quickly. Hang a heavier one and it lifts it more slowly. At the load it can only just hold, the speed is zero: that is this module's case, and its force is the number Tab 2 counted. Now add a little more load than that. The muscle does not fail. It lengthens slowly, still pulling, with more force than it can make holding still. §3.5 said where that goes: strains happen at the junction when a muscle that is switched on is being lengthened, at forces well above its best.

Assumption broken. "A muscle's force is set by its length alone." It is not. It also depends on how fast the muscle is shortening or being stretched, because its heads are working on a filament that is moving past them. Module 10 measures that curve, names it, and puts it together with this module's force–length curve and the tendon's spring into one model of a muscle.
