The mistakes these problems are built to catch

Every problem here joins two relations, and each has a slip that lands close to the right number — so the written setup is what shows you did it. These are the ones that cost; each is worked through under its problem below.

  1. Problem 1 (a): the angle in one place. \(\delta_{\rm eff} = \delta\cos 35^\circ\) replaces \(\delta\) twice — in the step the membrane sees and in the work done against it. With the angle forgotten entirely the number often lands close, because a shorter step and a cheaper step nearly cancel at low load; the written \(\delta_{\rm eff}\) in both places is what shows you did it.
  2. Problem 1 (b): the stall force as the break-even. The grip the edge needs is \(N_mF_m(1 - v_{pol}/v_0)\): the stall times a race between two speeds. The bracket is the physics — set \(v_{edge} = 0\) and let it fall out.
  3. Problem 2 (a): the ground left out of the chain. The clutch and the ground carry the same force and add their stretches — springs in series, \(1/\kappa_{\rm eff} = 1/\kappa_c + 1/\kappa_s\). \(\kappa_c\) alone is a rigid dish; \(\kappa_c + \kappa_s\) is parallel, a chain stiffer than either part. Part (c) inherits whichever you chose.
  4. Problem 2 (c): two times instead of the integral. \(e^{-t_T/\tau(F_T)}\) is wrong by orders of magnitude: the ramp spends almost all its time at low force, where the bond lives for seconds. Bell integrated along the ramp (eq. 6.1.3) is the odds, and it splits into the rigid dish’s factor times the ground’s.
  5. Problem 3: the unit bridges. 1 kPa·µm = 1 pN/nm (write the chain, not the claim); 1 pN/µm² = 1 Pa; the in-line bead moves ⅔ of \(u_0\,a/r\), the beside-the-pull bead ⅓.
  6. Signs. A loaded bond cannot outlive an unloaded one (\(e^{-f/f_\beta}\)); a stiffer gel moves the same bead less (\(u\,E/E_2\)); the edge advances only if the printed grip is above your own break-even.

The worked problems

Problem 1 — The edge against the dermis [25] · pairs Week 5 §1.1–1.2 with Week 5 §1.3–1.4

The story. The neutrophil pushes its leading edge into the dermis: filament tips ratchet against the membrane while the motors behind the edge haul the whole network rearward. The adhesions decide who wins. (a) is the tip, (b) the grip the cell needs, (c) what the printed grip actually buys.

The chain at a glance — the equations used, and how they combine
(a) eq. 5.1.3 into eq. 4.5.1 — the loaded ratchet, with the angle in both places
$$\begin{aligned}\delta_{\rm eff} &= \delta\cos\theta = 2.7\,\mathrm{nm}\times\cos 35^\circ = 2.212\ \mathrm{nm} \\ v_{pol} &= \delta_{\rm eff}\Bigl(k_{on}C\,e^{-F_{mem}\delta_{\rm eff}/k_BT} - k_{off}\Bigr) = 2.212\Bigl(78.4\times e^{-1.809} - 1.2\Bigr) = 2.212\,(12.85 - 1.2) = \mathbf{25.8\ \mathrm{nm/s}}\end{aligned}$$
(b) eq. 4.4.3 with eq. 5.1.5, set \(v_{edge}=0\) — break-even, eq. 5.1.6
$$\begin{aligned}v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_0\Bigl(1 - \tfrac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\Bigr) = 0 \;&\Rightarrow\; F_{FA}^{*} = N_mF_m\Bigl(1 - \tfrac{v_{pol}}{v_0}\Bigr) = 300\Bigl(1 - \tfrac{25.8}{90}\Bigr) = \mathbf{214\ \mathrm{pN}}\end{aligned}$$
(c) eq. 5.1.5, then eq. 4.4.3 — the printed grip in the load line
$$\begin{aligned}v_{retro} &= v_0\Bigl(1 - \tfrac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\Bigr) = 90\Bigl(1 - \tfrac{225}{300}\Bigr) = 22.5\ \mathrm{nm/s} \\ v_{edge} &= v_{pol} - v_{retro} = 25.8 - 22.5 = \mathbf{+3.3\ \mathrm{nm/s}}\quad\text{→ advancing; consistent with } F_{FA} = 225 > F_{FA}^{*} = 214\end{aligned}$$

(a) How fast does one filament tip advance the membrane?

Given. C = 8 µM at the tips, kon = 9.8 µM⁻¹s⁻¹, koff = 1.2 s⁻¹ (37 °C), Fmem = 3.5 pN per filament; the subunit rise δ = 2.7 nm and the lamellipodial angle θ = 35° from Notes.
1 Choose the relation
The Brownian ratchet under load, with the angle in both places it belongs
A filament tip does not push; it waits for the membrane to fluctuate a gap \(\delta\) open and then a subunit fills it. A steady load makes that gap rarer by the Boltzmann factor \(e^{-F\delta/k_BT}\) — on the arrival term only, because a subunit leaving needs no gap. In a lamellipodium the filaments run at ±35° to the membrane normal, so the step the membrane sees is \(\delta\cos\theta\) — and the load is pushed back by that same shorter distance, so the work per insertion is \(F\delta\cos\theta\) too. Replace \(\delta\) by \(\delta_{\rm eff}\) everywhere or nowhere.
Where it is taught. Notes › “The ratchet under load” and “The gap a load must open” · Week 5 §1.1 “A filament cannot push. So how does it push?” (eq. 4.5.1 with the Boltzmann factor) and §1.2 “The ceiling, and who sets it” (eq. 5.1.2–5.1.3, the box “Why cos θ appears twice”).
2 Set it up
$$\delta_{\rm eff} = \delta\cos\theta$$
eq. 5.1.3 — the step the membrane sees
$$v_{pol} = \delta_{\rm eff}\left(k_{on}C\,e^{-F_{mem}\delta_{\rm eff}/k_BT} - k_{off}\right)$$
eq. 4.5.1 at 5.1.3’s angle

Regime check before the arithmetic: the tip only grows if \(C\) is above the loaded threshold \(C^{*}(F) = (k_{off}/k_{on})\,e^{F\delta_{\rm eff}/k_BT}\) (eq. 5.1.2). Units: µM⁻¹s⁻¹ × µM = s⁻¹; times nm gives nm/s.

3 Put the numbers in
$$\begin{aligned}\delta_{\rm eff} &= 2.7\times\cos 35^\circ = 2.7\times 0.8192 = 2.2117\ \mathrm{nm} \\ \frac{F_{mem}\,\delta_{\rm eff}}{k_BT} &= \frac{3.5\times 2.2117}{4.28} = 1.8086\qquad e^{-1.8086} = 0.1639 \\ k_{on}C &= 9.8\times 8 = 78.4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \qquad \text{loaded arrival} = 78.4\times 0.1639 = 12.85\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \\ v_{pol} &= 2.2117\times(12.85 - 1.2) = 2.2117\times 11.65 = 25.76\ \mathrm{nm/s}\end{aligned}$$

Check: \(C^{*}(F) = (1.2/9.8)\,e^{1.809} = 0.75\) µM, well below the printed 8 µM — the tip grows. Equivalently the filament’s own stall, \((k_BT/\delta_{\rm eff})\ln(C/C_c) = 8.1\) pN, is above the 3.5 pN load. Unloaded the same tip would run at 171 nm/s: the membrane costs it a factor 6.6.

Answer. \(v_{pol} = 25.8\) nm/s
A complete write-up also shows
  • The load on the arrival term only, with a sentence saying why: the departure needs no gap.
  • The angle entering twice — in the exponent and in the step. For about half the datasets \(\delta = 2.7\) nm everywhere lands close to the right number, so the written \(\delta_{\rm eff}\) in both places is the only thing that shows you did it.
  • A regime check — \(C\) above the loaded threshold, or the load below the filament’s stall — and the units carried (s⁻¹ × nm).
  • \(k_BT\) at 37 °C (4.28 pN·nm) cited from Notes or a source; 4.14 pN·nm at 300 K moves the answer 3 % and is a rounding choice.
Common mistakes
  • 171 on this dataset. The Boltzmann factor dropped — the unloaded ratchet, no load at all. The membrane must fluctuate a gap open before a subunit can add; \(e^{-F\delta_{\rm eff}/k_BT}\) is how often it does.
  • 20.0 on this dataset. \(\theta\) forgotten entirely: \(\delta = 2.7\) nm in the step and the exponent. Replace \(\delta\) by \(\delta\cos 35^\circ\) everywhere or nowhere — and the question says everywhere.
  • 16.4 on this dataset. \(\cos\theta\) on the speed only (\(\delta = 2.7\) nm left in the exponent) — Week 4’s eq. 4.4.3 form. The load is pushed back by the same shorter distance, so the work per insertion is \(F\delta\cos\theta\) too.

(b) How much force must the adhesions take off the motors before the edge advances at all?

Given. Your own vpol from (a), Nm = 150 motor modules of Fm = 2 pN, unloaded flow v₀ = 90 nm/s.
1 Choose the relation
The edge speed (eq. 4.4.3) set to zero, with the retrograde flow on the motors’ load line (eq. 5.1.5) — that is break-even, eq. 5.1.6
The edge moves at \(v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_{retro}\). The motors do not run at one speed: they run slower the harder they pull, from \(v_0\) down to a dead stop at the team’s stall \(N_mF_m\), and what they pull against is the adhesions’ grip \(F_{FA}\). So \(v_{retro} = v_0(1 - F_{FA}/N_mF_m)\). “Before the edge advances at all” is \(v_{edge} = 0\) — the tips build forward exactly as fast as the network slides back — and solving that for \(F_{FA}\) gives the grip the cell needs. The integrin contributes no force of its own: it is a force divider, not a motor.
Where it is taught. Notes › “The motors’ load line, stall, edge speed, break-even” · Week 5 §1.3 “Meanwhile, the motors are pulling the other way” (eq. 5.1.5, eq. 4.4.3 with both terms alive) and §1.4 “The integrin is a force balance” (eq. 5.1.6 and the box “Where break-even comes from”).
2 Set it up
$$v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_{retro},\qquad v_{retro} = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\right)$$
eq. 4.4.3 and eq. 5.1.5
$$v_{edge} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{FA}^{*} = N_mF_m\left(1 - \frac{v_{pol}}{v_0}\right)$$
eq. 5.1.6 — break-even

Read the shape first: a force (the stall) times a pure number between 0 and 1. The number is the race — how fast the tips build against how fast myosin would slide the network if nothing held it.

3 Put the numbers in
$$\begin{aligned}N_mF_m &= 150\times 2 = 300\ \mathrm{pN} \qquad \frac{v_{pol}}{v_0} = \frac{25.76}{90} = 0.2862 \\ F_{FA}^{*} &= 300\times(1 - 0.2862) = 300\times 0.7138 = 214.1\ \mathrm{pN}\end{aligned}$$

Per integrin, at the Week 5 cast’s ~2 pN per bound bond, that is on the order of 107 bound clutches under this edge — a sanity check a good solution makes.

Answer. \(F_{FA}^{*} = 214\) pN — about 71 % of the motors’ 300 pN stall
A complete write-up also shows
  • \(v_{edge} = 0\) written as the condition, then eq. 5.1.5 substituted — not eq. 5.1.6 quoted from nowhere. What the integrin contributes to the force is nothing: it divides.
  • Your own \(v_{pol}\) from (a) carried in.
  • The bracket present and read: the grip needed is most of the stall because the tips are slow compared with the free flow.
Common mistakes
  • 300 on this dataset. The stall force itself — the bracket dropped (“the adhesions must hold the motors”). The adhesions need not hold the whole team: the tips’ own advance covers the fraction \(v_{pol}/v_0\) of the flow.
  • 85.9 on this dataset. \(N_mF_m\,v_{pol}/v_0\) — the ratio where its complement belongs. Break-even is where the flow has been slowed to \(v_{pol}\) — by the fraction \(1 - v_{pol}/v_0\).
  • -748 on this dataset. The ratio inverted, \((1 - v_0/v_{pol})\). A negative grip is the tell; \(v_{pol} < v_0\), so the ratio inside the bracket is below 1.

(c) With the printed grip, how fast does the edge move — and is it advancing?

Given. Your own vpol from (a); the adhesions actually hold FFA = 225 pN; NmFm = 300 pN, v₀ = 90 nm/s.
1 Choose the relation
The same two relations, now with the printed grip put into the load line: \(v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_0(1 - F_{FA}/N_mF_m)\)
The grip does not push the cell forward — nothing here pushes. It gives the motors something to pull against, and every piconewton the adhesions hold is a piconewton the motors spend on them instead of on the flow. So the flow slows along the load line, and the edge moves at the tips’ speed minus what is left of the flow. Forward is positive; a negative answer is an edge sliding backwards under its own retrograde flow while the tips keep building.
Where it is taught. Notes › “The motors’ load line, stall, edge speed, break-even” · Week 5 §1.3 (eq. 4.4.3 “both terms alive this time”, eq. 5.1.5, and the free body drawn as a three-sided tug-of-war, not two).
2 Set it up
$$v_{retro} = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\right),\qquad v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_{retro}$$

Sign convention stated: + forward (the direction the cell is going). The verdict is the sign of \(v_{edge}\) — and it must agree with (b): the edge advances if and only if the printed \(F_{FA}\) is above your own break-even.

3 Put the numbers in
$$\begin{aligned}v_{retro} &= 90\times\Bigl(1 - \tfrac{225}{300}\Bigr) = 90\times 0.25 = 22.5\ \mathrm{nm/s} \\ v_{edge} &= 25.76 - 22.5 = +3.3\ \mathrm{nm/s}\end{aligned}$$

Consistency: \(F_{FA} = 225\) pN is above the break-even 214 pN from (b), so the edge advances — the sign agrees. 3.3 nm/s is 12 µm per hour: a slow advance, with the grip only just past break-even.

Answer. \(v_{edge} = +3.3\) nm/s — the edge is ADVANCING (verdict: “advancing — the grip beats the flow”)
A complete write-up also shows
  • The sign convention stated and the sign explained — what a negative edge speed means for the cell (it slides back; the tips are building but the flow wins).
  • The comparison with your own break-even made explicitly: grip above break-even ⇔ advance. Across the datasets the edge retracts on about 63 % and advances on 37 %, so a retracting verdict is the common right answer — yours follows from your own \(F_{FA}\) against your own (b).
  • The flow clamped to \([0, v_0]\): no grip in this homework exceeds the stall, so the clamp never bites, but noticing it is good physics.
Common mistakes
  • 25.8 on this dataset. \(v_{pol}\) alone — the flow forgotten (“the adhesions stop the flow”). The adhesions slow the flow; they do not stop it.
  • -64.2 on this dataset. \(v_{pol} - v_0\) — the grip forgotten, flow at its unloaded speed. The printed grip takes the fraction \(F_{FA}/N_mF_m\) of the flow away, along the load line.
  • -41.7 on this dataset. The flow taken as \(v_0F_{FA}/N_mF_m\) — the load line read backwards. A grip that speeds the flow up is the tell; the flow falls from \(v_0\) as the grip rises.
The recorded verdict. For the sample dataset the choice is “advancing — the grip beats the flow”. Yours is right only if your own FFA-against-break-even comparison says so: a verdict that follows honestly from your own (b) is the right verdict for your work, even where (b) itself went wrong.

Problem 2 — Will the adhesion mature? [25] · pairs Week 5 §2.2 (Bell) with Week 6 §1.3 (the chain and the race)

The story. One integrin–talin clutch has just bound under the flowing actin, on ground that gives. Its load climbs; its lifetime shrinks as it climbs; talin unfolds and the adhesion reinforces only if the bond is still there when the load reaches the threshold. (a) is how fast the load climbs, (b) how long the bond would last at the threshold, (c) the odds it gets there — and what the ground cost.

The chain at a glance — the equations used, and how they combine
(a) eq. 6.1.1 (one clutch, in series with the ground) into eq. 5.2.2
$$\begin{aligned}\kappa_{\rm eff} &= \frac{\kappa_c\kappa_s}{\kappa_c + \kappa_s} = \frac{0.6\times 0.3}{0.6 + 0.3} = 0.200\ \mathrm{pN/nm} \\ \dot f &= \kappa_{\rm eff}\,v_0 = 0.200\times 90 = \mathbf{18.0\ \mathrm{pN/s}}\end{aligned}$$
(b) eq. 4.2.5 as a lifetime — Bell at constant force
$$\begin{aligned}\tau(F_T) &= \frac{1}{k_0}\,e^{-F_T/f_\beta} = \frac{1}{0.1}\,e^{-12/2.2} = 10\times 0.00428 = \mathbf{0.0428\ \mathrm{s}}\end{aligned}$$
(c) eq. 6.1.3 — Bell integrated along the ramp; the odds split into the rigid dish × the ground’s factor
$$\begin{aligned}K &= \frac{k_0 f_\beta}{v_0}\Bigl(e^{F_T/f_\beta} - 1\Bigr) = \frac{0.1\times 2.2}{90}\Bigl(e^{5.455} - 1\Bigr) = 0.569\ \mathrm{pN/nm} \\ P &= e^{-K/\kappa_{\rm eff}} = e^{-0.569/0.200} = e^{-2.846} = \mathbf{0.058}\qquad = e^{-K/\kappa_c}\,e^{-K/\kappa_s} = 0.387\times 0.150 \\ \text{the ground’s factor } g &= e^{-K/\kappa_s} = 0.15,\quad 1/g = 6.7\ \text{→ “the ground cuts the odds by 2–10×”}\end{aligned}$$

(a) At what rate does the load on this bond climb?

Given. κc = 0.6 pN/nm (the clutch), κs = 0.3 pN/nm (the ground under a micron-sized adhesion), v₀ = 90 nm/s (a fresh bond, so the flow is still the unloaded flow).
1 Choose the relation
Springs in series (eq. 6.1.1 with one clutch) feeding the loading rate (eq. 5.2.2)
The clutch and the ground under it carry the same force and their stretches add — that is series, so the compliances add: \(1/\kappa_{\rm eff} = 1/\kappa_c + 1/\kappa_s\), always below the softer of the two. The actin drags the top of the chain at \(v_{retro} \approx v_0\) (the bond has just formed; the flow has not yet been slowed), so the force on the bond climbs at stiffness × speed: \(\dot f = \kappa_{\rm eff}v_0\). On a rigid dish it would be \(\kappa_cv_0\); the ground slows the loading by \(\kappa_{\rm eff}/\kappa_c\). The softer spring takes most of the stretch (\(x \propto 1/\kappa\)).
Where it is taught. Notes › “Series and parallel” and “The loading rate” · Week 6 §1.2 “From the motors to the gel — the whole chain on one spring” (eq. 6.1.1 minted) and §1.3 “How the ground’s give changes the force on the integrin” (eq. 6.1.1 again with \(n = 1\); eq. 5.2.2 again “through the chain”) · Week 5 §2.2 “One integrin under flow” (eq. 5.2.2 minted as \(r = \kappa v\) — the same quantity, written \(\dot f\) in Week 6).
2 Set it up
$$\frac{1}{\kappa_{\rm eff}} = \frac{1}{\kappa_c} + \frac{1}{\kappa_s} \;\Rightarrow\; \kappa_{\rm eff} = \frac{\kappa_c\kappa_s}{\kappa_c + \kappa_s}$$
eq. 6.1.1, \(n = 1\)
$$\dot f = \frac{df}{dt} = \kappa_{\rm eff}\,v_{retro} \approx \kappa_{\rm eff}\,v_0$$
eq. 5.2.2 through the chain

Units: pN/nm × nm/s = pN/s. Limits to quote: \(\kappa_s \to \infty\) gives \(\kappa_c\) (the rigid dish); \(\kappa_s \to 0\) gives \(\kappa_s\) (the bond hardly feels the flow).

3 Put the numbers in
$$\begin{aligned}\kappa_{\rm eff} &= \frac{0.6\times 0.3}{0.6 + 0.3} = \frac{0.18}{0.9} = 0.200\ \mathrm{pN/nm} \\ \dot f &= 0.200\times 90 = 18.0\ \mathrm{pN/s}\end{aligned}$$

Who stretches: the ground takes κeff/κs = 67 % of the stretch, the clutch 33 %. On a rigid dish the same bond would load at κcv₀ = 54 pN/s — the ground slows the loading to 33 % of that.

Answer. \(\dot f = 18.0\) pN/s
A complete write-up also shows
  • The series rule written in letters before any number, with a sentence on why it is series (same force, stretches add). Where the ground is much softer than the clutch, \(\kappa_s\) alone lands close to the right number — the written rule is what shows you did it.
  • Which spring takes most of the stretch, answered from \(x \propto 1/\kappa\).
  • The approximation \(v_{retro} \approx v_0\) named: a fresh bond, the flow not yet slowed.
Common mistakes
  • 54 on this dataset. \(\kappa_c\) alone — the rigid dish; the ground left out of the chain. That is a rigid dish; the ground under the adhesion is the second spring in the chain, and (c) inherits the slip.
  • 81 on this dataset. \(\kappa_c + \kappa_s\) — the stiffnesses added as if the springs were parallel. Stiffnesses add in parallel (same stretch, forces add); here the force is shared and the stretches add.
  • 27 on this dataset. \(\kappa_s\) alone — “the clutch is stiffer so it does not matter”. The clutch stretches too; the series rule keeps both, and \(\kappa_{\rm eff}\) is below the softer one.

(b) If the bond were held at the talin threshold, how long would it last?

Given. k₀ = 0.1 s⁻¹ with nothing pulling, fβ = 2.2 pN shortens the life e-fold, FT = 12 pN is talin’s threshold.
1 Choose the relation
Bell’s law, written as a lifetime (eq. 4.2.5): \(\tau(f) = \tau_0\,e^{-f/f_\beta}\), \(\tau_0 = 1/k_0\)
A slip bond held at a constant force lets go sooner the harder it is pulled — the work done by the force lowers the barrier out of the bound state. The off-rate rises as \(e^{+f/f_\beta}\), so the lifetime, its reciprocal, falls as \(e^{-f/f_\beta}\). “Held at the threshold” is exactly Bell’s constant-force case; the ramp is part (c)’s business. The sign is the whole content: a loaded bond cannot outlive an unloaded one.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Bell’s law and the catch bond” · Week 5 §1.5 “Reinforcement — and the limit one integrin runs into” (eq. 4.2.5 again, for the integrin–ligand bond) and §2.2 “One integrin under flow” (eq. 4.2.5 written as the lifetime the panel plots) · Week 6 §1.3 quotes it: 10 s unloaded, 0.025 s at 12 pN for the cast’s \(f_\beta = 2\) pN.
2 Set it up
$$\tau(f) = \tau_0\,e^{-f/f_\beta},\qquad \tau_0 = \frac{1}{k_0}$$
eq. 4.2.5 as a lifetime

Set \(f = F_T\). Units: s. Limit: \(f \to 0\) gives \(\tau_0\).

3 Put the numbers in
$$\begin{aligned}\tau_0 &= 1/0.1 = 10\ \mathrm{s} \qquad \frac{F_T}{f_\beta} = \frac{12}{2.2} = 5.455 \\ \tau(F_T) &= 10\times e^{-5.455} = 10\times 0.00428 = 0.0428\ \mathrm{s}\end{aligned}$$

That is 43 ms — the bond that lasts 10 s on its own survives a few hundredths of a second once the load reaches talin’s threshold.

Answer. \(\tau(F_T) = 0.0428\) s ≈ 43 ms
A complete write-up also shows
  • The sign of the exponent, and one sentence on why (force lowers the barrier; Bell).
  • \(\tau_0 = 1/k_0\) identified as the unloaded lifetime, and the answer set beside it.
  • A slip bond, not a catch bond: the problem gives \(k_0\) and \(f_\beta\), the two numbers of eq. 4.2.5; eq. 5.2.3 has nothing here to take.
Common mistakes
  • 2340 on this dataset. The sign slipped: \(e^{+F_T/f_\beta}/k_0\) — a loaded bond that outlives an unloaded one by a factor of hundreds. A loaded bond cannot outlive an unloaded one: the off-rate rises with force, so the lifetime falls.
  • 10 on this dataset. \(1/k_0\) — the load ignored. That is the unloaded lifetime; the question holds the bond at \(F_T\).

(c) What are the odds this bond, loaded at your rate from (a), lives to reach the threshold — and what did the ground cost?

Given. Your own ḟ from (a); k₀ = 0.1 s⁻¹, fβ = 2.2 pN, FT = 12 pN, v₀ = 90 nm/s; κc = 0.6 and κs = 0.3 pN/nm for the comparison with a rigid dish.
1 Choose the relation
The race to talin’s threshold (eq. 6.1.3): Bell integrated along the ramp, \(P = \exp\!\left[-\frac{k_0f_\beta}{\dot f}\left(e^{F_T/f_\beta} - 1\right)\right] = e^{-K/\kappa_{\rm eff}}\)
A ramped bond has no single lifetime. Its survival obeys \(dP/dt = -k_{off}(f)\,P\) with \(k_{off} = k_0e^{f/f_\beta}\) and \(f = \dot f t\), and \(P(0) = 1\) (the bond has just formed). Separating and integrating to the moment the load reaches \(F_T\) (\(t_T = F_T/\dot f\)) gives the closed form. Because \(\dot f = \kappa_{\rm eff}v_0\), the exponent is \(K/\kappa_{\rm eff}\) with \(K = (k_0f_\beta/v_0)(e^{F_T/f_\beta} - 1)\) — a stiffness, in pN/nm — and \(1/\kappa_{\rm eff} = 1/\kappa_c + 1/\kappa_s\) splits the odds into two factors: the clutch’s \(e^{-K/\kappa_c}\), which is the rigid dish, and the ground’s \(e^{-K/\kappa_s}\). It is not \(e^{-t_T/\tau(F_T)}\): the ramp spends almost all of its time at low force, where the bond lives for seconds; the lifetime at the final force applies only to the last instant.

The Challenge, worked — the ground that flows

25 points · graded for 566 students; attempted by everyone, and for 466 its weight redistributes over the three problems. Solved entirely by hand: the module records nothing for it.

The question. Week 6 §1.2 built up the force on an adhesion whose ground is a spring: the clutches load it, the force climbs toward the motors’ stall and the flow dies away (eq. 6.1.2). Now put the same adhesion on ground that flows — a damper of viscosity \(\eta_s\) (a Maxwell ground held long past its relaxation time) — with one spring \(\kappa\) between the actin and the damper (the clutches and whatever spring the ground still has, already in series). Above the spring the actin runs on the motors’ load line. How much force can the adhesion build — and does this edge advance?

Required: (1) the ODE for \(F(t)\) and a justified condition at \(t = 0\); (2) its solution, the long-time force and its clock, the \(\eta_s \to \infty\) limit recovering eq. 6.1.2 for both, and one sentence on what the flowing ground cost; (3) the steady flow, the edge speed and verdict, and the break-even viscosity compared with the printed one.

Where it is taught. Notes › “Damper, Maxwell, Kelvin–Voigt” (a damper cannot jump; a spring in series with it can), “The load builds to stall” (eq. 6.1.2) and “The motors’ load line” (eq. 5.1.5) · Week 6 §1.2 (eq. 6.1.2 and its Derive box), §2.3 “The Newtonian damper” (eq. 6.2.2), §2.4 “Maxwell” (eq. 6.2.3) · Week 5 §1.3 (eq. 5.1.5).
Sample dataset: κ = 6 pN/nm η_s = 3 pN·s/nm N_m F_m = 300 pN v₀ = 90 nm/s v_pol = 20 nm/s
The chain at a glance — the equations used, and how they combine
1 · eq. 5.5.1 (spring) + eq. 6.2.2 (damper) + eq. 5.1.5 (load line) → the ODE; F(0) = 0 because the clutch binds unstretched and a damper cannot jump
$$\begin{aligned}\dot F &= \kappa\,(v_a - \dot x_s),\quad \dot x_s = \frac{F}{\eta_s},\quad v_a = v_0\Bigl(1 - \frac{F}{N_mF_m}\Bigr) \;\Rightarrow\; \dot F + \kappa\Bigl(\frac{v_0}{N_mF_m} + \frac{1}{\eta_s}\Bigr)F = \kappa v_0,\qquad F(0) = 0\end{aligned}$$
2 · solve; limits
$$\begin{aligned}F(t) &= F_{ss}\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau'}\bigr),\qquad F_{ss} = \frac{N_mF_m}{1 + N_mF_m/(\eta_s v_0)} = \frac{300}{1 + 300/270} = \mathbf{142\ \mathrm{pN}} \\ \frac{1}{\tau'} &= \frac{1}{\tau_L} + \frac{1}{\tau_M} = \frac{\kappa v_0}{N_mF_m} + \frac{\kappa}{\eta_s} = \frac{1}{0.556\,\mathrm{s}} + \frac{1}{0.50\,\mathrm{s}} \;\Rightarrow\; \tau' = \mathbf{0.26\ \mathrm{s}} \\ \eta_s \to \infty:\ &F_{ss} \to N_mF_m,\ \tau' \to \tau_L = \frac{N_mF_m}{\kappa v_0}\ \text{(eq. 6.1.2)};\qquad \eta_s \to 0:\ F_{ss} \to \eta_s v_0\end{aligned}$$
3 · use it
$$\begin{aligned}v_{a,ss} &= v_0\Bigl(1 - \frac{F_{ss}}{N_mF_m}\Bigr) = \frac{F_{ss}}{\eta_s} = \mathbf{47.4\ \mathrm{nm/s}},\quad v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_{a,ss} = 20 - 47.4 = \mathbf{-27.4\ \mathrm{nm/s}}\ \text{→ retracts} \\ \eta_s^{*} &= \frac{N_mF_m\,(v_0 - v_{pol})}{v_0\,v_{pol}} = \frac{300\times(90 - 20)}{90\times 20} = \mathbf{11.7\ \mathrm{pN\cdot s/nm}}\ \text{vs printed } 3\ \text{→ the ground flows too easily}\end{aligned}$$

Step 1 — Set up the equation, and decide the condition at t = 0

Three rates, each its own law. The spring’s stretch \(x_c\) grows at the rate the actin slides minus the rate the damper lets the ground go:

$$\frac{dx_c}{dt} = v_a - \dot x_s,\qquad F = \kappa x_c \;\Rightarrow\; \dot F = \kappa\,(v_a - \dot x_s)$$
the spring, eq. 5.5.1
$$\dot x_s = \frac{F}{\eta_s},\qquad v_a = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F}{N_mF_m}\right)$$
the damper, eq. 6.2.2 · the load line, eq. 5.1.5
$$\dot F + \kappa\left(\frac{v_0}{N_mF_m} + \frac{1}{\eta_s}\right)F = \kappa v_0$$
first order, linear, constant coefficients

It is Maxwell’s eq. 6.2.3, \(\dot F + F/\tau_M = \kappa\dot x\) with \(\tau_M = \eta_s/\kappa\), driven by a displacement rate that itself depends on \(F\) through the load line — that is the whole point of the challenge.

The condition at t = 0 is \(F(0) = 0\), and you must argue it. The clutch binds unstretched, so the spring carries nothing; the damper has not moved and cannot jump — its rate is \(F/\eta_s\), zero when \(F\) is zero — and a spring in series with it can only be stretched at a finite speed. So the force starts from zero and climbs.
Wrong conditions seen: \(F(0) = N_mF_m\) (the motors are not stalled at the start — the network is free to flow at \(v_0\)); \(F(0) = \kappa v_0\) (dimensionally pN/s, not a force); \(F(0) = \eta_s v_0\) (that is the damper’s long-time force with no load line, not an initial value).

Step 2 — Solve it, take the limits, say what the flowing ground cost

Write \(B\) for the bracket. The homogeneous solution is \(Ae^{-Bt}\); the particular solution is the constant \(\kappa v_0/B\); \(F(0) = 0\) fixes \(A\):

$$B = \kappa\left(\frac{v_0}{N_mF_m} + \frac{1}{\eta_s}\right),\qquad F(t) = F_{ss}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau'}\right),\quad \tau' = \frac{1}{B}$$
$$F_{ss} = \frac{\kappa v_0}{B} = \frac{v_0}{\dfrac{v_0}{N_mF_m} + \dfrac{1}{\eta_s}} = \frac{N_mF_m}{1 + \dfrac{N_mF_m}{\eta_s v_0}}$$
the long-time force
$$\frac{1}{\tau'} = \frac{1}{\tau_L} + \frac{1}{\tau_M},\qquad \tau_L = \frac{N_mF_m}{\kappa v_0},\qquad \tau_M = \frac{\eta_s}{\kappa}$$
the clock: the two rates add

The limits. \(\eta_s \to \infty\) (the ground stops flowing): the \(1/\eta_s\) term vanishes, \(F_{ss} \to N_mF_m\) and \(\tau' \to \tau_L = N_mF_m/(\kappa v_0)\) — exactly Week 6’s eq. 6.1.2, force AND clock, with \(\kappa\) as the chain’s \(\kappa_{\rm eff}\). \(\eta_s \to 0\) (a ground that flows freely): \(F_{ss} \to \eta_s v_0\), the adhesion holds only the ground’s drag and the clock collapses to \(\tau_M\).

What the flowing ground cost, in one sentence: the adhesion can never reach the motors’ stall — its force is capped at \(N_mF_m/(1 + N_mF_m/(\eta_s v_0))\), set by how the ground’s drag \(\eta_s v_0\) compares with the stall — and because the force never reaches stall, the flow never stops.

Step 3 — Put the numbers in, and use it

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{v_0}{N_mF_m} &= \frac{90}{300} = 0.300\qquad \frac{1}{\eta_s} = \frac{1}{3} = 0.3333\qquad \text{sum} = 0.6333\ \mathrm{nm\,s^{-1}\,pN^{-1}} \\ F_{ss} &= \frac{90}{0.6333} = 142.1\ \mathrm{pN} \qquad(47\ \%\ \text{of the stall; check } \tfrac{300}{1 + 300/270} = \tfrac{300}{2.1111} = 142.1\ ✓) \\ B &= 6\times 0.6333 = 3.80\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \qquad \tau' = 1/3.80 = 0.263\ \mathrm{s} \\ \tau_L &= \frac{300}{6\times 90} = 0.556\ \mathrm{s} \qquad \tau_M = \frac{3}{6} = 0.50\ \mathrm{s} \qquad \frac{1}{0.556} + \frac{1}{0.50} = 3.80\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\ ✓\end{aligned}$$
the two clocks add as rates

So on this ground the force reaches 90 pN after one τ′ (0.26 s) and 95 % of 142 pN after three (0.79 s) — faster than the rigid-ground clock of 0.56 s, to a lower ceiling.

Common mistakes
  • Load line forgotten: \(v_a = v_0\) throughout. \(F_{ss} = \eta_s v_0 = 270\) pN, \(\tau' = \tau_M = 0.50\) s — a Maxwell element under a constant rate (eq. 6.2.3’s own case), no motors in it. No \(N_mF_m\) anywhere in the ODE; the \(\eta_s \to \infty\) limit then diverges instead of giving eq. 6.1.2.
  • Damper forgotten: \(\dot x_s = 0\). The rigid-ground result, \(F_{ss} = 300\) pN on \(\tau_L = 0.56\) s — eq. 6.1.2 itself. Nothing for \(\eta_s\) to do; step 2’s “what it cost” cannot be answered.
  • \(F(0) = N_mF_m\), \(\kappa v_0\) or \(\eta_s v_0\). A wrong \(A\) in the solution; the force falls instead of climbing, or has the wrong units. Check the sentence that justifies the condition.
  • \(\tau_M\) inverted: \(\kappa/\eta_s\). The clock’s units wrong (s⁻¹ in place of s). Unit track on \(\tau'\).
  • Steady flow read as \(v_0\), or as \(F_{ss}/\kappa\). \(v_{a,ss}\) should be 47.4 nm/s and equal \(F_{ss}/\eta_s\); \(F_{ss}/\kappa\) is a stretch (23.7 nm), not a speed. The damper’s check line is the tell.
  • Break-even with the ratio inverted, or \(\eta_s^{*} = N_mF_m/v_0\). 3.33 instead of 11.67 pN·s/nm — the load line left out of the closing step. Compare with eq. 5.1.6’s shape: stall × \((1 - v_{pol}/v_0)\), then ÷ \(v_{pol}\).
  • Sign of \(v_{edge}\), or the verdict with no comparison. \(v_{pol} - v_{a,ss}\), forward positive; the verdict must follow from \(\eta_s\) against \(\eta_s^{*}\).
