The scenario

You’ve been handed a vial of unlabeled nanoparticles and a virtual microscope. Your job, as the lab’s cell biophysicist, unfolds in stages: watch a single particle escape a corral you draw around it and discover the \(L^2\) law (Problem 1); track a whole ensemble to pin down the diffusion coefficient (Problem 2); drag a calibration bead with an optical tweezer to weigh the cytoplasm and unmask the particle’s identity (Problem 3); turn the same physics on a transcription factor hunting for one 10-bp address in a nucleus (Problem 4); ask how well a cell can count the ligand molecules raining onto its surface (Problem 5); and finally ask why, if diffusion is so effective at the micron scale, you can’t smell a banana from across the room (Problem 6). Every number you use is one you measured; every number you look up, you cite.

Data (use as needed). Problem 1 — Escape! [15 %] (Tab 1: first passage and the \(L^2\) law)

A single particle random-walks from the center of a dish at your temperature \(T\). You set the escape radius \(L\) (1–25 µm); the run stops the instant the particle crosses your boundary and reports the first-passage time \(\tau\). Transcribe each \((L, \tau)\) pair. Single runs scatter enormously, so the module enforces a replicated design: ≥ 12 runs across ≥ 4 distinct distances, with every distance repeated at least 3 times. The repeats at the same \(L\) are not padding — they are what makes the analysis in (c) possible.

(a) [5 %] Module questions (Q1 opens once that design is satisfied): - t1_factor — “When you double \(L\), the typical escape time is multiplied by ≈ what factor?” (the exponent check) - t1_D — “\(D\) (µm²/s) from the square law applied to your averaged times: \(D = L^2/(4\bar\tau)\) at each distance, averaged over distances.” - t1_extrap — “With YOUR \(D\): typical escape time (s) from \(L = 100\) µm.”

(b) [4 %] In your PDF: show by dimensional analysis why the mean escape time must scale as \(\tau \sim L^2/D\) — no other combination of \(L\) and \(D\) has units of time — and state the exact 2-D result \(\tau = L^2/4D\). Why does a single run scatter so widely around that mean, and what does that imply for how many rows to collect?

(c) [3 %] In your PDF — the two-step method, and why it is the method.

  1. Average \(\tau\) first, then convert. Escape times are heavy-tailed — the coefficient of variation across runs is ≈ 0.71 — so you must average \(\tau\) at each distance first and only then convert, \(D = L^2/(4\bar\tau)\). Averaging run-by-run values of \(L^2/4\tau\) instead inflates \(D\) badly, because \(\langle 1/\tau \rangle \neq 1/\langle \tau \rangle\) (Jensen’s inequality). In one or two sentences, say why the inequality runs in the direction it does — which runs dominate a mean of \(1/\tau\)? — and report the spread you actually observed (min, max, and standard deviation at one of your distances).
  2. Establish the exponent, then use the law. The free log–log fit’s slope is what tests whether the exponent is 2 — that is Q1’s (t1_factor) entire job. \(D\) must then come from the confirmed square law, \(D = L^2/(4\bar\tau)\) at each distance, averaged — that is Q2’s (t1_D) answer. Reading \(D\) off the free fit’s intercept is an extrapolation all the way down to \(L = 1\) µm, and it is wildly unstable when the slope is even slightly off. The module’s plot prints both readings; in one sentence, explain why the intercept route is the fragile one and which reading you reported.

(d) [3 %] In your PDF: hand-sketch your own \(\tau\)-vs-\(L\) table on log–log axes (plot the per-distance averages, and show the scatter of the repeats), draw your fit line, and label its slope. Mark the extrapolated point you reported in t1_extrap.

Problem 2 — The Ensemble [15 %] (Tab 2: MSD and measurement windows)

One particle told you the law; an ensemble tells you the number. Run tracking sessions (2–10 tracks; 12 s at 20 fps, realistic localization noise), then slide the fit window across the MSD curve; each window yields a row of window-lo, window-hi, slope, \(D_\text{fit}\). The plot of \(D_\text{fit}\) vs window center reveals the stable plateau between noise floor and long-lag scatter. A scenario switch adds a crowded cytoplasm [566], simulated as true fractional Brownian motion — so its MSD genuinely scales as \(\tau^\alpha\).

(a) [5 %] Module questions (Q1 opens after ≥ 4 rows): - t2_slope — “In a clean window the log-log slope reads ≈?” - t2_D — “Best estimate of \(D_1\) (µm²/s)?” — this should agree with Tab 1. - t2_unc — “± uncertainty on \(D_1\) from the window-to-window spread (µm²/s)?”

(b) [4 %] In your PDF: justify your fit window. Why exclude the shortest lag times (what does the localization-noise floor do to the apparent MSD?) and the longest (what happens to the statistics near the track length?)?

(c) [3 %] In your PDF: defend your uncertainty estimate (window-to-window spread, repeat sessions, track-to-track spread — your choice, but defend it); do t1_D and t2_D agree within it? Which do you trust more, and why?

(d) [3 %] [566 only] Module question t2_alpha (needs ≥ 2 crowded rows) — “Crowded-scenario exponent \(\alpha\)?” In your PDF, explain physically why crowding produces anomalous subdiffusion (\(\langle r^2\rangle \propto t^\alpha\), \(\alpha < 1\)) and give one concrete consequence for reaction kinetics. State explicitly how you obtained \(\alpha\): it is the slope of the time-averaged MSD measured in a clean window — not from a single lag, and not from the noise-dominated short lags or the statistics-starved long ones.

Problem 3 — Drag [15 %] (Tab 3: the Einstein relation)

An optical tweezer pulls the calibration bead (radius \(r_\text{bead}\), shown) with a force \(F\) you choose; the run stops when the bead crosses the 10 µm finish line and reports transit time and drift velocity \(v\). Transcribe \((F, v)\) rows; the plot of \(v\) vs \(F\) is a line through the origin, slope \(1/\zeta\).

(a) [6 %] Module questions (Q1 opens after ≥ 4 distinct \(F\); Q3 unlocks once t2_D is answered): - t3_zeta — “\(\zeta\) = 1/slope (pN·s/µm)?” - t3_eta_rel — “Cytoplasm viscosity as ×water at YOUR \(T\) (look up water — cite in PDF)?” - t3_rp — “Stokes–Einstein with your \(D_1\) → unknown-particle radius (nm)?”

(b) [5 %] In your PDF: the full Stokes–Einstein unit track. Starting from Einstein’s (1905) relation \(D = k_B T/\zeta\) with Stokes drag \(\zeta = 6\pi\eta r\), carry every unit explicitly from your \(\zeta\) in pN·s/µm through \(\eta\) in Pa·s to \(r_p\) in nm — no silent conversions — and say in one sentence why the same \(\zeta\) governs pulled drift and free jiggling (fluctuation–dissipation).

(c) [4 %] In your PDF: cite your source for the viscosity of water at your module temperature \(T\). Then identify a plausible biological particle of radius \(r_p\) (a specific protein complex, vesicle class, virus, ribosome…), cite your source for its size, and say in a sentence or two why the identification is (or isn’t) convincing.

Problem 4 — Filling the Hole [20 %] (Tab 4: FRAP and the searching factor)

Photobleach a spot in a nucleus expressing a GFP-tagged transcription factor. You choose the bleach-spot radius \(w\) (0.5–5 µm; your seeded \(w\) is the marked default); recovery animates, stops when it crosses 50 %, and reports \(\tau_{1/2}\). Transcribe \((w, \tau_{1/2})\) rows — the module requires ≥ 5 rows across ≥ 4 distinct spot sizes; the log–log plot builds toward slope ≈ 2.

(a) [8 %] Module questions (Q1 opens after ≥ 5 rows, ≥ 4 distinct \(w\)): - t4_factor — “Doubling \(w\) multiplies \(\tau_{1/2}\) by ≈?” (the exponent check) - t4_D2 — “\(D_2\) (µm²/s) from the square law: \(D_2 = 0.224\, w^2/\bar\tau_{1/2}\) at each spot size, averaged?” - t4_ttrav — “Time (s) to traverse YOUR nucleus (\(R_\text{nuc}\) shown), 3-D?” - t4_tsearch — “Mean time (s) for ONE TF to find a 10-bp site (\(a_\text{site} = 3.4\) nm)?”

(b) [6 %] In your PDF: argue from Fick’s second law (dimensionally) why \(\tau_{1/2} \propto w^2\) must hold — the 0.224 prefactor is the Soumpasis (1983) result, which you may quote. Then apply the same two-step logic as Problem 1, in a sentence or two: the log–log slope is the exponent check (t4_factor), and \(D_2\) comes from the law itself — \(D_2 = 0.224\, w^2/\bar\tau_{1/2}\) evaluated at each spot size and averaged — never from the fit’s intercept. State the 3-D scaling used for the traverse time and why it’s right. Then justify \(a_\text{site} \approx 3.4\ \mathrm{nm}\): what sets the length of 10 bp of DNA? Cite your source.

(c) [3 %] In your PDF: interpret t4_tsearch — on the timescale of gene regulation, is pure 3-D diffusion fast enough? Sanity-check \(D_2\) against \(D_1\): which particle is smaller, and do the magnitudes make sense?

(d) [3 %] [566 only] In your PDF: a real cell carries $$100 copies of the factor — what is the search time then? Qualitatively, what does facilitated diffusion — intermittent 1-D sliding along DNA — change, and why can 1-D + 3-D beat either alone? (Cite Berg & von Hippel 1985 or a comparable source.)

Problem 5 — Counting [20 %] (Tab 5: encounter rates and Berg–Purcell)

A cell of radius \(a\) (shown) sits in a ligand bath. You choose the concentration \(c\) (0.5–20 nM; your seeded \(c^\star\) is marked as “your cell’s environment”); the counter runs for 20 s and reports the slope-fit capture rate \(k\) (s⁻¹). Transcribe \((c, k)\) rows; \(k\) vs \(c\) is a line through the origin. Scenario B [566]: \(N\) discrete receptors — lower \(N\) until the rate halves.

(a) [8 %] Module questions (Q1 opens after ≥ 4 distinct \(c\) including \(c^\star\)): - t5_kslope — “Slope of \(k\) vs \(c\) (s⁻¹ per nM)?” - t5_ratio — “\(k\) measured at \(c^\star\) ÷ Smoluchowski \(4\pi D_2 a c^\star\) (derive 1 nM = 0.6022 µm⁻³ in the PDF)?” - t5_tauBP — “Berg–Purcell averaging time (s) at \(c^\star\), \(\delta = 0.1\)?”

(b) [4 %] In your PDF: derive and verify \(1\ \mathrm{nM} = 0.6022\ \text{molecules}/\text{µm}^3\) from Avogadro’s number, and use it to keep \(D\), \(a\), and \(c\) in one consistent unit system throughout.

(c) [5 %] In your PDF: Berg & Purcell (1977) showed that a cell measuring concentration by counting arrivals faces a fundamental noise floor. Give the scaling argument in words first — how many independent counts to resolve a fractional difference \(\delta\), and how fast can diffusion refresh the sample? — then compute \(\tau_{BP}\) by hand and confirm it matches your t5_tauBP.

(d) [3 %] [566 only] Module question t5_nrec (needs ≥ 3 finite-receptor rows) — “Receptors for half the perfect-absorber rate?” In your PDF: with \(k = k_\text{max}\, N s/(N s + \pi a)\), \(s = 1\) nm, estimate the fraction of the cell surface those receptors cover and why the answer is “surprisingly few.”

No module number is typed for this problem, but your estimate must be anchored to your own data: start from your measured \(D_1\).

(a) [7 %] Estimate the diffusion coefficient of a small odorant (e.g., isoamyl acetate) in air by scaling from your \(D_1\).
