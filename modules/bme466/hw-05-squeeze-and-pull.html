THE PROBLEMS

Challenge — the ring as a tap graded for 566 · everyone should attempt

The situation. In Problem 1 the squeeze was handed to you. Here you find the order from the nerves that produces it. Take Problem 1's skin arteriole, with your Problem 1 numbers. Whatever the lumen's radius \(R\), each side of the wall has to carry the load the pressure puts on it. The ring's muscle, at its resting length and with every one of its heads switched on, can make a stress \(\sigma_{max}\) across the wall's resting thickness \(h_0\). The nerves switch on only a fraction \(a\) of the heads, and the ring's pull is in proportion to \(a\). And a muscle makes less force the further it has shortened: take the ring's pull to fall in a straight line as the lumen narrows, from its full value at the resting radius \(R_0\) to nothing at \(R_{min} = \rho\,R_0\), with \(\rho\) printed below. (Measured on small arteries, the muscle's pull does fall away below its best length, and extrapolates to nothing at about 0.4 of it.)

The question: what order holds the vessel at rest, what order gives the squeeze you found in Problem 1, and why does a ring of muscle work as a tap and not as a switch?

Work it entirely by hand, in your written solutions: nothing on this tab is recorded, and your exported results file carries no challenge fields. There is no differential equation here; it is a balance of two forces, each written per metre of vessel. Your written solution must do three things. Each step has a 💡 hint under it: try the step first, then open the hint if you are stuck.

  1. Set up the balance and solve it. Write the force that each side of the wall must carry at lumen radius \(R\), per metre of vessel. Write the pull the ring can make at radius \(R\) when a fraction \(a\) of its heads is on. Set them equal and solve for the radius the ring settles at, \(R^*(a)\).
    💡 Hint
    • The load. Cut the vessel along a diameter and keep one half, as for the stress in Problem 1(a). The pressure pushes on a floor \(2R\) wide; two cut faces share it. What does each face carry, per metre of vessel?
    • The pull at rest. A stress times the thickness it acts across is a force per metre: with every head on, at the resting radius, \(T_{max} = \sigma_{max}\,h_0\).
    • The pull at radius \(R\). A straight line that is \(T_{max}\) at \(R_0\) and zero at \(R_{min}\), scaled by the fraction of heads that are on: \(a\,T_{max}\,(R - R_{min})/(R_0 - R_{min})\).
    • Solving. The balance has \(R\) on both sides, once each. Collect the two terms in \(R\) on one side.
  2. Check it, three ways. (i) Find the order \(a_0\) that holds the vessel exactly at \(R_0\), and show that it is the share of the muscle's strength you found in Problem 1(a). (ii) Let the order grow without limit: where does the radius go, and what does that say about closing the vessel completely? (iii) At a fixed order, nudge the radius a little below \(R^*\): which is larger now, the load or the pull, and which way does the wall move? Say in one sentence why this makes the ring a tap and not a switch.
    💡 Hint
    • At \(R = R_0\) the straight line's bracket is 1, so the balance reads \(a_0\,T_{max} = P\,R_0\). Divide through by \(h_0\) and compare with Problem 1(a).
    • A tidier form. Write the order as a multiple of the resting one, \(x = a/a_0\), and \(\rho = R_{min}/R_0\). Your \(R^*\) should become \(R^*/R_0 = x\rho/(x - (1 - \rho))\). Check that \(x = 1\) gives 1.
    • The limit. For very large \(x\) the \((1 - \rho)\) in the bottom stops mattering.
    • The nudge. Compare how fast each force falls as \(R\) falls: the load loses \(P\) for every unit of radius; the pull loses \(a\,T_{max}/(R_0 - R_{min})\). Use the balance itself to say which is larger.
  3. Use it. With your numbers: \(a_0\); the order that produces your Problem 1 squeeze, as a fraction of all the heads and as a multiple of \(a_0\); and the order that would halve the flow through this bed. Then: a drug blocks the receptors that make this ring contract, and the order falls below \(a_0\). What does your balance say happens to the radius, and what, in a real arteriole, takes the load then?
    💡 Hint
    • The order for a given squeeze. Set \(R^*/R_0 = f\) in the tidy form and solve for \(x\): \(x = f\,(1 - \rho)/(f - \rho)\).
    • Halving the flow is a statement about the radius first: by the fourth power, which radius factor gives half the flow?
    • Below \(a_0\) the tidy form gives a radius larger than \(R_0\), where the straight line no longer describes the muscle. What does that tell you about whether the ring can hold the vessel at all? Think of what else is in a real wall besides muscle.

📓 Notes — equations & when they apply

Notes — every relation of Lecture 9 this homework can use, and when each one applies

This sheet is your field guide. No question in this homework names its equation — you match the situation to the relation, and every problem needs more than one. For each entry: what it says, the assumptions it stands on, and what breaks it. Nothing here is graded and nothing is hidden. Equation numbers are the course's (module.tab.order); the three that begin with 9 are Lecture 9's own, and the others are earlier weeks' relations used again.

How to search this sheet. Lecture 9 is three sentences. A wall under pressure carries a stress set by its shape, and a ring of muscle that changes the shape changes the flow by the fourth power. A muscle's force is its cross-section times what its motors can do, reaches the bone through a lever, and depends on how far its filaments overlap. A tendon is a spring with a clock: held, it creeps; cycled, it keeps a little of the work. Ask: what is pushing or pulling, what holds it, and what is the geometry or the clock?

Notation (the course's): \(P\) a pressure · \(R\) a lumen's radius, \(h\) a wall's thickness · \(\sigma\) a stress, \(\varepsilon\) a strain, \(E\) a modulus · \(F\) a force, \(k\) a stiffness, \(x\) a stretch · \(Q\) a flow · \(S\) a sarcomere's length · \(\tau\) a clock · the dot means \(d/dt\). Week 4's \(r\) is the duty ratio, not a radius.

Stress, strain and the modulus — and a spring's stiffness from its shape (eq. 5.5.1, 5.5.2 again)

$$F = k\,x,\qquad \sigma = \frac{F}{A},\qquad \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L},\qquad \sigma = E\,\varepsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = \frac{E\,A}{L}$$
  • SymbolsF = force · k = stiffness (N/mm) · x = stretch · σ = stress (Pa; 1 kPa = 1 000 pN/µm²; 1 GPa = 1 000 N/mm²) · A = the cross-section that carries the force · L = the length being stretched · E = Young's modulus.
  • What it saysA stress is a force shared over an area, so a force is a stress times an area. A material has a modulus; an object made of it has a stiffness that also depends on its shape: thicker is stiffer, longer is softer.
  • When it appliesSmall stretches of an elastic material, pulled along its length; a tendon in its straight-line range.
  • Fails whenThe material creeps or is pulled fast (then the stiffness depends on the clock, below); very small strains of a tendon, where its fibres are still straightening.

The hoop stress in the wall of a tube (eq. 9.1.1, Laplace)

$$\sigma_\theta = \frac{P\,R}{h}$$
  • Symbolsσ_θ = the stress in the wall, in the direction that runs round the tube (the average across the wall's thickness) · P = the pressure inside counted from the pressure outside (1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa) · R = the lumen's radius · h = the wall's thickness.
  • What it saysCut the tube along a diameter and keep one half, a length L of it. The pressure pushes the half away on a floor 2R wide, with P·2R·L; the wall holds it at its two cut faces, each h wide, with σ_θ·2hL. Set them equal. Each face therefore carries a load P·R for every metre of vessel. Only the ratio R/h matters for the stress: a wide tube is hard to hold, a thick wall makes it easy.
  • When it appliesAny tube at rest under a steady pressure, for the average stress across its wall, with R the inner radius.
  • Fails whenYou need the stress at one depth in a thick wall (it is higher at the inside), or the tube is not round.

A wall that keeps its volume

$$R_{out} = R + h,\qquad R_{out}^2 - R^2 = (R_0 + h_0)^2 - R_0^2$$
  • SymbolsR_out = the outer radius · R_0, h_0 = the lumen radius and wall thickness at rest · R, h = the same at any other moment.
  • What it saysA wall of cells is mostly water and does not change volume. For a fixed length of vessel, the area of the wall seen end-on, π(R_out² − R²), stays the same. Narrow the lumen and the same wall has to fit round a smaller hole, so it thickens; widen it and the wall thins.
  • When it appliesA vessel whose length does not change while its lumen does.
  • Fails whenThe vessel lengthens or shortens as it changes width, or the wall takes up or loses water.

A team of myosin heads (eq. 4.2.6 again)

$$F_{tot} = N\,r\,F_{step}$$
  • SymbolsF_tot = the team's force · N = the number of heads · r = the duty ratio, the share of them attached at any instant · N r = the heads holding on · F_step = the force of one attached head (3–4 pN measured; 3.5 pN in this course).
  • What it saysForce adds with the heads that are holding on. Run it backwards: a force divided by F_step is the number of heads that must be attached to carry it; a stress divided by F_step is that number on each unit of area.
  • When it appliesMuscle holding a load, smooth or skeletal: every attached head carries about the same force.
  • Fails whenYou want the total number of heads N: that needs the duty ratio, which is 0.05 for free-running myosin II and far higher in smooth muscle's latch state.

The fourth power, and beds that share one pressure (eq. 7.1.4, 6.2.1 again)

$$R_h = \frac{\Delta P}{Q} = \frac{8\,\eta\,L}{\pi R^4}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{Q}{Q_0} = \left(\frac{R}{R_0}\right)^4 \ \text{at one pressure}$$
  • SymbolsR_h = a vessel's resistance to flow · ΔP = the pressure across it · Q = the flow · η = viscosity · L = length · R = lumen radius · the subscript 0 = before the change.
  • What it saysAt a fixed pressure the flow through a vessel goes as the fourth power of its radius, so a modest change of radius is a large change of flow. Organs hang off the same arterial pressure side by side (in parallel): each bed's flow is set by its own arterioles, and changing one bed's radius does not change the pressure the others see.
  • When it appliesA bed whose resistance sits in the vessels whose radius changes (its arterioles), in steady flow, with the arterial pressure held.
  • Fails whenMuch of the bed's resistance is in vessels that do not change, or so many beds open at once that the heart cannot hold the pressure.

What tells the ring — receptors on the smooth muscle

  • What it saysThe same messenger can tighten one ring and loosen another: what a ring does depends on the receptor its muscle carries. α1 receptors make the ring contract, so the lumen narrows (skin, gut, kidney). β2 receptors make it relax, so the lumen widens (skeletal muscle, liver). The sympathetic nerves release norepinephrine, which acts mostly on α receptors and does most of the work of shutting the skin; the adrenal gland's epinephrine reaches both kinds, and at ordinary levels its clearest effect is to open the muscles' arterioles. A working tissue's own products (CO₂, acid, potassium, adenosine) also relax the rings beside it, and a ring answers stretch by contracting.
  • When it appliesOrdinary levels of epinephrine. Contracting always means the same thing: the cells shorten round the vessel, the lumen narrows, and the wall thickens.
  • Fails whenEpinephrine is very high (then its α action wins even in muscle), or a drug blocks one receptor type.

Series and parallel, for muscle (eq. 6.2.1 again)

$$\text{series: } F = F_A = F_B,\;\; x = x_A + x_B \qquad\qquad \text{parallel: } x = x_A = x_B,\;\; F = F_A + F_B$$
  • SymbolsF = force · x = change of length · A, B = any two elements joined end to end (series) or side by side (parallel).
  • What it saysSarcomeres end to end along a fibre are in series: each carries the whole force, and their length changes add, so every sarcomere takes the same share of a fibre's shortening. Fibres side by side are in parallel: their forces add, so a muscle's force goes with its cross-section, F = σmaxA (about 22.5 N per cm² for skeletal muscle). A muscle and its tendon are in series: one force in both, and what one gives in length the other must take up if the joint does not move.
  • When it appliesAlways, for elements joined by massless links.
  • Fails whenNever as a rule; what fails is assuming every element is identical.

Moments about a joint (statics)

$$\sum M = 0:\qquad F_t\,d = W\,L_f$$
  • SymbolsF_t = the force in the tendon · d = its lever arm, the distance from the joint to the tendon's line of pull · W = the load (a mass times g, in newtons) · L_f = the load's lever arm.
  • What it saysA body that is not turning has no net moment about any point. Take moments about the joint and the unknown force at the joint drops out. Muscles usually pull on the short arm, so they pull harder than the load they hold.
  • When it appliesHeld still, or moved slowly; lever arms measured across the line of each force.
  • Fails whenThe limb is accelerating, or the lever arms change with joint angle and you use one angle's values at another.

The overlap of the filaments — force against sarcomere length (eq. 9.2.1)

$$\frac{F}{F_0} = \frac{a + 2b - S}{a - c}\qquad \text{(the long side)}$$
  • SymbolsF = the force the sarcomere can make at length S · F_0 = its best, with every head covered · a = the thick filament's length · b = one thin filament's length (there are two, one from each end) · c = the bare middle of the thick filament, which carries no heads.
  • What it saysA head can pull only where a thin filament lies beside it. The overlap ends at S = a + 2b (no force). Shorten from there and it grows in a straight line until every head is covered, at S = 2b + c (the best force). From there down to S = 2b nothing changes (the plateau). Shorter still and the filaments get in each other's way: measured on frog fibres (a = 1.6, 2b = 2.05, c = 0.15 µm), the force is 84 % at 1.67 µm and falls in a straight line to nothing at 1.27 µm.
  • When it appliesA muscle held at one length and fully switched on. On the long side the line follows from the filament lengths of the muscle in question; the short side is a measurement.
  • Fails whenThe muscle is shortening or being lengthened as it pulls, or is stretched so far that its passive tension matters.

A tendon held under load — two stiffnesses and a creep time (eq. 6.2.5 again)

$$F + \tau\,\dot F = k_\infty\,x + \tau\,k_0\,\dot x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x(t) = \frac{F}{k_\infty} - \left(\frac{F}{k_\infty} - \frac{F}{k_0}\right)e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\ \ \text{under a held force}$$
  • Symbolsk_0 = the stiffness the moment it is pulled · k_∞ = the lower stiffness after a very long hold · τ_ε = the creep time · x = the stretch · F = the held force.
  • What it saysWeek 6's standard linear solid. Pull and hold: the stretch starts at F/k_0 and creeps to F/k_∞. After a hold of length t it has collected the share 1 − e^(−t/τ_ε) of that creep. Let go and it comes back on the same clock.
  • When it appliesA tendon (with the sheet of tendon it grows from) under a steady pull for seconds to minutes.
  • Fails whenThe pull changes during the hold; real tendon also has faster clocks, which matter in a movement (below) and not in a hold.

A tendon pulled at a steady rate, and the loop it leaves (eq. 6.2.5 again; eq. 9.3.1)

$$\sigma = E_{slow}\,\varepsilon + (E_{fast} - E_{slow})\,\dot\varepsilon\,\tau_f\left(1 - e^{-\varepsilon/(\dot\varepsilon\,\tau_f)}\right),\qquad \text{share lost} = \frac{U_{pull} - U_{back}}{U_{pull}}$$
  • Symbolsσ, ε = the tendon's stress and strain · ε̇ = the strain rate · E_slow, E_fast = its modulus pulled slowly and pulled faster than its fast clock τ_f · U_pull = the work put in on the way up (the area under the pull) · U_back = the work returned on the way back.
  • What it saysPulled slowly the stress is E_slow ε; pulled much faster than τ_f it is E_fast ε; in between it lies between. The way back runs below the way up, and the loop between them is work left in the tendon as heat. Measured: about 7 % in an excised tendon and 18 % in a living calf, and a tendon's modulus barely changes over a tenfold change of speed.
  • When it appliesA pull and release in a movement, seconds or less.
  • Fails whenYou expect one clock to describe every speed: a real tendon has many.

Constants and bridges (cite them)

  • Units1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa · 1 kPa = 1 000 Pa = 1 000 pN/µm² · 1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa = 1 000 N/mm² · 1 mL/min = 1/60 mL/s · 1 W = 1 J/s · weight = mass × 9.81 m/s².
  • MuscleOne attached head: 3.5 pN (Finer 1994, 3–4 pN). Arterial smooth muscle's most stress: 220 kPa per area of wall (Murphy 1974). Skeletal muscle's: about 225 kPa = 22.5 N/cm² (Powell 1984).
  • FilamentsFrog: thick 1.6 µm, thin pair 2.05 µm, bare middle 0.15 µm (Gordon, Huxley & Julian 1966). Human: thick about 1.65 µm, thin 1.19–1.37 µm each (Gokhin 2012).
  • TendonThe calf's: modulus about 1 GPa, cross-section 60–80 mm², about a tenth less stiff after ten minutes of stretch (Magnusson 2003; Kubo 2001).
