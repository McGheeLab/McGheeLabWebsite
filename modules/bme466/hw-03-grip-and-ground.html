THE PROBLEMS

Challenge — the ground that flows graded for 566 · everyone should attempt

The situation. Week 6 §1.2 built up the force on an adhesion whose ground is a spring: the clutches load it, the force climbs toward the motors' stall, and the flow dies away. Now put the same adhesion on ground that flows. Hold a Maxwell element under a steady pull for much longer than its relaxation time and its spring's stretch stops changing; from then on only its damper takes up motion. So treat the ground as a damper of viscosity \(\eta_s\) (printed). Between the actin and that damper sits one spring of stiffness \(\kappa\) (printed): the adhesion's clutches and whatever spring the ground still has, already joined in series the way Week 6 §1.3 joined them. Above the spring the actin network runs on the motors' load line — \(N_m\) modules of \(F_m\) each, unloaded speed \(v_0\) (printed; the same cell as Problems 1 and 2). Below the damper the far ground never moves. At this edge the filament tips grow at \(v_{pol}\) (printed, measured).

The question: how much force can an adhesion build on ground that flows — and does this edge advance?

Work it entirely by hand, in your written solutions: nothing on this tab is recorded, and your exported results file carries no challenge fields. Let \(F(t)\) be the force the adhesion carries, with the clutch bound and unstretched at \(t = 0\). Your written solution must do three things:

  1. Set up the differential equation that \(F\) obeys. A way in: the spring's stretch changes at the rate the actin slides minus the rate the damper lets the ground go; write each of those two rates in terms of \(F\) — the actin's speed is the motors' load line, the damper's rate is its own law — and you have \(\dot F\) in terms of \(F\). It is first order, linear, with constant coefficients. Then decide, and justify from the physics, the condition at \(t = 0\) that pins down its solution.
  2. Solve it. Show what \(F\) becomes as \(t \to \infty\) and name the time on which it gets there. Then let the ground stop flowing (\(\eta_s \to \infty\)) and show that both the limiting force and its clock become Week 6's spring-ground result (§1.2). Say in one sentence what the flowing ground has cost the cell.
  3. Use it. Report the steady retrograde flow on this ground, the edge speed (does this edge advance or retract?), and the ground viscosity at which the edge would just break even. Compare that viscosity with the printed one.

Notes — equations & when they apply

Notes — every law of Weeks 5–6 this homework can use, and when each one applies

This sheet is your field guide. No question in this homework names its equation — you match the situation to the law, and every problem needs two. For each entry: what it says, the assumptions it stands on, and what breaks it. Nothing here is graded and nothing is hidden. Equation numbers are the weekly modules' (module.tab.order), so you can open the derivation any time.

How to search this sheet. Everything in Weeks 5 and 6 is one of three sentences dressed for a different stage: a force sets a rate (a Boltzmann factor on an arrival, a Bell factor on a departure, a load line on a motor); a force stretches a spring (a clutch, a gel, a nucleus — and springs join by two rules); a spring has a clock when a damper is beside or behind it. Ask three questions — what is pushing or pulling, what is resisting, what is the geometry or the clock? — then read the "when it applies" lines against your answers.

Notation (the course's): \(F\), \(f\) a force (pN; \(f\) for one bond) · \(x\), \(u\) a displacement (nm; \(u\) in a gel) · \(k\), \(\kappa\) a stiffness (pN/nm; \(\kappa_c\) a clutch, \(\kappa_s\) the ground under an adhesion) · \(\eta\) a viscosity (pN·s/nm) · \(E\), \(G\), \(\nu\) a material's modulus, shear modulus, Poisson ratio (kPa, kPa, –) · \(\sigma\), \(\varepsilon\), \(\mathbf t\) stress, strain, traction · \(v_0\) the motors' unloaded sliding speed · \(N_m F_m\) their stall · \(\dot f\) a rate of loading (the dot means \(d/dt\)) · \(k_0\), \(f_\beta\) a bond's unloaded off-rate and force scale · \(C\), \(C_c\) a concentration and the critical one · \(\delta\) the subunit rise.

The gap a load must open — Boltzmann on an arrival (eq. 1.2.7 → 5.1.1)

$$P(\text{gap} \ge \delta) = e^{-F\delta/k_BT}$$
  • SymbolsF = the load on the tip (pN) · δ = the gap a subunit needs (nm) · kBT = 4.28 pN·nm at 37 °C (constants below).
  • What it saysA subunit can only insert when thermal jiggle has opened a gap at least δ wide against the load; the odds of that are a Boltzmann factor on the work Fδ. It multiplies the arrival rate and nothing else — a departure does not need a gap.
  • When it appliesA tip pushing on something that jiggles freely in its absence; the load constant over a subunit's insertion time.
  • Fails whenPut on the departure term, or on both; or when δ in the exponent is not the same δ the subunit actually opens (see the angle, next entry).

The ratchet under load — speed, stall and the loaded threshold (eq. 4.5.1, 5.1.2, 5.1.3)

$$v = \delta_{eff}\left(k_{on}\,C\,e^{-F\delta_{eff}/k_BT} - k_{off}\right),\qquad C^*(F) = C_c\,e^{F\delta_{eff}/k_BT},\qquad F^* = \frac{k_BT}{\delta_{eff}}\ln\frac{C}{C_c},\qquad C_c = \frac{k_{off}}{k_{on}},\qquad \delta_{eff} = \delta\cos\theta$$
  • Symbolskon (µM⁻¹s⁻¹), koff (s⁻¹) = the barbed end's rates · C (µM) = free polymerization-competent actin at the tip · δ = 2.7 nm the rise per subunit · θ = the angle the filament makes with the membrane normal (≈ 35° in a lamellipodium; 0 for a filament head-on).
  • What it saysLoad slows the arrivals by the gap's Boltzmann factor; the departures are untouched. Three readings of one line: the speed at a given load; the concentration below which a loaded tip shrinks, Cc raised by the same factor; and the stall load at a given concentration — a logarithm, so doubling the pool buys only kBT ln 2/δ of force.
  • When it appliesOne tip, a well-mixed pool at constant C, a load that is steady over an insertion. In a lamellipodium the angle enters twice — the step the membrane sees is δ cos θ, and so is the distance the work is done over — so replace δ by δeff everywhere, or nowhere.
  • Fails whencos θ is applied to the speed only (≈ 18 % off); the Boltzmann factor is dropped (the unloaded ratchet, several times too fast under a few pN); C is below the loaded threshold and the sign goes negative without your noticing.

The motors' load line, the stall, the edge speed and break-even (eq. 4.2.4, 5.1.5, 5.3.1, 4.4.3, 5.1.6)

$$v_{retro} = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\right)\ \ (0 \le v_{retro} \le v_0),\qquad F_{stall} = N_mF_m,\qquad v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_{retro},\qquad F^*_{FA} = N_mF_m\left(1 - \frac{v_{pol}}{v_0}\right)$$
  • Symbolsv0 = the network's rearward speed with nothing holding it · Nm modules of Fm each (Bangasser 2017's lumped module: Fm = 2 pN, duty ratio already inside) · FFA = the force the adhesion carries, which is the load the motors feel · vpol = the tip's growth speed from the ratchet.
  • What it saysThe flow falls linearly with the force the adhesion takes off the motors, to zero at their stall. The edge moves at the tip's speed minus the flow; it advances only when the grip exceeds the break-even force, which is the stall scaled by how much slower the tip is than the free flow. The integrin contributes no force of its own — it is a force divider.
  • When it appliesA lumped motor ensemble (not a single head — Week 4's N r Fstep and 4 µm/s are a different object); grip below stall. At or above stall the flow is zero and the edge moves at vpol.
  • Fails whenThe ratio in the break-even is inverted; the flow is allowed to go negative; v0 of the motors is confused with v0 of an unloaded filament.

Hooke — for a clutch, a filament, a gel (eq. 5.5.1, 5.3.2, 6.1.4)

$$F = k\,x,\qquad f_i = \kappa_c\,x_i,\qquad F_{FA} = \kappa_s\,u_0 = \kappa_s\,x_s$$
  • Symbolsk, κ = a stiffness in pN/nm (the force per nanometre of stretch) · xi = how far clutch i has been dragged · u0, xs = how far the gel under the adhesion has moved.
  • What it saysForce is proportional to stretch; the constant belongs to one object — this clutch, this filament, the gel under this adhesion. The gel's spring is shared by every clutch on the patch.
  • When it appliesSmall stretches; no time in it (a spring has no clock on its own). Run it both ways: F → x by dividing, x → F by multiplying.
  • Fails whenA stiffness is quoted for a material (that is a modulus — see below); nm meets µm or pN meets nN (×1000 each).

Series and parallel — how any two elements join (eq. 6.2.1, 6.1.1)

$$\text{series:}\ \ F = F_A = F_B,\ x = x_A + x_B \Rightarrow \frac1k = \frac1{k_A}+\frac1{k_B};\qquad \text{parallel:}\ \ x = x_A = x_B,\ F = F_A + F_B \Rightarrow k = k_A + k_B;\qquad \kappa_{eff} = \frac{n\kappa_c\,\kappa_s}{n\kappa_c + \kappa_s}$$
  • What it saysEnd to end, the elements share one force and their stretches add — the softer one takes the larger share of the stretch. Side by side, they share one displacement and their forces add — the stiffer one carries more. n clutches side by side (nκc) in series with the gel (κs) is the chain under every adhesion; with one clutch, κeff = κcκs/(κc + κs).
  • When it appliesAny two elements with a shared joint. Decide which quantity the joint shares before writing anything: the control that sets the shared quantity needs no splitting.
  • Fails whenStiffnesses in series are added (that is parallel); the ground's stiffness is dropped because "the clutch is softer" — κeff is always below the smaller of the two.

The loading rate — what a flow does to a bond (eq. 5.2.2, and through the chain)

$$\dot f = \frac{df}{dt} = \kappa\,v,\qquad\text{through the chain:}\quad \dot f = \kappa_{eff}\,v_{retro} \approx \kappa_{eff}\,v_0\ \ \text{(early, before the grip slows the flow)}$$
  • What it saysA bond in a cell is not held at a force; it is ramped. Actin slides at v, the linkage stretches, and the load climbs at the linkage's stiffness times the speed. On ground that gives, the linkage is the whole chain, so the ground slows the loading in exactly the ratio κeff/κc.
  • When it appliesA freshly bound clutch dragged at a steady flow. Once many bonds hold, the flow itself falls (the load line) and so does \(\dot f\).
  • Fails whenWeek 5's r is read as a new quantity — it is the same \(\dot f\); the clutch stiffness alone is used on ground that gives.

Bell's law, and the catch bond that bends it (eq. 4.2.5, 5.2.3)

$$\tau(f) = \tau_0\,e^{-f/f_\beta},\qquad k_{off}(f) = \frac{1}{\tau(f)} = k_0\,e^{f/f_\beta},\qquad \tau_0 = \frac1{k_0};\qquad\text{catch:}\ \ k_{off}(f) = k_s e^{f/F_s} + k_c e^{-f/F_c}$$
  • Symbolsk0 = the off-rate with nothing pulling (1/k0 the unloaded lifetime) · fβ = the force that shortens the lifetime e-fold (kBT over the bond's own give) · a catch bond has two exits, one that force opens and one that force closes, and so a lifetime with a peak.
  • What it saysA slip bond's lifetime falls exponentially with the load on it: fβ of load costs a factor e, 2fβ a factor e², and so on. Read it as a lifetime when the question asks how long; as a rate when it asks how often.
  • When it appliesA bond held at a constant force. Under a ramp the lifetime at any one force is not the answer — integrate (the race, below).
  • Fails whenThe exponent's sign is slipped (a loaded bond living longer than an unloaded one); a catch bond's window is forgotten; a lifetime at the final force is used for the whole ramp.

Load sharing, and the adhesion's force (eq. 5.2.4, 5.3.4)

$$\bar f = \frac{F_{FA}}{N_{bound}},\qquad F_{FA} = \sum_{i\in\text{bound}} \kappa_c x_i = N_b\,\langle f\rangle$$
  • What it saysThe adhesion's force is the sum of what its bound clutches carry — their number times their mean — and on average each bound bond carries the total divided by how many are bound right now, not how many are present.
  • When it appliesCounting how many bonds a load needs at a sustainable force per bond (a few pN; talin's R3 gives at ≈ 5 pN, a vinculin–actin tether lives ten seconds at 7–10 pN).
  • Fails whenThe force a bond carries when it breaks is averaged in place of the force it carries while bound — the first is much larger.

The load builds to the motors' stall (eq. 6.1.2)

$$F_{FA}(t) = N_mF_m\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_L}\right),\qquad v_{retro}(t) = v_0\,e^{-t/\tau_L},\qquad \tau_L = \frac{N_mF_m}{\kappa_{eff}\,v_0}$$
  • What it saysPut the load line into the loading rate and the force on a spring ground climbs exponentially toward the stall while the flow dies away on the same clock — the stall divided by the initial loading rate. Derived in Week 6 §1.2 from \(\dot F = \kappa_{eff} v_0 (1 - F/N_mF_m)\) with F(0) = 0.
  • When it appliesClutches that hold (no unbinding) on a ground that is a spring. The challenge asks what changes when the ground flows.
  • Fails whenBonds let go before the stall is reached — then the force is set by the race below, not by this clock.

The race to talin's threshold — a ramped bond on a ground that gives (eq. 6.1.3)

$$P(\text{reach }F_T) = \underbrace{e^{-K/\kappa_c}}_{\text{rigid ground}}\times\underbrace{e^{-K/\kappa_s}}_{\text{the ground's cost}} = e^{-K/\kappa_{eff}},\qquad K = \frac{k_0 f_\beta}{v_0}\left(e^{F_T/f_\beta} - 1\right)$$
  • SymbolsFT = the load at which talin unfolds and vinculin reinforces (≈ 12 pN, del Rio 2009) · K = a stiffness built from the bond and the flow alone: larger for a bond that lets go faster or a threshold that sits higher, smaller for a faster flow.
  • What it saysBell's rate integrated along the ramp \(f = \dot f t\) with P(0) = 1 (Week 6 §1.3's Derive box). The ground's whole effect is one factor, e−K/κs: a ground much stiffer than K costs nothing, a ground equal to K cuts the odds to 1/e, a ground much softer all but ends them. Soft ground does not weaken the bond — it slows the loading until the bond almost always lets go first.
  • When it appliesOne bond, loaded from zero at the chain's rate, a slip bond. The ramp spends most of its time at low force where the bond is long-lived — which is why the odds are far better than e−tT/τ(FT), the two-times estimate.
  • Fails whenThe odds are taken as a comparison of two times; κs is dropped (that is the rigid-ground factor alone); v0 is entered in µm/s.

From a modulus to a spring — and stress, strain, modulus (eq. 6.3.1, 5.5.2)

$$\kappa_s = \frac49\,\pi E\,a \approx 1.4\,E\,a\quad(E\ \text{in kPa},\ a\ \text{in µm}\ \Rightarrow\ \kappa_s\ \text{in pN/nm});\qquad \sigma = \frac FA,\quad \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L},\quad \sigma = E\varepsilon,\quad k = \frac{EA}{L}$$
  • SymbolsE = the gel's Young's modulus (kPa; brain ≈ 0.2–1, muscle ≈ 12, a dish ≈ 10⁶) · a = the adhesion's radius (µm) — the grip's size · A, L = a bar's cross-section and length.
  • What it saysA stiffness belongs to an object; a modulus to a material. Grip the same gel through an adhesion twice as wide and the spring doubles. The 4/9 π is the half-space answer at ν = ½ (Saez 2005, eq. 2); run it backwards to turn Tab 1's best spring into a gel.
  • When it appliesA tangential pull through a disc on a thick, linear, incompressible gel. Mind the bridge: 1 kPa·µm = 10³ pN/µm² × 1 µm = 10³ pN/µm = 1 pN/nm.
  • Fails whenE is in Pa, a in nm, or a prefactor from a different geometry (πEa; Mindlin's rigid punch 8Ga/(2 − ν)) is mixed in; a thin film under the cell (then the film's own rule, below).

The reach of a pull, and reading beads backwards (eq. 6.1.5, 6.1.6)

$$u(r) = u_0\,\frac{a}{r}\times\begin{cases}\tfrac23 & \text{in line with the pull}\\ \tfrac13 & \text{beside the pull}\end{cases},\qquad u_0 = \frac{F}{\kappa_s};\qquad \mathbf u(\mathbf x) = \sum_k \mathbf K(\mathbf x - \mathbf x_k)\,\mathbf F_k$$
  • What it saysA pull is felt everywhere in the gel, falling as 1/r, and twice as strongly in line with it as beside it. Everything scales with u0 = F/κs: soften the gel and every bead moves more by the same factor. Read forwards it predicts the beads; read backwards one bead and the ground's spring give the pull, and many beads with several adhesions make a least-squares problem — traction force microscopy.
  • When it appliesBeads two or more adhesion radii away (within 0.1 % in line; ≈ 9 % low beside at 2a, 2 % at 4a); a thick gel; small displacements.
  • Fails whenThe direction's factor is dropped or swapped; r/a is forgotten (the bead's displacement taken as the adhesion's); nm and µm meet inside a/r.

Strain, traction, the shear modulus, a thin film, and the balance (eq. 6.3.2–6.3.6)

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(\mathbf u_2 - \mathbf u_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}}{L},\qquad \mathbf t = \boldsymbol\sigma\,\mathbf n,\qquad G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}\ (= E/3\ \text{at}\ \nu = \tfrac12),\qquad t = \frac{G\,u}{h}\ \text{(thin film)},\qquad \sum_k \mathbf t_k\,\Delta A_k = \mathbf 0$$
  • What it saysA strain is a difference of two displacements over the distance between them; a traction is the force per area on a cut, in Pa (1 pN/µm² = 1 Pa); a material needs E, ν and G, tied together; on a film much thinner than the adhesion the shear strain is u/h and the traction reads locally; and the tractions under a free cell sum to zero.
  • When it appliesTurning a force on an adhesion into a traction: divide by the area that carries it, πa² for a disc — and say so, because a traction map spreads the force over however much area the method assigns.
  • Fails whenThe diameter's area is used (×¼); nN meets µm²; a thin-film rule is applied to a thick gel or the reverse.

The damper, Maxwell, Kelvin–Voigt — elements with a clock (eq. 6.2.2, 6.2.3, 6.2.4)

$$F = \eta\,\dot x;\qquad \text{Maxwell (series):}\ \dot F + \frac F\tau = k\,\dot x,\ \tau = \frac\eta k,\ \ x = vt \Rightarrow F = \eta v\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right);\qquad \text{Kelvin–Voigt (parallel):}\ F = kx + \eta\dot x$$
  • What it saysA damper's force is set by the rate of stretch, not the stretch. Spring and damper in series (Maxwell) answer a steady pull like the spring at first (F ≈ kvt) and like the damper late (F → ηv): the force is capped by the speed, not by how far the ground has been dragged. Side by side (Kelvin–Voigt) they add forces and the element creeps toward F/k on τ = η/k.
  • When it appliesDecide which quantity is controlled (force or displacement) and which joint shares it; the direct case needs no equation, the other is a first-order ODE whose boundary condition you must argue (a damper cannot jump; a spring in series with it can).
  • Fails whenA Maxwell element under a held stretch is expected to hold its force (it relaxes to zero); a Kelvin–Voigt element is stepped (the damper's force is infinite); τ is read from the wrong pair of parts.

The standard linear solid — two stiffnesses, two clocks (eq. 6.2.5; and 6.2.6, 6.2.7)

$$F + \tau\dot F = k_\infty x + \tau k_0\dot x,\qquad k_0 = k_1 + k_\infty,\quad \tau = \frac{\eta}{k_1},\quad \tau_\varepsilon = \tau\frac{k_0}{k_\infty};\qquad x(t)\big|_{F_0\ \text{held}} = \frac{F_0}{k_\infty} - \left(\frac{F_0}{k_\infty} - \frac{F_0}{k_0}\right)e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}$$
  • Symbolsk∞ = the lone spring (what is left after everything has relaxed) · k1, η = the Maxwell arm beside it · k0 = the instantaneous stiffness, both springs at once · τ = the relaxation clock (held stretch) · τε = the creep clock (held force), longer by k0/k∞.
  • What it saysPull fast and you meet k0; wait and you meet k∞. Hold a stretch and the force relaxes from k0x to k∞x on τ; hold a force and the stretch creeps from F/k0 to F/k∞ on τε — starting at F/k0, not at zero, because the damper has had no time to move. The simplest solid that relaxes to a nonzero force and creeps to a finite stretch; a gel, a cell, a nucleus. A single-spring surrogate keff(t) = F/x (eq. 6.2.6) exists for each loading but is not one number; the honest use of a relaxation stiffness is Boltzmann's sum over the history (eq. 6.2.7).
  • When it appliesLinear, small strains, a single relaxation process. Which clock you see depends on which quantity the experiment holds fixed — name it.
  • Fails whenτ is quoted where τε belongs (short by k∞/k0); k1 is taken for k0; the creep is started from x = 0.

Through a pore — the nucleus as a creeping element (eq. 6.5.1)

$$x(t_{pass}) = \Delta = D_{nucleus} - D_{pore}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{pass} = -\tau_\varepsilon\,\ln\frac{F_0/k_\infty - \Delta}{F_0/k_\infty - F_0/k_0}$$
  • What it saysUnder a held push F0 the nucleus creeps (previous entry); it passes the moment its squeeze reaches Δ. Three regimes, read off the two stiffnesses: at once if F0/k0 ≥ Δ (the instantaneous squeeze already covers it — F0 ≥ k0Δ); never if F0/k∞ ≤ Δ (even the eventual squeeze falls short — F0 ≤ k∞Δ); between them a wait on the creep clock that grows without bound as the pore closes toward the never-threshold.
  • When it appliesA push held steady; the nucleus deforming as the standard linear solid; Δ measured in the same unit as the squeeze (nm with pN/nm).
  • Fails whenThe logarithm's ratio is inverted (a negative time); µm meets nm or nN meets pN inside it; the regime is not checked first.

Constants and bridges (cite them)

$$k_BT = 4.28\ \text{pN·nm at 37 °C};\quad \delta = 2.7\ \text{nm};\quad \theta \approx 35^\circ;\quad F_m = 2\ \text{pN};\quad 1\ \text{kPa·µm} = 1\ \text{pN/nm};\quad 1\ \text{pN/µm}^2 = 1\ \text{Pa};\quad 1\ \text{nN} = 10^3\ \text{pN};\quad 1\ \text{µm} = 10^3\ \text{nm}$$
  • Where they come fromkBT = 0.01380649 × T(K) pN·nm — 4.28 at 310 K (the cell), 4.14 at 300 K. δ = 2.7 nm per subunit along the filament (Holmes, Popp, Gebhard & Kabsch 1990, Nature 347:44). θ ≈ 35°: Arp2/3 branches at 70°, so lamellipodial filaments run at ±35° to the membrane normal (Week 5 §1.2). Fm = 2 pN, v0 = 120 nm/s, κc = 0.8 pN/nm, k0 = 0.1 s⁻¹, fβ = 2 pN: Bangasser et al. 2017, Suppl. Table 2 (Week 5 Tab 3's cast). FT ≈ 12 pN: del Rio et al. 2009. The 4/9 π: Saez et al. 2005, eq. 2.
  • The bridges, derived once1 kPa = 10³ Pa = 10³ N/m² = 10³ pN/µm² (a piconewton is 10⁻¹² N and a square micron is 10⁻¹² m²), so 1 kPa × 1 µm = 10³ pN/µm = 1 pN/nm. The fingerprints of a slip: ×1000 is a unit, ×0.25 or ×4 is a radius–diameter, ×2 or ×½ is an in-line–beside or a 2/3.

How to work a problem whose givens are printed

  • Symbols firstWrite the relation in letters, check its units and its limits (what happens as the ground goes rigid, as the load goes to zero, as the gap opens), and only then put numbers in. A limit that gives back an earlier week's result is your check.
  • Two relations, one situationEvery problem here needs two laws from different parts of the modules. Name which quantity the first one hands to the second; part (b) is usually built on your own part (a), so an error in (a) carries — and the grader follows your chain, not the key.
  • Signs and regimesAn edge speed can be negative; a probability cannot exceed 1; a loaded bond cannot outlive an unloaded one; a stiffer gel cannot move more under the same pull; a push between two thresholds gives a wait, outside them an instant or a never. Check the regime before the arithmetic.
  • Your error barHow much the answer moves across defensible choices (37 °C or 25 °C, 1.4 or 4π/9, 2a or the exact adhesion) is your uncertainty; say it.
