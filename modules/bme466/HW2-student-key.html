The mistakes that cost the most

The first is the error the class actually made most often on this homework; the rest are the slips the problems are built around. Each is worked through under its problem below.

  1. Problem 5 (a): a 2 or a 6 in the Péclet number. About a third of the class. The Péclet number the module defines (eq. 4.1.9) is \(\mathrm{Pe} = vL/D\): the diffusion time in it is \(L^{2}/D\), with no dimension factor. The \(L^{2}/2dD\) of eq. 1.2.8 answers a different question — how long a walker takes to first reach \(L\) — and carrying its 2 or 6 into Pe halves or sixths the answer.
  2. Problem 4 (a): one end’s threshold for the balance point. The barbed end’s own \(C_c\) is where that end stalls; the filament treadmills between the two thresholds, where both ends are in the balance — \(C_{ss} = (k_{off}^B + k_{off}^P)/(k_{on}^B + k_{on}^P)\). Because the barbed end dominates, the wrong number is often close to the right one; the written two-end balance is what shows you did it.
  3. Problem 1 (c): \(a_s\) left in nm. \(N a_s\) and \(\pi a\) are two lengths and must share a unit. Left in nm, both fractions pin at ≈ 1 and the verdict follows the slip.
  4. The Microscope: the fastest run called the plateau. A sweep that never reached saturation under-reports \(v_{max}\); the plateau is where the Hanes line points, not the top of your dial.
  5. The nM bridge, in both directions. 1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³: the receptor slide’s slope is divided by it, a \(K_D\) in molecules/µm³ is divided by it. Derive it once and the direction takes care of itself.
  6. Units at the seams. µm/min against seconds (Problem 5 b), µm/s against nm (Problem 4 d), pN·nm/s reported as aW (Problem 3), \(r_G\) in nm inside a ceiling in µm²/s (Problem 4 e). Convert before the first multiplication, and write the unit on every line.

Receptor slide — the capture rate and the release rate

Given. Your kept runs: at each dial setting C (nM, dial 0.3–300) the plateau occupancy θ, the rise time to 63 % of the plateau with ligand present, and the fall time to 37 % of it after the wash. Ns = 400 sites in view is printed on the slide.
1 Choose the relation
The Langmuir door’s relaxation: the rise rate is \(k_{on}C + k_{off}\), the wash rate is \(k_{off}\) alone
Independent sites filling from a step in \(C\) relax exponentially to their plateau with rate \(k_{on}C + k_{off}\) (eq. 3.2.5), so the time to 63 % is \(\tau_{\rm rise} = 1/(k_{on}C + k_{off})\) — and \(1/\tau_{\rm rise}\) against \(C\) is a straight line whose slope is \(k_{on}\) and whose intercept is \(k_{off}\). After the wash there is no ligand left to rebind, so the decay to 37 % knows only \(k_{off}\): \(1/\tau_{\rm fall} = k_{off}\). Two independent routes to \(k_{off}\); the wash is the cleaner one. The dial reads nM, so the slope comes out in nM⁻¹s⁻¹ and must cross the bridge (÷0.6022) to µm³/s.
Where it is taught. Notes › “How fast the door settles — the relaxation time” and “The concentration bridge” · Week 3 §2.3 “Revolving doors: Langmuir” (eq. 3.2.4–3.2.9) and §3.7 (eq. 3.3.13, \(\tau_c = 1/(k_{on}C + k_{off}) = (1 - \theta)/k_{off}\)) · rehearsed on the ★ Practice tab’s Example 1 (the calibration slide).
2 Set it up
$$\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm rise}} = k_{on}C + k_{off},\qquad \frac{1}{\tau_{\rm fall}} = k_{off}$$
eq. 3.2.5; the wash
$$k_{on}\,[\mathrm{µm^{3}/s}] = \frac{\text{slope}\ [\mathrm{nM^{-1}s^{-1}}]}{0.6022}$$
the bridge: 1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³

Bracketing rule: kept usable runs with \(\theta < 0.5\) and \(\theta > 0.5\) (the half-coverage point is \(C = K_D\)), at ≥ 4 settings, ≥ 5 runs. Tag runs that never reached their plateau “noisy” — the first-visit coach says so.

3 Put the numbers in
$$\begin{aligned}\text{ideal line: }\ \frac{1}{\tau_{\rm rise}} &= 0.01505\,C + 0.118\quad(C\ \text{in nM})\qquad\Rightarrow\quad k_{on} = \frac{0.01505}{0.6022} = 0.025\ \mathrm{µm^{3}/s},\quad k_{off} = 0.118\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \\ \text{the wash: }\ \tau_{\rm fall} &= \frac{1}{0.118} = 8.47\ \mathrm{s}\ \text{at every setting}\qquad \theta = ½\ \text{at}\ C = K_D = 7.84\ \mathrm{nM},\ \text{where}\ \tau_{\rm rise} = \frac{1}{2k_{off}} = 4.24\ \mathrm{s}\end{aligned}$$

At C = 1 nM, for scale: θ = 0.113, τrise = 7.52 s, τfall = 8.47 s. Honest five-run sweeps on this dataset landed within a few percent of both numbers (three hand-drives, worst 13 %).

Answer. \(k_{on} = 0.025\) µm³/s and \(k_{off} = 0.118\) s⁻¹
A complete write-up also shows
  • The axes justified: why \(1/\tau_{\rm rise}\) against \(C\) is a straight line and what its slope and intercept mean — a sketch of the sweep with the line drawn is the clearest way.
  • The nM → µm³ bridge crossed once, the right way, with the sentence that says why (a rate per molecule per volume).
  • The second route to \(k_{off}\) — the wash against the intercept — and how far apart the two came out.
  • An uncertainty from which runs you called usable. Runs tagged noisy for a stated reason (never reached the plateau; pinned at the top of the dial) are good practice.
Common mistakes
  • 0.0689 on this dataset. The bridge crossed backwards (×0.6022 instead of ÷). Derive the bridge once: 1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³, so a rate per nM is divided by 0.6022 to become a rate per molecule per µm³.
  • 0.0415 on this dataset. The slope left in nM⁻¹s⁻¹ — no bridge. µm³/s and nM⁻¹s⁻¹ are two units of the same rate constant; the Notes’ bridge converts them.
  • 0.236 on this dataset. \(\tau_{\rm rise}\) at the midpoint read as \(1/k_{off}\) (it is \(1/(2k_{off})\) there). At \(C = K_D\) the rise rate is \(2k_{off}\), so \(\tau_{\rm rise}\) there is \(1/(2k_{off})\); only the wash gives \(1/k_{off}\) directly.

Actin slide — the barbed end’s on-rate and its threshold

Given. Your kept runs: at each free-monomer setting C (µM, dial 0.02–20) the signed elongation rate of the barbed end and of the pointed end, in subunits per second, with the instrument’s ± on the barbed rate.
1 Choose the relation
A filament end is a door: the growth rate is a straight line in \(C\), slope \(k_{on}\), zero crossing \(C_c = k_{off}/k_{on}\)
Each end adds subunits at \(k_{on}C\) and loses them at \(k_{off}\), so its rate in subunits/s is \(k_{on}C - k_{off}\) (eq. 4.1.1 with \(\delta\) divided out) — a straight line whose slope is the on-rate and whose \(C\)-axis intercept is the critical concentration below which that end shrinks (eq. 4.1.3). Each end has its own line; the barbed end’s is the steep one. The pointed end’s line is read from the same sweep and feeds Problem 4, but it is not one of the six earned numbers.

2 Set it up
$$\frac{v}{\delta} = k_{on}C - k_{off}\ \ [\mathrm{subunits/s}],\qquad C_c = \frac{k_{off}}{k_{on}}$$
eq. 4.1.1, eq. 4.1.3 — one line per end

Bracketing rule: at least one usable run with the barbed end shrinking (\(v < 0\)) and one growing (\(v > 0\)), so the line interpolates to its zero crossing. The sweep plot fits weighted by the instrument’s ±.

A complete write-up also shows

Myosin slide — the limiting gliding speed and the half-saturation ATP

Given. Your kept runs: at each [ATP] setting (µM, dial 1–5000) the gliding speed in µm/s, with its ±.
1 Choose the relation
Michaelis–Menten for a saturating rate: \(v = v_{max}[\mathrm{ATP}]/(K_M + [\mathrm{ATP}])\), straightened on Hanes axes
A head cycles once per ATP, and the cycle rate saturates with its substrate exactly as an enzyme’s does (eq. 3.2.15, reused for the motor in Week 4 §2.3); a filament gliding over a dense lawn reports that cycle rate directly as a speed. The speed at the highest setting is only a lower bound on the plateau; the shape of the whole sweep gives the limit. On Hanes axes — \([\mathrm{ATP}]/v\) against \([\mathrm{ATP}]\) — the hyperbola is a straight line with slope \(1/v_{max}\) and intercept \(K_M/v_{max}\) (the module also offers \(1/v\) against \(1/[\mathrm{ATP}]\)).
Where it is taught. Notes › “The door with a third exit — Michaelis–Menten and K_M” and “Motors” · Week 3 §2.7 “The door that eats — Michaelis–Menten” (eq. 3.2.13–3.2.15; the linearizations) · Week 4 §2.3 “Power output of a single motor” (eq. 3.2.15 again — “the cycle rate’s ATP dependence”).
2 Set it up
$$v = \frac{v_{max}[\mathrm{ATP}]}{K_M + [\mathrm{ATP}]} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{[\mathrm{ATP}]}{v} = \frac{1}{v_{max}}[\mathrm{ATP}] + \frac{K_M}{v_{max}}$$
eq. 3.2.15; Hanes form

Bracketing rule: the fastest usable kept speed at least three times the slowest, so the sweep spans the bend. Half of the plateau is reached at \([\mathrm{ATP}] = K_M\); 90 % needs \(9K_M\).

3 Put the numbers in
$$\begin{aligned}\text{ideal Hanes line: slope } \frac{1}{v_{max}} &= 0.200\ \mathrm{s/µm} \;\Rightarrow\; v_{max} = 5\ \mathrm{µm/s} \\ \text{intercept } \frac{K_M}{v_{max}} &= 20.0\ \mathrm{µM\,s/µm} \;\Rightarrow\; K_M = 20.0\times 5 = 100\ \mathrm{µM}\end{aligned}$$

For scale: at the top of the dial (5 000 µM) the speed is 4.90 µm/s — within 2 % of the plateau, so reading it off there is legitimate; at 200 µM it is only 3.33 µm/s.

Answer. \(v_{max} = 5\) µm/s and \(K_M = 100\) µM
A complete write-up also shows
  • The straightening axes named and the two parameters read from the line’s slope and intercept — not “the fastest speed I saw”, unless your sweep demonstrably reached saturation.
  • Why the gliding speed reports the cycle rate (one stroke per ATP; a dense lawn of heads).
  • A sweep that spans the bend: the fastest usable speed at least three times the slowest.
Common mistakes
  • 3.33 on this dataset. Stopped sweeping at 200 µM and called the fastest run the plateau. The plateau is where the Hanes line’s slope points, not the top of your sweep; a sweep that stops early under-reports \(v_{max}\) by the factor \([\mathrm{ATP}]/([\mathrm{ATP}] + K_M)\).
  • 900 on this dataset. The 90 % point reported as the half point. Half of the plateau is reached at \([\mathrm{ATP}] = K_M\); 90 % needs \(9K_M\).

(a) The concentration at which half the receptors are occupied

Given. Your own kon and koff from the receptor slide.
1 Choose the relation
The Langmuir isotherm: \(\theta_{eq} = K_{eq}C/(1 + K_{eq}C)\) with \(K_{eq} = k_{on}/k_{off}\) — half full at \(C = 1/K_{eq} = k_{off}/k_{on}\), then the bridge to nM
At equilibrium the filling rate \(k_{on}C(1 - \theta)\) equals the emptying rate \(k_{off}\theta\) (eq. 3.2.4 with \(d\theta/dt = 0\)), which gives the isotherm; it is exactly half full where \(K_{eq}C = 1\). \(K_{eq}\) is a volume (µm³), so its reciprocal is a concentration in molecules per µm³ — biochemists call it \(K_D\) — and the bridge 1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³ turns it into nM. Equilibrium applies because the transient (\(\tau \approx 1/(k_{on}C + k_{off})\), seconds here) is long over on the timescale of a cell’s reading.

$$\theta_{eq} = \frac{K_{eq}C}{1 + K_{eq}C},\quad K_{eq} = \frac{k_{on}}{k_{off}} \;\Rightarrow\; C_{½} = \frac{1}{K_{eq}} = \frac{k_{off}}{k_{on}}$$
eq. 3.2.7, 3.2.8
$$C_{½}\,[\mathrm{nM}] = \frac{k_{off}/k_{on}\ [\mathrm{molecules/µm^{3}}]}{0.6022}$$
the bridge
3 Put the numbers in

(b) How many times more ligand the neutrophil intercepts, as a perfect adsorber

Given. aN = 10 µm, aB = 1.5 µm; the ligand’s D in tissue fluid.
1 Choose the relation
The diffusion-limited encounter ceiling: arrivals per second = \(4\pi D a C\) — proportional to the radius, not the area
A perfectly adsorbing sphere consumes everything that reaches it, so the steady flux into it is Smoluchowski’s \(4\pi DaC\): the ceiling on every capture rate, and it scales with the first power of the radius because the diffusive supply through larger shells grows as fast as the surface does. Both cells see the same \(D\) and \(C\), so the ratio is simply \(a_N/a_B\). This is a checkpoint: it is computable from printed givens alone, so it is recorded and carries no credit — its job is to make you find the encounter law before (c).
Where it is taught. Notes › “Encounters — the diffusion-limited ceiling (eq. 1.5.1)” · Week 1 §5.1 “The hungry receptor” (eq. 1.5.1), derived in Week 2 §3; Week 3 §1.1 “Arrival” restates it as the delivery to a pocket.
2 Set it up
$$\frac{J_N}{J_B} = \frac{4\pi D a_N C}{4\pi D a_B C} = \frac{a_N}{a_B}$$
eq. 1.5.1 — \(D\) and \(C\) cancel
3 Put the numbers in
$$\text{ratio} = \frac{10}{1.5} = 6.67$$

For scale, with \(D_L = 134.8\) µm²/s: \(k_s = 4\pi D_L a\) is 16938 µm³/s for the neutrophil and 2541 µm³/s for the bacterium — multiply by \(C\) in molecules/µm³ for arrivals per second.

Answer. 6.67× — the ratio of the radii
A complete write-up also shows
  • Why the ceiling is linear in the radius: the diffusive supply through larger shells grows as fast as the surface does.
  • The ratio of radii, with \(D\) and \(C\) cancelling symbolically.
Common mistakes

(c) The fraction of that ceiling N receptors of radius as achieve — and (d) the verdict

Given. N = 20 000 receptors of capture radius as = 1 nm, spread evenly over a sphere of radius a (aN = 10 µm, aB = 1.5 µm).
1 Choose the relation
Berg & Purcell’s patchy sphere: \(J/J_{\rm perfect} = N a_s/(N a_s + \pi a)\) — a ratio of lengths, not areas
\(N\) small perfectly absorbing patches of radius \(a_s\) on a sphere of radius \(a\) capture a fraction \(N a_s/(N a_s + \pi a)\) of what the whole sphere would — the surprising result that a cell covered a fraction of a percent in receptors captures nearly as well as a perfect absorber, because a molecule that misses one patch diffuses along the surface and gets many more chances. Half the ceiling needs only \(N = \pi a/a_s\) receptors. It assumes the patches are independent, evenly spaced and each a perfect sink; \(a_s\) must be in the same unit as \(a\).
Where it is taught. Notes › “Reading a concentration — the decision band and the counting floor” (the patchy-sphere line, eq. 2.4.7) · Week 2 §4.4 “Does it fire, and when?” (eq. 2.4.7, Berg & Purcell 1977), recalled in Week 3 §3.6 as “Week 2’s loose end” and §3.7 (eq. 3.3.15, where the same ratio appears as the Damköhler number \(\mathrm{Da} = N k_{on}/(4\pi D a)\) with \(k_{on} = 4\pi D a_s\)).
2 Set it up
$$\frac{J}{J_{\rm perfect}} = \frac{N a_s}{N a_s + \pi a}$$
eq. 2.4.7 — \(a_s\) and \(a\) in the same unit

Ceiling test for (d): a cell is “at its ceiling” when this fraction is ≥ 0.9. To reach it a cell needs \(N a_s \ge 9\pi a\), i.e. \(N \ge 9\pi a/a_s\).

The Challenge, worked — the walk to the wound

20 points · graded for 566 students; attempted by everyone, and for 466 its weight redistributes. Solved entirely by hand: the module records nothing for it. Which limit is honest differs by dataset — that is deliberate.

The question. A bacterial colony doubles every \(t_{double}\); the nearest capillary is \(L_c\) away. A neutrophil leaves it and crawls at \(v_{cell}\) but forgets its heading on \(\tau_p\) (\(d\langle\mathbf v\rangle/dt = -\langle\mathbf v\rangle/\tau_p\)), with no gradient to steer it. How long until its rms displacement reaches the colony — and has the colony doubled first?

Required: (1) the ODE for \(G(t) = \langle r^{2}\rangle\) via \(H = \langle\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\rangle\), and the two conditions at \(t = 0\) justified from the physics; (2) its solution and both limits, naming what multiplies \(t\) in the long-time limit; (3) the time at which \(\sqrt G = L_c\), in minutes, with the limit it falls in checked, against \(t_{double}\) and against a straight crawl.

Sample dataset: L_c = 200 µm v_cell = 10 µm/min = 0.1667 µm/s τ_p = 180 s t_double = 25 min
The chain at a glance — the equations used, and how they combine
1 · product rule on r·r and on r·v, with the relaxation law; G(0) = 0 (starts at the capillary), G′(0) = 2H(0) = 0 (r = 0)

Step 1 — Set up the equation, and decide the conditions at t = 0

Let \(G = \langle\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r\rangle\) and \(H = \langle\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\rangle\). The product rule, the relaxation law, and the fact that the random part of the turning is uncorrelated with where the cell is:

$$\frac{dG}{dt} = 2\langle\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\rangle = 2H,\qquad \frac{dH}{dt} = \langle\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v\rangle + \Bigl\langle\mathbf r\cdot\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}\Bigr\rangle = v^{2} - \frac{H}{\tau_p}$$
the random turning averages to nothing against \(\mathbf r\)
$$G'' + \frac{G'}{\tau_p} = 2v^{2}$$
eliminate \(H\): second order, linear, constant coefficients, inhomogeneous
The conditions are \(G(0) = 0\) and \(G'(0) = 0\), and you must argue both. \(G(0) = 0\): the cell starts where it left the capillary, no displacement yet. \(G'(0) = 2H(0) = 2\langle\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\rangle = 0\) because \(\mathbf r = 0\) at \(t = 0\) — equivalently, the walk starts ballistic, \(G \approx v^{2}t^{2}\), whose slope at the origin is zero.
Wrong conditions seen: \(G'(0) = v^{2}\) or \(2v^{2}\) (\(G'\) has units µm²/s, \(v^{2}\) has µm²/s² — a catch you should make); \(G'(0) = v\); a “reflecting wall” or other spatial boundary — this is an equation in time, and its two conditions are initial values.

Step 2 — Solve it, and take both limits

Homogeneous solution \(A + Be^{-t/\tau_p}\); particular solution \(2v^{2}\tau_p\,t\) (linear in \(t\) — try it). \(G(0) = 0\) gives \(A = -B\); \(G'(0) = 0\) gives \(B/\tau_p + 2v^{2}\tau_p = 0\), so \(B = -2v^{2}\tau_p^{2}\):

$$G(t) = 2v^{2}\tau_p\Bigl[t - \tau_p\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau_p}\bigr)\Bigr]$$
eq. 4.3.6 — derived, not quoted
$$t \ll \tau_p:\quad G \approx v^{2}t^{2}\qquad\qquad t \gg \tau_p:\quad G \approx 2v^{2}\tau_p\,t = 4D_{cell}\,t,\quad D_{cell} = \frac{v^{2}\tau_p}{2}$$
ballistic (expand the exponential to second order) · diffusive (drop it)
The quantity multiplying \(t\) in the long-time limit is \(4D_{cell}\), with \(D_{cell} = v^{2}\tau_p/2\) in two dimensions (the module’s convention). A student who says \(6D\) for three dimensions and states it is also right — the identification changes, \(G\) does not.

Step 3 — When does the rms excursion reach the colony?

Set \(G(T) = L_c^{2}\). Which limit applies differs by dataset — that is deliberate — so you must compute \(T/\tau_p\) and justify the form you use. In the diffusive limit the root is explicit: \(T \approx L_c^{2}/(2v^{2}\tau_p) + \tau_p\) (the exponential is dead, so the \(-\tau_p\) inside the bracket just shifts the clock).

$$\begin{aligned}2v^{2}\tau_p &= 2\times 0.1667^{2}\times 180 = 10.00\ \mathrm{µm^{2}/s}\qquad D_{cell} = 2.50\ \mathrm{µm^{2}/s} \\ T_{diff} &= \frac{200^{2}}{10.00} = 4000\ \mathrm{s} = 66.7\ \mathrm{min} \\ \text{exact root of } G(T) = L_c^{2}:\quad T &= 4180\ \mathrm{s} = 69.7\ \mathrm{min} \\ \text{ballistic form (also the straight crawl): }\ \frac{L_c}{v} &= 1200\ \mathrm{s} = 20.0\ \mathrm{min}\end{aligned}$$
\(T/\tau_p = 23.2\) → diffusive: the limit form is honest
Answer. T ≈ 70 min (diffusive; the long-time limit form gives 67 min), against a doubling time of 25 min — the colony doubles 2.8 times (×6.9 cells) before an unbiased neutrophil’s rms excursion reaches it; a straight crawl would take 20 min.
