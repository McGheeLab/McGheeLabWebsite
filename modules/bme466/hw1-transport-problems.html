The scenario

A vendor sells your lab a fluorescently tagged antibody, suspended in a crowding medium. The datasheet gives the parts — bare antibody 150 kDa, hydrodynamic radius 5.6 nm (by DLS); 30 kDa per tag — and the medium’s viscosity (printed as η_rel × water at your T). It does not give the number of tags per antibody: that is proprietary, and it sets the conjugate’s size.

You own two instruments — a tracking microscope and a photobleaching rig. Characterize the conjugate once, with both, then spend that measurement on five biological questions and one challenge. Every number you use is one you measured or one printed in your dataset; every constant you look up, you cite.

Constants live in Notes (k_B, the nM bridge, documented geometric constants). Citing them from Notes is fine; citing the primary source is better.

Bench 1 — Track it [with Bench 2: 20 %]

Run tracking sessions on your particle. Record ≥ 4 fit windows you trust (≥ 3 dilute) and ≥ 2 crowded-scenario windows into the table. In-module answers: your best D (from your dilute windows — the conversion is yours to find, and remember what a camera actually records) and the crowded exponent α.

In the PDF (a) justify your fit-window choices against the noise floor and the long-lag scatter (a sketch of the MSD with your window marked is ideal); (b) name the relation you used to convert the raw fit into D and say why its dimensionality is right; (c) state your uncertainty on D from the spread across your windows.

Bench 2 — Bleach it

Bleach ≥ 4 spot sizes, ≥ 5 rows. In-module answer: a second, independent D from your stopwatch readings — the relation is a citable classic of the photobleaching literature; find it, cite it. In the PDF: (a) show the exponent your own τ½-vs-w data claims before you convert anything; (b) the conversion, symbolically first.

Problem 1 — Size it, count its tags, get it through a film [15 %]

Your datasheet viscosity, your measured D, your temperature, and the QC numbers above. (a) the conjugate’s hydrodynamic radius, in nm — set it beside the unlabeled antibody in the PDF; (b) how many fluorescent tags each antibody carries, on average — for compact proteins, molecular weight and hydrodynamic radius convert into each other; find the conversion, anchor it to the QC pair (150 kDa ↔︎ 5.6 nm), and defend it. Mass-to-count is then simple accounting: N = (conjugate mass − 150 kDa) ÷ 30 kDa per tag; (c) the steady flux (per µm², per second) of conjugates across the porous film in your dataset (thickness L, held difference ΔC). Defend every relation and its assumptions (what must be steady? what must be dilute?).

Problem 2 — The crowding tax [10 %]

Two dishes, identical at t = 0 — one dilute, one crowded (your α). After t★ seconds (printed): how many times larger is the mean-squared displacement in the dilute dish? Use your own window fits from both scenarios — nothing else is needed. PDF: derive the ratio symbolically and show why its time-dependence survives even though the crowded prefactor’s units are strange.

Problem 3 — One address in the whole nucleus [15 %]

A thought experiment: a transcription factor that diffuses exactly like your conjugate, in a nucleus of radius R (printed), hunting a single 10-bp site. To the searcher the site is a tiny absorbing target — touch it and the search ends. Model it as a sphere of effective target radius a_site: the length of 10 base pairs of DNA, a documented number you find and cite. (a) the mean search time, with your bleaching D; (b) that search time divided by the simple wall-to-wall crossing time. PDF: show that everything you measured cancels out of (b) — and say what that ratio physically is.

Problem 4 — Delivery or chemistry? [10 %]

Your molecule rains onto an absorbing cell of radius a in a bath at c★ (both printed). (a) if the cell were a perfect absorber, the capture-rate slope in s⁻¹ per nM (the unit conversion is part of the problem — show it); (b) the recorded verdict: with ~1 committed event/s needed downstream, is delivery or chemistry the bottleneck for YOUR numbers? Defend it in the PDF.

Problem 5 — Two instruments, one conjugate [10 %]

(a) your tracking D divided by your bleaching D; (b) the recorded choice: the LEAST plausible contributor to the gap. PDF: one paragraph on why agreement between instruments that share nothing is the strongest evidence a measurement can have.

Challenge — one cell, sealed in a droplet [20 %; graded for 566, attempted by all]

Droplet microfluidics seals a single cell (radius a) inside a droplet of radius R_drop holding one nutrient at C₀; the cell’s surface consumes the nutrient on contact (every molecule that reaches it is gone). Your dataset prints a, R_drop, and the nutrient’s molecular radius r_nut — a far smaller molecule than your conjugate, in the same medium.

How long until 90 % of the nutrient is gone — and was your model even legitimate?

That is the entire prompt — choosing the physics and carrying it to numbers is what makes this the challenge. It is solved entirely by hand and graded from your PDF; the module records nothing for it. In the PDF: set up and solve the governing differential equation — define every symbol, state every assumption — and answer the question; get the depletion time constant two independent ways (one route built on a D you measured, one that never touches your measurements) and reconcile them; and rule on your own well-mixed assumption with a symbolic ratio of timescales.
