0.1 · The receptor, domain by domain — and the tether

"Notably, receptor oligomerization is not driven solely by ECD interactions, but also relies on intracellular domains, particularly the juxtamembrane (JM) and tyrosine kinase domains (TKD). Ligand binding induces conformational changes in these domains that facilitate clustering, a process that requires the TKD to adopt an active-like configuration. In contrast, the JM domain plays a dual role: it contributes to dimer stability and interacts with anionic phospholipids, such as PI(4,5)P2 …" … "mutations within the transmembrane (TM) domain invert formation of the EGF-type into the TGFα-type JM structure even in the presence of EGF, highlighting direct communication between the ECD, TM, and JM domains."

EGFR is one polypeptide of 1,186 residues (mature numbering) that crosses the membrane once. Outside: an ectodomain of 621 residues in four domains — I and III bind the ligand between them, II carries the dimerization arm, IV anchors it. Inside: a juxtamembrane segment (JM-A, JM-B), the tyrosine kinase (N lobe, C lobe, ~270 residues), and a C-terminal tail of ~230 residues carrying the tyrosines the rest of this tab is about. In the resting receptor the arm of domain II is tethered to domain IV and cannot reach a partner; ligand binding between I and III forces a rearrangement that breaks the tether and swings the arm out [Ferguson 2003]. The sim below treats that as a two-state equilibrium — Week 1's Boltzmann (eq. 1.2.7) — coupled to Week 3's binding.

The tether as a Boltzmann factor

Two conformations, tethered (T) and extended (E), differ by a free energy ΔG; ligand binds only E with affinity KE. Write the three populations as Boltzmann weights KT : 1 : C/KE (eq. 1.2.7 for the first ratio, Week 3's mass action for the second). Then

eq. P1.0.1$$f_{exp} = \frac{1 + x}{K_T + 1 + x}, \qquad \theta = \frac{x}{K_T + 1 + x}, \qquad x = \frac{C}{K_E},\ K_T = e^{\Delta G/k_BT} \;\Rightarrow\; K_D^{app} = K_E(1+K_T)$$

Two consequences. The receptor is never completely off: a fraction 1/(1+KT) has its arm out with no ligand, which is why overexpressed receptors dimerize spontaneously (Endres 2013 measured exactly this density dependence) and why Tab 6's tumours signal without ligand. And autoinhibition weakens the apparent affinity by (1+KT): part of the ligand's binding energy is spent breaking the tether. Week 3 measured KD as one number; here it is a composite, and the composition matters because the JM and TM mutations the review describes change KT, not KE.

2. Which domain does a therapeutic antibody such as cetuximab need to block, if it is to prevent activation rather than just binding?

Answer

Domain III (the ligand site) suffices if it also locks the tether — cetuximab binds III and sterically blocks the extended conformation. Blocking the arm on domain II alone would leave ligand binding intact but prevent pairing.

0.2 · Phosphorylation, trans-phosphorylation, dephosphorylation

"Canonical EGFR signaling is initiated at the PM when ligand binding … induces receptor homodimerization and the formation of asymmetric kinase dimers that allosterically activate each other. This process results in EGFR transphosphorylation and the activation of downstream signaling." … "low EGF concentrations induce EGFR dimerization and trans-phosphorylation of C-terminal Tyr residues, triggering canonical CME."

Phosphorylation is the transfer of the γ-phosphate of ATP onto a side chain — here the hydroxyl of a tyrosine — leaving ADP and a phosphotyrosine (pY) that carries two negative charges. A kinase does the writing; a phosphatase (PTP1B, SHP2 and others) hydrolyses the phosphate off. Trans means the kinase of one receptor writes the tail of the other receptor in the pair: the EGFR kinase is autoinhibited when alone, and in the dimer one kinase (the activator) presses its C-lobe against the other's N-lobe like a cyclin against a CDK, switching that receiver on [Zhang 2006]. The receiver then phosphorylates the tail it can reach — the activator's. The JM-B latch of the receiver holds the pair [Jura 2009]. Every one of the "pY" marks in the review is written this way, and every one is erased on its own clock.

What you need to know: writers, erasers, and why a phosphate is a clock

The chemistry, in one line

ATP + Tyr–OH → ADP + Tyr–O–PO32−. The reaction is ~12 kBT downhill, so writing is essentially irreversible and erasure needs its own enzyme: kinases are writers, phosphatases erasers, and SH2/PTB domains (0.3) are readers [Jin & Pawson 2012]. Phosphate was chosen by evolution because the dianion is stable in water yet enzymatically removable in milliseconds [Hunter 2012].

The balance

A site is written while the receptor sits in a dimer as the activator (the fraction of time χ) and erased always:

eq. P1.0.2$$\frac{dp}{dt} = k_{kin}\,\chi\,(1-p) - k_{PTP}\,p \;\Rightarrow\; p_\infty = \frac{a}{1+a},\quad a = \frac{k_{kin}\chi}{k_{PTP}},\qquad \tau_{pY} = \frac{1}{k_{PTP}}$$

Three things follow. (i) pY is a steady state: change χ (dissolve the dimer, add a drug) and the mark relaxes in one phosphatase lifetime — seconds. That is why Tab 1's "flickering dimer" question is a real question, and why the review's H1 ("weak dimers escape phosphatases") is a statement about kPTP. (ii) Each write spends an ATP; a receptor kept phosphorylated is a running cost, not a stored bit. (iii) The trans geometry means a kinase-dead receptor is not a silent receptor — ErbB3 is written by whoever it pairs with, and its tail carries six PI3K sites (0.4).

0.5 · Cooperativity — and two things that only look like it

"high-dose EGF stimulation induces robust EGFR ubiquitination by Cbl, activating EGFR non-clathrin endocytosis (EGFR-NCE)" … [the experiment the review builds on:] EGFR ubiquitination is "threshold controlled … mechanistically determined by the cooperative recruitment of the E3 ligase Cbl, in complex with Grb2".

"Cooperative" is a word with a precise meaning and two common impostors. The precise meaning (Monod, Wyman and Changeux [1965]; Week 3's Hill coefficient) is energetic coupling: the first binding event makes the second more favourable by a factor γ, so the population goes all-or-none and the dose curve steepens. The first impostor is an AND gate: nothing is coupled, but the readout requires several independent events on one molecule, so it rises as xN — Tab 1's pY ∝ θ² and Tab 2's p³. The second is titration: a finite pool of something (a decoy, a limited ligase) must be filled before the response starts, so the curve is flat, then steep. All three give a Hill slope above 1 on eq. 3.2.12. They differ in what they cost and what breaks them, and the review's threshold uses all three at once.

What you need to know: the partition function, and how to tell the three apart

Coupled sites

N sites, ligand activity x = C/K, and a Boltzmann factor γ for every pair of occupied sites (ΔG = −kBT ln γ per pair, eq. 1.2.7):

0.6 · Ubiquitin — written by E1 → E2 → E3, chained, erased by DUBs

"The EGFR can undergo several types of ubiquitin modifications, the most abundant being multiple mono-ubiquitination and K63-linked ubiquitin chains … EGFR has also been reported to be modified, albeit to a minor extent, by K48-linked ubiquitin chains, a canonical signal for proteasomal degradation … the concept of proteasome-mediated degradation of a transmembrane receptor presents a topological challenge." … "Ubiquitin editing by endosomal deubiquitinases (DUBs) further regulates this process: USP8/UBPy and AMSH (K63-selective) remove or trim ubiquitin chains on EGFR and/or ESCRT-0 … Thus, the linkage type and density of EGFR ubiquitin marks, read by ESCRTs and edited by DUBs, constitute a combinatorial code."

Ubiquitin is a 76-residue, 8.6 kDa protein that is attached, whole, to other proteins: its C-terminal glycine (G76) is joined by an isopeptide bond to the ε-amine of a lysine on the target. Three enzymes relay it: E1 activates Ub at the cost of one ATP (→ AMP + PPi) and passes it to an E2 as a thioester; an E3 ligase — for EGFR, the RING-type Cbl — binds both the E2~Ub and the substrate and brings them together [Komander & Rape 2012]. Ubiquitin has seven lysines of its own, so a second Ub can be attached to the first: a chain, named by the lysine used. K48 chains send soluble proteins to the proteasome; K63 chains and multiple mono-Ub are the endocytic marks, and they are EGFR's — on lysines in the kinase domain [Huang 2006]; a single Ub suffices for internalization and degradation [Haglund 2003]. Deubiquitinases (DUBs) cut the isopeptide bond back: USP8 removes chains, AMSH cuts K63 links specifically [Clague 2019]. Like a phosphate, the mark is a balance.

0.7 · Two doors — clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and NCE at a tripartite site

"EGFR is internalized through clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) … and various clathrin-independent endocytosis (CIE) mechanisms. CME operates across all cell types and at all EGF concentrations, whereas CIE mechanisms … are typically activated at higher, yet physiologically relevant, EGF concentrations (≥ 10 ng/mL)." … "EGFR-NCE is characterized by the formation of tripartite contact sites between the PM, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and mitochondria" … "Ca²⁺ released from the ER upon EGF stimulation is rapidly taken up by adjacent mitochondria, which in turn enhances local ATP production."

Clathrin-mediated endocytosis is the cell's general-purpose door: adaptor AP2 recognises cargo motifs and phosphoinositides, clathrin triskelia polymerise into a ~100 nm cage that bends the membrane into a pit, and dynamin pinches it off — of order a minute per pit [Kaksonen & Roux 2018]. EGFR enters it through Grb2, AP2 and its phosphotyrosines. The door has a finite number of pits and adaptors, so it saturates. EGFR non-clathrin endocytosis (NCE) is a second door that opens only for ubiquitinated receptors, read by UIM proteins (Eps15, Eps15R, epsin) [Sigismund 2005], through tubular invaginations at places where the ER is tethered 10–30 nm from the plasma membrane by reticulon-3 [Caldieri 2017]. A mitochondrion parked behind the ER makes the site tripartite: ER Ca²⁺ (via IP3 receptors) is taken up by the mitochondrion (VDAC–MCU), which raises local ATP, and Ca²⁺ plus ATP drive the actin work that pinches the tubule off [Mesa 2024]. Which door a receptor takes decides whether it comes back (Tab 3).

0.8 · ESCRT — reading the mark into the lumen

"recognition of mono-ubiquitin and K63-linked ubiquitin chains on the EGFR is executed by the ESCRT machinery: ESCRT-0 (HRS/STAM) concentrates ubiquitinated EGFR at early endosomes through multiple ubiquitin-interacting motifs; ESCRT-I and ESCRT-II drive cargo selection and bud formation; ESCRT-III catalyzes ILV scission; and, VPS4A/B remodel and recycle ESCRT-III to terminate the reaction. Together, these steps determine lysosomal delivery of EGFR and signal termination."

An endosome is a bag whose inside is topologically outside the cell. To destroy a membrane receptor the cell must move it from the bag's limiting membrane into a small vesicle budded into the bag — an intraluminal vesicle (ILV) — so that when the bag fuses with a lysosome the receptor's kinase and tail are inside the lumen with the proteases. The machine that does this is ESCRT (endosomal sorting complex required for transport), four complexes in sequence [Raiborg & Stenmark 2009] [Henne 2011]: ESCRT-0 (HRS and STAM) grips ubiquitinated cargo with several ubiquitin-binding motifs at once — avidity, so more Ub means a firmer grip [Ren & Hurley 2010]; ESCRT-I/II corral cargo and begin the bud; ESCRT-III polymerises into a spiral that constricts the neck; VPS4, an ATPase, disassembles the spiral and cuts. Until the spiral closes, a DUB (USP8, AMSH — recruited by ESCRT-0 and -III themselves) can strip the Ub and the cargo escapes to recycle. The sim runs that race.

The race

eq. P1.0.8$$P_{ILV} = P_{cap}(n)\cdot\frac{k_{prog}}{k_{prog}+k_{DUB}}\cdot\frac{k_{prog}}{k_{prog}+\tfrac12 k_{DUB}},\qquad P_{cap}(n) = \frac{a_n}{a_n+e_n+r} + \frac{e_n}{a_n+e_n+r}\,P_{cap}(n-1),\quad a_n = k_0[1-(1-q)^n],\ e_n = n\,k_{DUB}$$

Each stage is a competition between moving forward and being undone, and independent competitions multiply. The first factor is a recursion because a DUB cut does not free the cargo at once — it removes one ubiquitin, and the receptor with n − 1 left is still capturable (Pcap(0) = 0). 0.9 · Synthesis — one receptor's biography, and the whole vocabulary at once

The vocabulary, in our words. The review assumes a receptor that starts tethered: its dimerization arm folded against domain IV, exposed a few per cent of the time (0.1). Ligand between domains I and III pays for the tether and locks the arm out; two such receptors — or one and an always-open HER2, or one and a kinase-dead HER3 (0.4) — form an asymmetric kinase dimer in which the activator's C-lobe switches on the receiver, and the receiver trans-phosphorylates the activator's tail (0.2). Each phosphotyrosine is an address (0.3): Grb2 at pY1068/pY1086 brings Sos to Ras and brings Cbl; Cbl reads pY1045 directly; Shc and PLCγ read theirs. Cbl is scarce, and it holds only when it can make several contacts at once — the "cooperative recruitment" the review names, which is a genuine coupling sitting on top of an AND gate and a titration (0.5). Once bound, Cbl is a RING E3: E1 spends ATP to activate ubiquitin, an E2 carries it, Cbl positions it on kinase-domain lysines as multiple mono-Ub or K63 chains (0.6); DUBs erase it continuously, so the mark is a balance that collapses within a minute of the ligase leaving. Phosphorylated receptors enter clathrin pits at any dose; ubiquitinated ones can also enter tubular invaginations at ER–PM contact sites where a mitochondrion supplies Ca²⁺-driven ATP — NCE at a tripartite site — and this door opens only when the mark has switched on, above ~10 ng/mL, while the clathrin door saturates (0.7). On the endosome, ESCRT-0 grips the mark with avidity, ESCRT-I/II bud, ESCRT-III closes the neck and VPS4 cuts; until the spiral closes a DUB can free the cargo to recycle, after it the cargo is in an intraluminal vesicle and, once the endosome meets a lysosome, gone (0.8). Every arrow in that story is a rate, and every mark on it is a steady state.

Everything at once: the sim below is that story as a Markov chain — eleven states, one receptor followed, 240 run — and it asks the one question the vocabulary lets you ask precisely: from the moment ligand arrives, how long does a receptor live, how much of that life is spent signalling, and how many times does it come back? The answer is a mean first-passage time (Week 1) on a graph instead of a line, and it changes with dose and ligand in exactly the ways the seven tabs of the review will explain.

eq. P1.0.9$$\mathbf t = (-Q_T)^{-1}\mathbf 1,\qquad (Q_T)_{ij} = q_{i\to j}\ (i\ne j),\quad (Q_T)_{ii} = -\sum_{j} q_{i\to j}$$

QT is the generator restricted to the ten transient states (everything but the lysosome); ti is the mean time to absorption starting from state i. Replace the vector of ones by an indicator of the signalling states and the same solve gives the expected time spent signalling; solve the transpose for the expected occupancy and you get the expected number of passes through recycling. The simulated population should agree with all three — and does, to within counting noise.

What the review says is happening — and the equation the patch just measured

Bookkeeping first

On a patch with \(R\) receptors per unit area, each receptor is free (\(M\)), liganded but alone (\(LM\)), or half of a dimer (\(D\) counts pairs):

eq. P1.1.1$$R = [M] + [LM] + 2[D]$$

Ligand binding is Week 3's revolving door, eq. 3.2.4, and its steady state is Langmuir, eq. 3.2.8: the liganded fraction is \(\theta = C/(C+K_D)\) whether or not anything downstream happens. Two liganded monomers meet at a rate proportional to the square of their density, and a dimer falls apart at \(k_{undim}\):

eq. P1.1.2$$\frac{d[D]}{dt} = k_{dim}[LM]^2 - k_{undim}[D]$$
eq. P1.1.3$$[D] = K_{dim}[LM]^2, \qquad K_{dim} = \frac{k_{dim}}{k_{undim}}$$

A receptor's phosphorylation level \(p\) is written while it sits in a dimer (the partner kinase does the writing — that is what "transphosphorylation" means) and erased by phosphatases at \(k_{pt}\). Averaged over receptors, with \(f_D\) the fraction in dimers:

eq. P1.1.4$$\frac{d\bar p}{dt} = k_p f_D(1-\bar p) - k_{pt}\bar p \;\Rightarrow\; \bar p_{\infty} = \frac{k_p f_D}{k_p f_D + k_{pt}}$$

The law the sim measured

At low occupancy almost every liganded receptor is alone, so \([LM] \approx R\theta\) and eq. P1.1.3 gives \(2[D] \approx 2K_{dim}R^2\theta^2\). The dimer fraction, and with it the signal, is then

eq. P1.1.5$$f_D = \frac{2[D]}{R} \approx 2K_{dim}R\,\theta^2 \qquad (\theta \ll 1)$$

Two things fall out. The exponent: signal \(\propto \theta^2\), so on a Hill plot (eq. 3.2.12) the pY points run at slope 2 where θ runs at slope 1 — with no allostery, no cooperativity, nothing but the requirement that two things meet. And the prefactor: \(R\) is in it. Double the receptor density and the same θ produces twice the dimer fraction. Two cells reading the same concentration with the same KD can disagree about what it means, by exactly the ratio of their receptor numbers. The review's phrase for the consequence is that certain ligands "can sustain EGFR phosphorylation even at low receptor levels" while others "may require higher receptor densities" — in this model that is the ratio \(k_{dim}R/k_{undim}\), and nothing else.

Why matched duty cycles are the fair test — and what each hypothesis has to assume

A dimer's lifetime is a bond's lifetime: exponentially distributed with mean \(1/k_{undim}\) (eq. 3.1.7, Week 3's survival law, applied to a protein–protein contact instead of a ligand). Its duty cycle is the fraction of time it is paired, \(k_f/(k_f+k_{undim})\). If phosphorylation is a rate while paired and dephosphorylation a rate proportional to what is there, then integrating \(dp/dt = k_p s(t) - k_{pt}p\) over a long window gives

eq. P1.1.6$$\int_0^T p\,dt \;=\; T\cdot\frac{k_f}{k_f+k_{undim}}\cdot\frac{k_p}{k_{pt}}$$

— exact for linear kinetics (a pair has ~ten phosphosites and is far from saturating them), and it contains the duty cycle and not the switching speed. Two pairs with the same duty cycle integrate the same pY, however often they blink. That is the sim's first result, and it is negative: "continuous association–dissociation cycles" do nothing by themselves. The second hypothesis only works if each association delivers something a steady dimer does not — a burst of phosphorylation on formation (the pulse slider). That may well be true; kinase dimers are asymmetric and may phosphorylate fastest as they form. But it is an added assumption, and the review's phrasing hides it.

The first hypothesis — Freed and colleagues' reading [Freed 2017] — needs a different assumption: that a dimer's negative regulators arrive on a clock. A stable dimer lives long enough to be found by its phosphatase or its ubiquitin ligase; a flickering one dissolves before the referee arrives, and every new dimer starts the clock again. That is testable in a way the review points at: slow the referee (raise τneg) and the flicker's advantage should shrink. Slow the switching (lower the ratio) and it should shrink too.

The engine's \(1/n\) is chosen so that a lecture audience can see the effect in thirty seconds. 1.4 · Synthesis — the ligand-dependent receptor, all of it

What the review's first section says, in full. Canonical EGFR signalling starts when a ligand binds the extracellular domain and the receptor pairs: the two kinase domains form an asymmetric dimer in which one activates the other, the tails are transphosphorylated, and adaptors dock on the phosphotyrosines. EGFR also heterodimerizes with ErbB2, ErbB3 and ErbB4, and the partner changes which effectors are recruited. Seven ligands drive this: EGF, TGFα, BTC and HB-EGF bind tightly (KD 0.1–10 nM); AREG, EREG and EPG bind weakly (KD > 100 nM); BTC, HB-EGF and EREG also engage ErbB4. The review's central claim is biased agonism: the ligands elicit different receptor conformations, signalling dynamics and trafficking, and the differences persist at full occupancy — so affinity is not the explanation.

Three mechanisms are offered, none alone sufficient. Dimer geometry and stability: EGF and TGFα produce symmetric extracellular-domain dimers that are stable; EREG and EPG produce asymmetric, transient ones — and, paradoxically, those unstable dimers give longer signalling and differentiation rather than proliferation (Freed 2017). Two explanations are on the table: unstable dimers escape recruitment of phosphatases and ubiquitin ligases, or they cycle through repeated activation events; the review says current assays cannot separate them. Receptor density modulates all of this — EREG and EPG sustain phosphorylation at low receptor levels while EGF-type ligands may need more receptors — and dimerization kinetics differ too (AREG is biphasic, perhaps engaging preformed dimers). Higher-order clustering: high ligand and receptor densities produce oligomers and reduce lateral mobility, strongly for EGF and barely for EREG; the intracellular juxtamembrane (JM) and kinase domains, and PI(4,5)P2 under the JM, are needed for clusters to form — but slowing receptors artificially does not reproduce the signal, so the architecture, not the mobility, matters. JM conformation: EGF and HB-EGF induce a hydrophobic "EGF-type" JM coiled coil, TGFα, AREG, EREG and EPG a polar "TGFα-type" one, and transmembrane mutations can flip one into the other — allostery running from the ectodomain through the membrane. Ronan et al. measured effector recruitment for all seven ligands and found two clusters (BTC/TGFα/EPG and AREG/EREG, EGF nearer the latter) with HB-EGF on its own.

The review's own conclusion is that ligand identity, ligand concentration, receptor number, the ErbB partners present and the membrane's lipid composition must all be integrated to predict the output, and that this needs live-cell imaging and computational modelling together. The paper's mechanism, as five lines of algebra

From dimer to phosphosite

Tab 1 gave the dimer fraction \(f_D\) as a function of dose and receptor number. Each of the three tail tyrosines is written by the partner kinase while the receptor is dimerized and erased by phosphatases, so at steady state a site is phosphorylated with probability

eq. P1.2.1$$p = \frac{a}{1+a}, \qquad a = \frac{k_{kin}}{k_{PTP}}\,f_D$$

(the sim uses the graded limit \(p \propto f_D\)). This is the blue curve, and it is what an anti-phosphotyrosine blot reports: Hill slope ≈ 1.

The AND gate

Sigismund's finding is that Cbl needs pY1045 and a Grb2 docked at pY1068 or pY1086 — on the same receptor. If the three sites are phosphorylated independently, sort receptors by how many Cbl:Grb2 contacts they can offer, with pY1045 required:

eq. P1.2.2$$P_1 = p(1-p)^2, \qquad P_2 = 2p^2(1-p), \qquad P_3 = p^3$$

The cooperativity Capuani modelled is that each extra contact tightens the complex by a factor \(\gamma\):

eq. P1.2.3$$K_c = \frac{K_1}{\gamma^{\,c-1}}, \qquad c = 1,2,3$$

For large \(\gamma\) the three-contact class wins the ligase even though it is the rarest, and its abundance is \(p^3\): a cubic in a graded input. That is the whole threshold.

A limited ligase

Cbl is scarce — about five thousand copies for a hundred thousand receptors. Free Cbl \(C_f\) is what is left after every competent receptor has taken its share (Week 3's Langmuir on each class, eq. 3.2.8, with the ligase as the "ligand"):

eq. P1.2.4$$\mathrm{Cbl}_T = C_f\Big[1 + R\sum_{c=1}^{3}\frac{P_c}{K_c + C_f}\Big]$$

which the sim solves by bisection. Ubiquitination then follows Cbl occupancy, saturating:

eq. P1.2.5$$b = \sum_c \frac{P_c\,C_f}{K_c + C_f}, \qquad \mathrm{Ub} = \frac{k_r b}{1 + k_r b}$$

The reader's law, and why a code needs both a writer and an eraser

A receptor's chain is an integer that random events move up and down — the E3 adds, the DUB removes:

eq. P1.2.6$$n \xrightarrow{\;k_{el}\;} n+1 \ (n < n_{max}), \qquad n \xrightarrow{\;k_{DUB}\,n\;} n-1$$

The DUB rate is per ubiquitin, so long chains are trimmed faster — an eraser that works proportionally to what there is to erase. A reader with \(m\) binding motifs, each engaging an available ubiquitin independently with probability \(q\), holds the cargo when at least \(m\) contacts form. That is a binomial tail:

eq. P1.2.7$$P_{cap}(n) = \sum_{j=m}^{n}\binom{n}{j}q^{\,j}(1-q)^{n-j}$$

and it is zero for \(n < m\) by construction: a receptor with fewer marks than the reader has hands cannot be held, however long it waits. That single fact is the "code." Mono-ubiquitin under a two-handed reader is a recycling ticket; the same receptor with a K63 chain of three is captured with probability 0.28 per attempt and, in a race against a 0.05/s recycling exit, ends up in the lysosome more often than not. The writer (Tab 2.1) set how many receptors get marked; the chain type sets how many marks per receptor; the eraser sets how long the marks last; the reader's \(m\) sets what counts. Four knobs, one binary outcome per receptor — and the review's word for the result, combinatorial, is the honest one.

2.3 · Synthesis — the ubiquitin code from dose to fate

What the review's section says, in full. Ubiquitination is the early mark that follows phosphorylation. It is written mainly by the Cbl family of RING E3 ligases, which bind the receptor directly at pY1045 and indirectly through Grb2 at pY1068 and pY1086; other E3s — ZNRF1, PARK2, NEDD4 — contribute in ways whose interplay is unresolved, and FBXW7 has been proposed to route EGFR to the proteasome, which raises the topological puzzle of how a transmembrane protein would be extracted from the bilayer (ERAD-like machinery is one candidate). CHIP ubiquitinates mutant receptors selectively. The marks themselves are mostly multiple mono-ubiquitination and K63-linked chains, both used at different steps of endocytosis — internalization, endosomal sorting, incorporation into intraluminal vesicles — with K48 chains a minor, stress-associated species.

The code is read by the ESCRT machinery on early endosomes: ESCRT-0 (HRS/STAM) concentrates ubiquitinated cargo through multiple ubiquitin-interacting motifs, ESCRT-I and -II select cargo and bud the membrane, ESCRT-III pinches the vesicle off, and VPS4 recycles ESCRT-III. It is edited by deubiquitinases — USP8/UBPy broadly, AMSH selectively for K63 chains — acting on the receptor and on ESCRT-0 itself, which tilts the balance between recycling and degradation. The review's phrase is that linkage type and density of the marks, read by ESCRTs and edited by DUBs, form a combinatorial code governing compartmentalization, signalling duration and downregulation.

Two experimental anchors carry the section: Sigismund 2013 showed that ubiquitination is a sharp threshold function of EGF dose (off at 1 ng/mL, on at 10) because Cbl:Grb2 is recruited cooperatively to receptors carrying pY1045 together with pY1068 or pY1086, and that this threshold is exactly the dose–response of non-clathrin endocytosis; Capuani 2015 showed by modelling and experiment that both site multiplicity and cooperativity are required, that the threshold is tuned to physiological receptor numbers, and that at cancer-level receptor or ligand the phosphorylation and ubiquitination arms uncouple. Write a rate for every arrow in the diagram. With \(S_f\) free surface receptor, \(S_L\) liganded surface receptor, \(E_r\) the recycling (CME) endosome, \(E_d\) the degradative (NCE) endosome, and \(\phi_{Ub}\) the ubiquitinated fraction from Tab 2:

Everything is first order (\(r_2 = 0.4\) is the NCE endosome's partial recycling); total receptor is conserved except for the lysosome (and slow synthesis). eq. P1.3.2$$f_{NCE} = \frac{k_{NCE}\,\phi_{Ub}}{k_{CME} + k_{NCE}\,\phi_{Ub}}$$

and the signal the cell integrates counts liganded receptor wherever it can still signal:

eq. P1.3.3$$I_{ERK} = \int_0^T \big(w_{PM}S_L + w_{endo}E_r\big)\,dt$$

At low dose \(\phi_{Ub} \approx 0\): everything goes through CME, recycles, and re-binds — the receptor is a loop, and the signal it produces, small as it is, holds steady for the whole hour. The clock, the rate, and a ligand's "potency"

The early endosome's V-ATPase pumps protons; a single-exponential approach to a final pH is the simplest honest clock:

eq. P1.3.4$$\mathrm{pH}(t) = 7.2 - 1.2\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$

eq. P1.3.5$$k_{off}(\mathrm{pH}) = k_{off,7.2}\;10^{\,\beta\,(7.2 - \mathrm{pH})}$$

The gap as a diffusion length

What the review's section says, in full. The plasma membrane and the early, recycling and late endosomes are all signalling platforms, and the quality, amplitude and duration of EGFR output are set by which compartment the receptor is in and for how long. Two internalization routes exist: clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) works at every dose, and clathrin-independent routes — for EGFR the non-clathrin route NCE — switch on at high, still physiological doses (≥ 10 ng/mL) in a context-dependent way. In epithelial cells and keratinocytes, low dose means CME, recycling and sustained signalling from endosomes and membrane; high dose means robust Cbl ubiquitination, NCE, tripartite contacts between membrane, ER and mitochondria, lysosomal degradation and long-term attenuation. Sorting at the early endosome is the ESCRT/DUB race of Tab 2.

Ligand identity decides the sorting. EGF and TGFα bind alike at neutral pH, but EGF stays bound in the acidic endosome and is degraded while TGFα dissociates, is deubiquitinated and recycles — hence TGFα's greater mitogenic potency and its correlation with aggressive breast cancer; the review notes this pH story is largely in-vitro and that dimer geometry may contribute. AREG stays bound but is only transiently ubiquitinated and recycles, driving DNA synthesis like TGFα while uniquely producing an elongated, mesenchymal morphology and enhanced motility; HB-EGF and BTC stay bound and are degraded; EREG and EPG are weakly ubiquitinated and recycle with prolonged but weaker signalling.

The organelle contacts are the section's novelty. At high dose the ER, tethered to the plasma membrane by reticulon 3, forms sites with mitochondria interposed: EGF-triggered Ca²⁺ release through IP3R is taken up by VDAC1/MCU, mitochondrial ATP rises, and that ATP powers both NCE and the actin remodelling for collective migration (Caldieri 2017; Mesa 2024) — so high dose is read as "stop proliferating, move". Later, ER–endosome contacts let the ER phosphatase PTP1B dephosphorylate the receptor (needing Annexin A1, S100A11 and, at the late endosome, LRRK1), TFG at ER exit sites shapes early endosomes, mitochondria contact endosomes through Rab21/MFN2/PTPRJ (dephosphorylation; lost in renal carcinoma) and PPTC7/VPS4A (recycling), and VDAC2 anchors PI3K to promote endosome maturation. 4.1 · The narrowing gap — mechanotransduction with no mechanosensor

A source in a channel: Fick II with a shedding term

Along the gap (coordinate \(z\), from the lumen at 0 to the basal side at \(L\)), ligand is shed from both walls at areal rate \(S\) into a slab of width \(w\) — a volumetric source \(2S/w\) — and diffuses out the ends, which the surrounding fluid holds at zero. Fick's second law (eq. 2.2.1) at steady state, with the source:

eq. P1.4.1$$D\frac{d^2c}{dz^2} + \frac{2S}{w} = 0,\quad c(0)=c(L)=0 \;\Rightarrow\; c(z) = \frac{S\,z(L-z)}{D\,w}$$

The profile is a parabola and its peak, midway along the gap, is

eq. P1.4.2$$c_{max} = \frac{S L^2}{4 D w} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{w}$$

Every factor is geometry or transport: shedding rate, cell height, diffusivity, gap width. Halve the gap and the concentration doubles; compress the epithelium tenfold and the cells lining the gap see ten times the ligand — Tschumperlin's "approximate tenfold increase in soluble HB-EGF," derived rather than fitted. The receptors then read \(c_{max}\) through Langmuir (eq. 3.2.8), and because the unloaded epithelium sits low on the isotherm (θ ≈ 0.05), a tenfold concentration is nearly a tenfold occupancy: the response is in the sensitive window, which is not an accident of the model but a fact about resting tissue.

What kind of claim this is. Nothing in eqs. P1.4.1–P1.4.2 senses force. The "mechanotransduction" is the mechanics of a volume: compression is transduced into concentration by a constant source and a shrinking sink. The control experiment is in the chips — inhibit the shedding protease (ADAM17) and the narrow gap has nothing to concentrate; Tschumperlin's group did exactly that. The review's broader point is that this mechanism is one of several by which tissue geometry sets EGFR signalling, alongside polarity (basolateral receptors, luminal ligand) and receptor density (4.2).

4.2 · How far a ligand travels — affinity sets the range

"Within the crypt base, Paneth cells secrete Wnt3, EGF, and NRG1 to sustain the niche for LGR5-positive stem cells." … "Interestingly, low-affinity ligands such as EREG and AREG generated broader ERK propagation compared with high-affinity ligands, which are rapidly sequestered by EGFR. Week 2 gave you a ligand's range as \(\lambda = \sqrt{D\tau}\), with τ a lifetime set by clearance. In an epithelium the clearance is the receptors themselves: a ligand that binds and is internalized while bound is gone. That single fact makes affinity a range: high-affinity EGF is eaten by the first cells it meets; low-affinity EREG passes through many. And receptor density is in the same formula, under the square root.

Uptake as a product, and range as a square root

A receptor consumes a ligand only if it internalizes it while bound. Binding happens at \(k_{on}\) per receptor; once bound, the ligand is internalized at \(k_{int}\) or released at \(k_{off}\), so the probability of consumption per binding is \(k_{int}/(k_{int}+k_{off})\). With \(\Gamma_R\) receptors per unit membrane and \(\sigma\) membrane area per unit tissue volume, the volumetric uptake rate and the resulting range are

eq. P1.4.3$$k_{up} = k_{on}\,\Gamma_R\,\sigma\;\frac{k_{int}}{k_{int}+k_{off}}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{D}{k_{up}}}$$

— eq. 2.3.7 with \(\tau = 1/k_{up}\). Two consequences, both in the review. First, affinity: \(k_{off}\) for EGF (~0.02 /s) is comparable to \(k_{int}\), so a large fraction of bindings end in consumption and \(\lambda\) is a cell or two; \(k_{off}\) for EREG (~4 /s) is a thousand times larger, so almost every binding ends in release, \(k_{up}\) collapses, and \(\lambda\) is tens of cells. Low affinity is not weakness; it is reach. Second, density: \(\Gamma_R\) sits under the square root, so tenfold more receptor is a range √10 ≈ 3 times shorter — a receiver cell that overexpresses EGFR sequesters its own paracrine supply, which is what Lin and colleagues observed.

The Damköhler row is a decoy in the Week-3 sense: it asks whether the field is delivery-limited (Da ≫ 1) or uptake-limited (Da ≪ 1). At ΓR = 20 /µm² and D = 50 µm²/s, Da ≈ 0.07 — uptake-limited, which is what licenses treating the cells as a smooth first-order sink at all. Push ΓR to 1000 /µm² and Da approaches 1; the cells then deplete their own boundary layer and the honest model is Week 3's relay, not this one.

4.4 · Synthesis — ligand availability in a tissue, all of it

What the review's section says, in full. In vivo the ligand concentration a receptor sees is set by tissue architecture: cell density, matrix, gradients, diffusion barriers and local trapping produce signalling that homogeneous culture never shows. The ligands are expressed tissue by tissue — TGFα widely in epithelia (autocrine and paracrine, perhaps juxtacrine); EGF in salivary and Brunner's glands and the distal kidney tubule; AREG in placenta and mammary gland and in activated T cells, Treg and TH2, where it drives tissue repair; EREG and BTC in epithelia and mesenchyme; EPG in nerve and heart; HB-EGF abundantly in epithelia and placenta and as the one ligand with demonstrated juxtacrine signalling. Unlike hormones, their levels are local: low in plasma, 20–200 ng/mL in milk, urine and prostatic fluid, rising with injury or inflammation. In wound healing platelets release stored EGF at once and keratinocytes and fibroblasts amplify with HB-EGF and AREG.

Release is by ectodomain shedding: ADAM10 cleaves EGF and BTC, ADAM17 cleaves TGFα, HB-EGF, AREG and EREG, and ADAM17 is itself regulated by mechanical stress — which confines signalling to the wound edge. Dying epithelial cells shed AREG through ADAM17 to activate EGFR on their neighbours, coupling cell loss to compensatory proliferation. Tissue geometry then organizes the ligands: along the nephron HB-EGF rises in proximal tubules after ischaemia (regeneration, and fibrosis if sustained), TGFα and AREG rise with injury, EGF is high in the thick ascending limb and distal tubule, and in the collecting duct EGFR activation attenuates ENaC sodium reabsorption and vasopressin-stimulated water permeability. In the intestinal crypt, Paneth cells secrete EGF, Wnt3 and NRG1 to sustain LGR5⁺ stem cells whose EGFR is uniformly distributed, while differentiated enterocytes hold EGFR basolaterally, away from luminal ligand until the barrier breaks — the basis of oral EGF's benefit in a neonatal necrotizing-enterocolitis model.

Finally the physics of the space itself: Tschumperlin's compression narrows the lateral intercellular space and concentrates shed ligand tenfold without any force sensor; junctions restrict paracellular diffusion and confine signalling; ADAM17-cleavable ligands launch ERK activation waves across polarized sheets, low-affinity ligands propagate them farther because high-affinity ones are sequestered, receptor overexpression in the receivers shortens the range, and losing α-catenin or claudins impairs the sustained wave — exogenous ligand starts a wave but cannot sustain it. Bell's law, and the bond that breaks the rule

A bond is a well the bath kicks you out of (Week 3 1.3): its off-rate is \(k_0 e^{-\Delta U/k_BT}\). Pull on it with a force \(F\) and the work done over the distance \(x_\beta\) between the bound state and the barrier top is subtracted from the barrier:

— eq. 3.1.6 with mechanical work in the exponent. At 37 °C one \(k_BT\) is 4.28 pN·nm, so with \(x_\beta = 0.6\) nm a force of 5 pN adds 0.7 \(k_BT\) and doubles the off-rate; 30 pN raises it 67-fold. A cage that opens at \(k_{off}(F)\) and closes at \(k_{cage}\) leaves a fraction of receptors free:

eq. P1.5.2$$f_{free} = \frac{k_{off}(F)}{k_{off}(F) + k_{cage}}$$

and the freed receptors bind ligand at Langmuir's θ (eq. 3.2.8). That is the review's sentence, quantified: tension opens the cage, and the epithelium signals. Under Bell's law the free fraction rises monotonically with force.

The E-cadherin extracellular domain does not obey Bell's law. Manibog and colleagues held single cadherin X-dimers under force and found a catch bond [Manibog 2014]: up to roughly 30 pN, pulling lengthens the bond's lifetime — tension bends the domains into extra hydrogen bonds that lock the pair together — and only beyond that does the Bell term win. Under the measured bond the free-EGFR curve dips before it rises. A gently loaded epithelium sequesters more receptor than a resting one; a hard pull releases it. The review's statement is right in sign for large forces and inverted for small ones, and the difference is not a footnote — resting epithelial tension is in the tens of pN per bond, right where the catch lives.

Why this is the lesson. "Mechanical stress releases EGFR" is a sentence without a number. Bell's law gives it one, and the measured bond gives it a different one. Whenever the review says a structure is mechanosensitive, ask: over what force range, and in which direction?

5.2 · Stiff matrix, more receptor — where the stiffness acts

"substrate stiffness significantly influences EGFR–ligand interactions: cells cultured on soft matrices show markedly reduced binding of fluorescently labeled EGF at both cell–cell and cell–media interfaces, compared to those on stiff matrices or glass. … On soft matrices, different EGF doses yield similarly restricted ERK signaling, whereas stiff matrices enable a broader dynamic range of responses, suggesting that the mechanical environment also modulates the sensitivity and amplitude of cellular responses to growth factor stimulation."

Cells on a soft gel respond less to EGF than cells on glass. The tempting model runs through the cytoskeleton: stiff matrix → more traction → integrin signalling → a more excitable ERK pathway. The deficit is at the receptor: soft substrates lower surface EGFR and lower EGF binding. So the honest model has no clutch and no traction in it. Boltzmann with tension in the exponent, and a door that needs no ligand

eq. P1.5.5$$P_o(\sigma) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\big[-(\sigma - \sigma_{1/2})\,\Delta A / k_BT\big]}$$

Units make the sensor's steepness visible: 1 mN/m is 1 pN/nm, so at σ½ = 1.4 mN/m and ΔA = 20 nm² the gating energy is 28 pN·nm ≈ 6.5 \(k_BT\) — a change of 0.3 mN/m moves the channel from mostly shut to mostly open. 5.4 · Synthesis — ligand-independent EGFR, all of it

What the review's section says, in full. EGFR has functions that need neither ligand nor kinase: knockout mice die around birth while mice with a kinase-crippled receptor survive with mild skin and eye defects, and mice lacking any single ligand are nearly normal. Cellular stresses — UV, oxidants, hyperosmolarity, chemotherapy — drive a ligand- and kinase-independent internalization through p38-MAPK phosphorylation of serines and threonines on the tail, through clathrin and AP2 (Ser1006 sits in a dileucine motif; its constitutive phosphorylation in lung cancer lowers surface receptor and resists targeted drugs). Stress-internalized receptor is sequestered in a distinct, reversible MVB population and returns to the surface when p38 is inhibited; at low ligand (≤ 1 ng/mL), the few activated dimers phosphorylate the surrounding unliganded monomers through p38 and internalize them alongside, prolonging signalling from endosomes; TNF-α combined with EGF does the same. Ligand-free EGFR also initiates autophagy from late endosomes (LAPTM4B/Sec5/Rubicon releasing Beclin-1) and acts as a Rheb GEF at lysosomes.

The mechanical inputs are the section's centre. At junctions, E-cadherin holds EGFR in 2:1 heterotrimers that cannot engage ligand; tension dissociates them and releases monomers that dimerize and signal. Integrin engagement with fibronectin, laminin or collagen phosphorylates EGFR through Src-family kinases without ligand, promotes adhesion-dependent cell-cycle re-entry, and cooperates with ligand signalling; EGFR in turn activates NHE1, acidifies the pericellular space, desialylates integrins and drives their galectin-3 GL-Lect endocytosis and polarized recycling. EGFR is part of the rigidity sensor itself — needed on rigid but not soft substrates (Sheetz) — and substrate stiffness changes how much EGF a cell binds and where; Farahani showed the stiffness effect is at the receptor, not the pathway, with soft matrices compressing the response range. Piezo1, opened by pressure, stretch, shear or Yoda1, activates EGFR through Src and p38 serine phosphorylation, internalizes it through clathrin, and drives nuclear ERK, AP-1 and YAP — a regenerative and malignant program distinct from EGF's.

"EGFR ligands can regulate one another's expression, creating self-reinforcing autocrine and paracrine loops. The loop, its two states, and three arrows

Let \(L\) be the ligand concentration a cell sits in and \(A\) its ERK activity between 0 and 1. The cell receives an external supply \(S_b\), sheds more when active, and the bath clears; ERK is driven by receptor occupancy (Langmuir, eq. 3.2.8) and decays:

eq. P1.6.1$$\frac{dL}{dt} = S_b + S_{max}\frac{A^2}{K_A^2 + A^2} - k_{cl}L, \qquad \frac{dA}{dt} = k_a\,g(\theta)(1-A) - k_dA, \quad \theta = \frac{L}{L+K_D},\ g = \frac{\theta^3}{\theta_{1/2}^3 + \theta^3}$$

The occupancy θ is Langmuir (eq. 3.2.8); \(g\) is the MAPK cascade's ultrasensitivity — three kinases in series turn a graded occupancy into a steep ERK response, which is why \(g\) is a Hill function and not θ itself. (With \(g = \theta\) the loop amplifies but never switches; the sim's honesty line says so.) Set both derivatives to zero and you have two curves in the \((L, A)\) plane; their intersections are the steady states. With a steep nonlinearity on each side they can cross once or three times. When they cross three times, the middle crossing is unstable and the outer two are the cell's memory:

eq. P1.6.2$$\text{bistable}\iff A_\infty(\text{from } A{=}0)\;\ne\;A_\infty(\text{from } A{=}1)\quad\text{at the same } S_b$$

The sim finds the two by integrating from rest and from full activity; the right-hand chart sweeps \(S_b\) upward from rest and downward from activity. Where the branches separate, the supply that turns the loop ON is larger than the supply that turns it OFF, and the gap between them — the hysteresis width — is the amount of ligand withdrawal a switched-on cell survives. A tumour whose shedding capacity is ten times an epithelium's has a loop that, once triggered by a growth factor, no longer needs one.

Each therapy is a map on the parameters of eq. P1.6.1:

eq. P1.6.3$$\text{TKI: } k_a \to (1-f)k_a \qquad \text{mAb: } L \to (1-f)L \text{ in } \theta \qquad \text{ADAM-i: } S_b, S_{max} \to (1-f)S$$

and each moves the OFF branch differently. The TKI lowers how much ERK a given occupancy produces, so more ligand is needed per unit of activity and the loop's own shedding may fall short. The antibody hides ligand from the receptor — the same effect, one step upstream. The shedding inhibitor attacks the loop's source, which is why the review's interest in "targeting EGFR ligands themselves" is not a detour: in a bistable loop the feedback arrow is the one that matters, and a receptor-directed drug is fighting the cell's own supply.

Why a kinase inhibitor can raise the amount of receptor

Follow the arrows backward. Degradation (Tab 3) needs the NCE door; the NCE door needs ubiquitin (Tab 3, eq. The review names the problem — TKIs that degrade the receptor do so "only at toxic doses" — and the ledger shows it as a bar above 1.

The antibody trap is the same one, upstream: hide the ligand, lose the phosphotyrosines, lose the degradation. A degrader is different in kind. First-order removal of receptor needs no phosphorylation, so it does not depend on the signal it is meant to stop, and it removes the scaffold along with the kinase. The review's table of emerging approaches lists "targeted protein degraders against EGFR" under every cancer type it covers; the ledger is the argument for why.

It cannot put a number on kinase-independent survival — the Rheb-GEF at the lysosome, the release of Beclin-1, the stabilization of SGLT1 — because the review names these functions and no paper has given them a rate. The badge marks where the model's knowledge ends. Treat every review claim about "kinase-independent functions" as exactly that kind of statement: real, important, and not yet quantitative.

6.4 · Synthesis — EGFR in cancer, all of it

What the review's section says, in full. EGFR is dysregulated in cancer by amplification, point mutations, deletions and overexpression, reinforced by autocrine and paracrine ligand loops. Lung adenocarcinoma carries kinase-domain mutations (exon 19 deletions, L858R) sensitive to first-, second- and third-generation TKIs, with resistance through T790M and C797S, exon 20 insertions, MET/HER2 bypass, EMT and histologic transformation; glioblastoma has amplification and the ligand-independent EGFRvIII deletion, with no approved EGFR therapy and the blood–brain barrier as the obstacle; triple-negative breast cancer overexpresses wild-type receptor and resists through HER2/3, MET, IGF1R, AXL, PI3K/AKT/mTOR bypass and ligand-independent signalling via BAP31 or BRK; colorectal cancer responds to cetuximab and panitumumab only with wild-type RAS. Emerging approaches across all four: fourth-generation TKIs, bispecifics, antibody–drug conjugates, CAR-T against EGFRvIII, and targeted protein degraders.

Beyond the genetics, four non-genetic mechanisms. Ligands: TGFα, AREG and HB-EGF are raised across carcinomas and predict worse prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma, while high AREG/EREG predicts response to anti-EGFR antibodies in RAS-wild-type colorectal cancer; tumour-associated macrophages and stroma supply ligand; ligands induce one another (EGF → AREG), creating positive feedback — yet some tumours grow on very little ligand and, in glioblastoma, low ligand promotes invasion in EGFR-amplified cells. Trafficking: shifting the CME/NCE balance changes drug response — neratinib degrades receptor only at toxic doses, gefitinib plus CME inhibitors slows tumours, and available endocytic inhibitors are too broad; RAB5A overexpression drives endosomal ERK, tissue unjamming, active wetting of spheroids, nuclear-envelope rupture and cGAS–STING. Mechanotransduction: interstitial shear in ovarian cancer raises EGFR by blocking degradation; stiff matrices activate EGFR through YAP/TAZ in triple-negative breast cancer and sustain residual EGFR-mutant lung cells (rescued by YAP inhibitors); agrin–integrin–YAP and ILK–YAP loops resist TKIs; α6β4 integrin and calpain-2 drive EGF-independent migration. Nuclear and metabolic EGFR: nuclear EGFR co-regulates proliferation, angiogenesis and DNA-repair genes and bypasses gefitinib and erlotinib; EGFR drives glycolysis through MYC and PI3K/AKT, stabilizes SGLT1 as a kinase-independent scaffold so that depletion, not inhibition, kills under nutrient stress, phosphorylates TXNIP to free GLUT1, and supports xCT, fatty-acid synthase and glutamine transport — the metabolic ground of TKI resistance.

7.1 · Every tab was an average

"Historically, early computational studies of EGFR signaling were primarily deterministic, formulated as systems of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) … These deterministic approaches provided clear and interpretable mathematical descriptions of signaling dynamics … However, these models inherently capture only average population behaviors, neglecting the stochastic fluctuations and single-cell variability that are now known to shape signaling outcomes. … stochastic and 'rule-based' models were introduced to capture the probabilistic nature of receptor interactions, dimerization, and endocytic events … these simulations reproduced cell-to-cell variability, noise propagation, and rare signaling events not captured by deterministic approaches."

The step, the spread, and the assumption that expires

A deterministic rate \(a_j\) for reaction \(j\) becomes, over a short step \(h\), a random count of firings:

eq. P1.7.1$$n_j \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(a_j h), \qquad a_j = k_j \times(\text{molecules available})$$

Every arrow in eq. P1.3.1 becomes one such draw; the boxes hold integers. Because independent Poisson events have variance equal to their mean, a compartment that holds on average \(\mu = fN\) receptors fluctuates with standard deviation of order \(\sqrt{\mu}\), and its relative spread across cells is

eq. P1.7.2$$\mathrm{CV} = \frac{\sigma}{\mu} \approx \sqrt{\frac{1-f}{fN}} \;\sim\; \frac{1}{\sqrt N}$$

— the same \(1/\sqrt N\) that set the counting limit on a receptor's reading in Week 3 (eq. 3.5.1), now setting the limit on how well a cell's fate is determined by its dose. At a hundred receptors the surface pool differs between identical cells by tens of percent; some have crossed the "attenuated" line and some have not. Every equation in Tabs 1–6 described the mean of this histogram and none of its width. That is the assumption with the expiration date, and the review's own words for it — ODEs "inherently capture only average population behaviors" — are why its final section turns to stochastic, hybrid and data-driven models.

Two things this tab does not say. First, it does not say the averages were useless: at ten thousand receptors the ODE is nearly exact, and every ranking in this module survives. Second, it does not say the spread it measures is the spread in a real dish — that is dominated by extrinsic variability (receptor number, ligase level, cell-cycle phase), which the review's single-cell data capture and this sim deliberately leaves out. What it says is narrower and safer: even with every cell identical, dose does not fix fate, and the size of the gap is \(1/\sqrt N\).

About 400 receptors for 5 % agreement between identical cells; 100 receptors gives 10 %. The mean is a good model of abundant things and a poor model of scarce ones — and signalling is full of scarce things.

7.2 · Synthesis — decoding across space and time, all of it

What the review's section says, in full. The field is being remade by measurement and by models. Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer biosensors report ligand-specific signalling in real time compartment by compartment; combined with super-resolution and lattice light-sheet microscopy they show receptor clustering, endosomal signalling and trafficking at nanometre scale — single-particle tracking of unliganded EGFR found tetraspanin nanodomains gating ligand binding and early activation. The Egfr-Emerald mouse lets endogenous receptor be watched in living tissue. Spatially resolved proteomics, phosphoproteomics and metabolomics map intratumoral heterogeneity of RTK activity and the compartment-specific phospho-changes that let cells stay active or resist kinase inhibition; with mechanotransduction data these converge on a view of EGFR across space, time and conformation.

Modelling followed the same arc. The 1990s and 2000s ODE models (Starbuck, Kholodenko, Schoeberl, Wiley, Resat) linked EGFR phosphorylation kinetics to MAPK and AKT and explained integration and adaptation; Capuani's ODE model predicted how ubiquitination patterns encode trafficking and found weak nodes cancer could exploit; operational models (Karl et al., after Black and Leff) quantified ligand bias as coupling efficiency. But ODEs capture only averages. Stochastic and rule-based models (Blinov, Hsieh, Wang) reproduced cell-to-cell variability, noise propagation and rare events; hybrid schemes couple deterministic bulk species to stochastic receptor states. Machine learning now infers parameters, reduces models, denoises and segments images, tracks cells, and is beginning to integrate spatial transcriptomics, phosphoproteomics and metabolomics — not yet applied to EGFR specifically, but, the review says, only a matter of time.
