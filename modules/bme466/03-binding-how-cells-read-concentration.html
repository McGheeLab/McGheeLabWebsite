A cell has no eyes. It cannot see the bacterium forty microns away, taste the hormone in the blood, or feel the drug you swallowed an hour ago. Everything a cell will ever know about the world outside its membrane arrives the same way: a molecule wanders in, touches the surface, and sticks. For two weeks we followed those molecules — how they move (Week 1) and how often they arrive (Week 2) — and every calculation ended at the moment of touch. Touching is not reading. Sticking is. Before deriving any of that, watch a cell try to use it.

0.1 · A cell in a gradient

A crowd of cells is dropped into a pocket where an attractant is a hundred times more concentrated at the right wall than at the left. Each cell crawls, and like every crawling cell it forgets its heading in a couple of minutes — left alone, it random-walks. Blue cells listen: every ten seconds they compare how full the receptors on their four sides are and turn part of the way toward the fuller side — by a fraction \(g\) of the angle. Grey cells are deaf. Nothing here is derived yet; the whole module is the derivation. Carry two questions out of it: how much listening is enough — and how much is too much?

1.1 · Arrival: how often does a diffusing protein reach a surface?

Go back to Week 1's box — but not to the gas. A protein in water does not fly. Between two collisions with water it moves a hundredth of a nanometre and forgets which way it was going; ten trillion times a second its path is re-randomised, and what is left is the random walk of Week 1, with its diffusion coefficient \(D\). So the first question of binding — how often does a molecule reach the surface? — cannot be answered the way kinetic theory answers it for a gas, by counting molecules striking a wall. A protein sitting beside a surface touches it endlessly, but it is the same protein. The question that matters to a cell is how often a fresh one is delivered, and that is a transport question, not a speed question.

Below, \(N\) proteins random-walk in a box. The left wall is the cell surface, and for this section it is perfectly sticky: every protein that reaches it is captured and counted, and a replacement is released at the far wall so the supply is held. Two dials — how many proteins (the concentration) and how fast they diffuse (their size, through Week 1's Stokes–Einstein). Watch two things: the arrival counter, and the concentration profile across the box. The surface empties its own neighbourhood.

  1. Read the arrivals per second at the defaults against the prediction 2ND/L². Then watch the profile on the right settle into a straight line — zero at the surface, highest at the supply. That slope is the depletion layer the surface has dug for itself.
  2. Double N: arrivals double. Concentration enters every rate this week through this one fact.
  3. Double D (a smaller protein): arrivals double too. Not because the molecule moves faster between collisions — it forgets its direction just as often — but because it wanders twice as far in a second. In a gas this dial would read √T; in water it reads D.

Name it. The surface takes what reaches it, and what reaches it is set by Fick's first law (Week 1, eq. 1.3.5) across the layer the surface has emptied. If the concentration falls from its bulk value \(C\) to zero at the surface over a distance \(\delta\), the arrival flux — fresh molecules per area per second — is

eq. 3.1.1 $$J_{\text{arr}} = \frac{D\,C}{\delta} \qquad\text{(the sim: } \delta = L,\ C = 2N/L^2 \text{ at the supply, so arrivals/s} = 2ND/L^2\text{)}$$

Read \(D/\delta\) as an effective speed: how fast a random walker covers the last \(\delta\). For a peptide (\(D \approx 500\ \mathrm{\mu m^2/s}\)) crossing a micron that is 500 µm/s — a quarter of a million times slower than its ballistic 120 m/s, because almost all of that speed is spent going back and forth. And unlike a kinetic hit rate, eq. 3.1.1 has geometry in it: \(\delta\) is set by the surface and its surroundings — a film, a distance to the source, or, for a whole cell, its own radius, which is how Week 2's eq. 2.3.5 turned the same law into \(4\pi D a\,C\).

Every rate this week is proportional to \(C\) — and, in water, to \(D\). Whatever a surface does with the molecules that reach it, it can act only on the ones delivered; delivery is diffusion; and diffusion carries \(C\) and \(D\) and nothing about how fast the molecule moves between collisions. \(k_{on}\) is often glossed as "the rate at which molecules hit the surface" — read hit as reach, and then ask which arrivals count. That is §1.2.

1.2 · Which arrivals count: the site, and the stick

A cell is not a sticky wall. Most of its surface is inert lipid; the parts that can bind a given ligand are receptors — patches of area \(\pi a_s^2\), \(\Gamma_{max}\) of them per unit area, together a fraction \(f = \Gamma_{max}\pi a_s^2\) of the surface. And touching a site is not binding: the molecule must arrive in the right orientation, with the right groups facing the pocket, so a contact with a site binds with some probability that chemistry sets and physics has to accept — call the sim's dial \(p_c\), the odds per touch. Below, the same random walkers (a little slower, so the sites take longer to fill) meet a wall that is no longer perfectly sticky. It has sites; a walker that touches an open site sticks with probability \(p_c\); everywhere else, and on a taken site, it bounces and keeps walking. A bound molecule stays bound — a one-way site — until you turn the last dial, which gives every bound molecule a probability per second of letting go, \(k_{off}\).

Watch the wall-contact counter as you change \(p_c\). A walker that is refused does not leave: it is still there, and it touches again, and again. In a gas a refused molecule flies off and is replaced by a fresh one; in water a refused molecule gets many more tries. So the price of a small \(p_c\) is not what a gas would pay — and the probability that matters, the odds that an arriving molecule ends up bound, is not \(p_c\) at all. The sim reports it as the fraction of the diffusive ceiling the wall binds.

  1. At pc = 0.1, read the counters: wall contacts per second, bindings per second, and the two dashed lines — the diffusive ceiling (every arrival binds) and the prediction for this wall. Bindings sit well below the ceiling; contacts run far above it.
  2. Switch to pc = 1: bindings rise almost to the ceiling — the wall is nearly as good as perfectly sticky although only a third of it is sites. Then the sites fill and bindings die while the contacts keep coming. The surface has gone deaf; raise c and it fills sooner, not fuller.
  3. Switch to pc = 0.01: contacts climb — the refused molecules touching over and over — while bindings fall to a trickle. But not a hundredfold below pc = 1: the odds per touch are a dial, delivery is a ceiling, and the odds per arrival — P — are what comes out.
  4. Back at pc = 0.1, turn up the unbinding probability. The sites no longer fill: bound molecules leave, the occupancy settles where bindings balance departures, and bindings per second hold steady instead of dying. The surface has stopped going deaf. What sets that probability is §1.3.

Name what you watched, defining each symbol as it appears. Call \(C\) the concentration far from the surface — the supply — and \(C_s\) the concentration in the liquid touching the surface. Let \(\Gamma_{max}\) be the sites per unit area and \(\theta\) the fraction of them taken, so \(\Gamma_{max}(1-\theta)\) are open. An open site consumes the ligand touching it at an intrinsic rate \(k_r\): the volume of liquid it clears per second (m³/s), so that \(k_r C_s\) is one open site's bindings per second. The bindings per unit area per second are then

eq. 3.1.2 $$J_{\text{bind}} = k_r\,\Gamma_{max}(1-\theta)\,C_s \;\equiv\; \kappa\,C_s, \qquad \kappa \equiv k_r\,\Gamma_{max}(1-\theta)$$

\(\kappa\) has the units of a speed (m/s): how fast the surface as a whole drinks the liquid touching it. Every dial of the sim lives in it — more sites, stickier sites, more of them open, larger \(\kappa\) — and its value is printed in the stats. \(k_r\) is the chemistry. It is not a count of touches: for a random walker the number of touches depends on how finely you look — the sim's wall-contact counter would change if its time step did, and a true Brownian path touches a wall infinitely often (566 box). What is well defined is how fast an open site consumes ligand that is right there, and that is \(k_r\).

But \(C_s\) is not \(C\). The surface drinks from the liquid touching it, and diffusion refills that liquid at the rate §1.1 found — eq. 3.1.1 with the surface no longer empty, \(J = D(C - C_s)/\delta\). In steady state the two fluxes are one flux: what diffusion delivers is what the surface takes. Set them equal and solve for the concentration at the surface:

eq. 3.1.3 $$\frac{D\,(C - C_s)}{\delta} = \kappa\,C_s \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; C_s = \frac{C}{1 + \kappa\delta/D}, \qquad J_{\text{bind}} = \frac{C}{\delta/D + 1/\kappa}$$

Read the last form as two resistances in series: \(\delta/D\) is the resistance of the delivery, \(1/\kappa\) the resistance of the chemistry, and the flux is the concentration divided by their sum. A fast surface (\(\kappa \gg D/\delta\)) empties its neighbourhood — \(C_s \to 0\) and \(J \to DC/\delta\), the ceiling of §1.1; that is the \(p = 1\) chip. A slow surface (\(\kappa \ll D/\delta\)) barely dents the liquid — \(C_s \to C\) and \(J \to \kappa C\), the chemistry alone; that is the \(p = 0.01\) chip. The dotted prediction line is eq. 3.1.3 with the box's own \(\delta\) and the sim's \(\kappa\). And the ratio of the two, \(J_{\text{bind}}/(DC/\delta) = 1/(1 + D/\kappa\delta)\), is the probability that a molecule diffusion delivers ends up bound — the sticking probability per arrival, \(P\). It is the number the stats print as "fraction of the ceiling", and it is what survives when the touch count does not.

Now the constant a cell actually shows. A cell does not know \(C_s\); it is embedded in \(C\). So define the association rate constant \(k_{on}\) as one open site's binding rate per unit of the bulk concentration, and write the event as a reaction — a free site \(s\) and a free protein \(p\) become a bound pair \(sp\), one-way for now (§1.6 fixes this notation for the rest of the course). From eq. 3.1.2 one open site binds at \(k_r C_s\), so \(k_{on} = k_r C_s/C\): the intrinsic rate, discounted by how much the surface has emptied its own neighbourhood. For a flat wall eq. 3.1.3 gives the discount at once, \(k_{on} = k_r/(1 + \kappa\delta/D)\). For the case that matters most — a single receptor pocket of radius \(a_s\), fed from all sides — the delivery is not a film but Week 2's spherical one, now written with the surface concentration it feeds into:

eq. 1.5.1 again — Smoluchowski, as the delivery to a pocket whose surface concentration is \(C_s\) $$\text{arrivals per second at one pocket} = 4\pi D a_s\,(C - C_s)$$

  2. Solve for the surface concentration.
    \(C_s = \dfrac{4\pi D a_s}{k_r + 4\pi D a_s}\,C\)
    — the pocket's version of eq. 3.1.3: the faster the chemistry relative to the delivery, the emptier the pocket's surroundings.
  3. Write the binding rate against the bulk concentration.
    \(\text{bindings per second at one open site} = k_r C_s = \dfrac{k_r\cdot 4\pi D a_s}{k_r + 4\pi D a_s}\,C \;\equiv\; k_{on}\,C\)
  4. Name the two factors. \(4\pi D a_s\) is the encounter rate constant \(k_s\) of eq. 1.5.1 — arrivals per second per unit concentration — and what multiplies it is the fraction of arrivals that stick:
    \(k_{on} = k_s\cdot\dfrac{k_r}{k_r + k_s} \;=\; P\,k_s, \qquad P \equiv \dfrac{k_r}{k_r + k_s}\)
    — arrive, then stick. \(P\) is the sticking probability per arrival; it is what the sim's "fraction of the ceiling" measures.
  5. Check units and limits. \(k_r\) and \(4\pi D a_s\) are both volumes per second, so \(k_{on}\) is too; and
    \(\dfrac{1}{k_{on}} = \dfrac{1}{k_r} + \dfrac{1}{k_s}\)
    — the same two resistances in series as eq. 3.1.3, one pocket at a time.
eq. 3.1.4 $$s + p \;\xrightarrow{\;k_{on}\;}\; sp, \qquad \frac{d\Gamma_{sp}}{dt} = k_{on}\,C\,\Gamma_s, \qquad k_{on} = \frac{k_r\,k_s}{k_r + k_s} = P\,k_s, \quad k_s = 4\pi D a_s,\ \ P = \frac{k_r}{k_r + k_s}$$

That is what an association rate constant is made of, and why its units are volume per time: a rate at which one site clears ligand out of the volume around it, with the delivery already discounted. When the chemistry is fast (\(k_r \gg 4\pi D a_s\)), \(P \to 1\) and \(k_{on} \to k_s\): the pocket binds everything delivered and is diffusion-limited — the \(p_c = 1\) chip on the ceiling. When it is slow, \(P \to k_r/k_s\) and \(k_{on} \to k_r\) and the pocket is reaction-limited — the \(p_c = 0.01\) chip. Collins and Kimball (1949) first wrote this series law; Tab 3 scales the same ratio — chemistry over delivery — from one pocket up to a whole surface and gives it a name.

Two resistances, in series. Delivery — \(D\), geometry, \(C\) — and consumption — sites and sticking. A real receptor's \(k_{on}\) sits at or below \(4\pi D a_s\), usually far below, as the worked example shows; and every arriving molecule gets many touches before it wanders off, which is why a modest \(p\) per touch can still bind most arrivals. Which resistance is larger, and by how much, is exactly the question Tab 3 turns into a dimensionless number.

The last dial previewed what comes next. Give bound molecules a probability per second of letting go, \(k_{off}\), and the surface stops going deaf: bindings and departures balance at an occupancy where \(k_{off}\,\theta\,N_{\text{sites}}\) equals the binding rate, and the surface holds a steady reading. Tab 2 derives that balance (Langmuir's isotherm) and Tab 3 folds delivery into it. What sets \(k_{off}\) is the next two sections: a bound molecule is a molecule in a well, kicked by water.

Deeper (566): what the sim predicts, and why "touches" is not a physical count

The sim's walkers take Gaussian steps of variance \(2D\,h\) every time step \(h\); a "wall contact" is a step that would cross \(x = 0\), and the walker is absorbed with probability \(p_{abs} = f\,p_c\,(1-\theta)\) or reflected. Erban and Chapman (2007) showed that this rule reproduces the continuum partially absorbing (radiation) boundary \(D\,\partial C/\partial x = \kappa\,C_s\) with \(\kappa = p_{abs}\sqrt{D/\pi h}\). Solve the steady state with a supply at \(x = L\): the surface concentration is \(c_s = J/\kappa\), the profile is linear, and the bindings per second are \(\dfrac{2ND/L^2}{1 + 2D/\kappa L}\) — the ceiling of §1.1 divided by one plus the ratio of delivery to consumption. That is the dashed prediction. Notice \(\kappa\) depends on \(p_{abs}\) and on \(h\): halve the time step and contacts double while \(p_c\) must halve to describe the same surface. The count of "chances" is an artefact of the clock; \(\kappa\) — or per site, \(k_r = \kappa/\Gamma_{max}\) — is the physics. Collins and Kimball's 1949 result for a sphere is the same series law, \(k_{on} = 4\pi D a_s\cdot k_r/(4\pi D a_s + k_r)\), which is eq. 3.1.4; Tab 3's Damköhler number is the same ratio, \(k_r\Gamma_{max}/k_m\), scaled up from one pocket to a whole surface.

Arrivals vs bindings: how picky a real receptor is
  1. Write what you know. The §1.1 pocket: \(s = 1\) nm, \(D = 500\ \mathrm{\mu m^2/s}\), 1 nM peptide. Tab 4's neutrophil receptor binds that peptide with \(k_{on} = 0.028\ \mathrm{\mu m^3/s}\) (Sklar 1984's \(10^{9}\ \mathrm{M^{-1}min^{-1}}\), converted).
    \(4\pi D a_s = 4\pi(500)(10^{-3}) = 6.3\ \mathrm{\mu m^3/s}, \qquad C = 0.602\ \mathrm{\mu m^{-3}}\)
  2. Arrivals — the diffusive ceiling, from §1.1:
    \(4\pi D a_s\,C = 6.3\times0.602 = 3.8\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  3. Bindings — eq. 3.1.4 with the measured \(k_{on}\):
    \(k_{on}C = 0.028\times0.602 = 0.017\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) — one binding per minute.
  4. How picky.
    \(\dfrac{3.8}{0.017} \approx 220\) — one arrival in about two hundred binds.
    Invert eq. 3.1.4 for the intrinsic rate: \(k_r = \dfrac{k_{on}\cdot4\pi D a_s}{4\pi D a_s - k_{on}} = \dfrac{0.028\times6.3}{6.27} \approx 0.028\ \mathrm{\mu m^3/s}\) — \(k_r \approx k_{on}\). Delivery is not the bottleneck; the pocket is reaction-limited, sitting on the \(k_r\) side of eq. 3.1.4, more than two hundred times below its ceiling.
Diffusion could supply this pocket with four molecules a second; it takes one a minute. Encounters are cheap and sticking is the expensive part — and that is true of most receptors, which is why, in Tab 3, most cells turn out to be reaction-limited. Its sticking probability per arrival is \(P = k_{on}/k_s = 0.028/6.3 = 0.0044\) — the same one-in-220. The sim's \(p_c = 0.01\) chip is the cartoon of this receptor; the real one is pickier still.

1.3 · Bombarded: what a bound molecule feels

A ligand that has bound is not at rest. It sits in its pocket inside the same bath that delivered it, and that bath never stops: water molecules at several hundred metres per second strike it some \(10^{13}\) times every second, from every side. Most kicks are ordinary — a thermal energy or so, the \(k_BT\) of Week 1 — and the bond shrugs them off. But the energies of those kicks are spread out, and the spread has a tail. Once in a while a kick arrives carrying more energy than the bond can hold, and the ligand is gone.

Below: one bound ligand (purple) in a pocket on the wall, and the water (blue), slowed by a factor of \(10^{11}\) so you can see individual hits. Each hit's energy is logged on the right against the bond depth \(\Delta U\). The question the sim answers is not whether the ligand leaves — it always does — but how long it lasts, and how that depends on \(\Delta U\) and on temperature.

  1. Watch the hit-energy histogram fill. Most kicks are under 1 kBT; the tail is exponential (Week 1's Boltzmann distribution, eq. 1.2.7, applied to kick energies). The shaded part beyond ΔU is the fraction of kicks that can break the bond.
  2. Raise ΔU from 3 to 6 kBT. The hit rate does not change; the shaded fraction drops twenty-fold, and so does the unbinding rate. Read the measured residence time against the prediction.
  3. Raise the temperature. Every kick is bigger relative to the same ΔU, the shaded tail grows, and the bond lets go sooner. Heat does not push the ligand out — it makes the rare kick less rare.

The reaction of §1.2 gets its reverse arrow. A bound pair \(sp\) comes apart at a rate \(k_{off}\), and the rest of this tab is about what sets it:

eq. 3.1.5 $$s + p \;\underset{k_{off}}{\overset{k_{on}}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; sp$$

The sim measured two things. First, the kick energies are Boltzmann-distributed — the same law that tilted Week 1's sedimentation column, now applied to the energy a colliding molecule carries into the bond:

eq. 1.2.7 again — Boltzmann, for the energy of a kick $$P(E_{\text{kick}} > \Delta U) = e^{-\Delta U/k_BT}$$

Second, the ligand leaves when a kick clears \(\Delta U\). So the unbinding rate is simply how often it is hit, times the fraction of hits that are strong enough:

eq. 3.1.6 $$k_{off} = k_0\,e^{-\Delta U/k_BT} \qquad (k_0 = \text{the attempt rate — here, kicks per second on the bound ligand})$$

Read it twice. The exponential is the whole story of why residence times span twelve orders of magnitude in biology while bond energies span barely one: each extra \(k_BT\) of depth cuts \(k_{off}\) by a factor of \(e\), each extra hydrogen bond (\(\approx 3\,k_BT\)) by twenty. And temperature sits in the denominator of the exponent — a warmer bath does not hit harder on average by much, but its tail reaches further, and the tail is all that matters.

A bond is not broken by force; it is outwaited. Nothing pushes the ligand out. It leaves when the bath, by chance, hands it enough energy at once — and chance, at a fixed rate per second, is exactly what "memoryless" means. The survival law §1.5 measures, \(e^{-k_{off}t}\), is already implied here.
Ten trillion kicks a second — so why does a bond last a minute?
  1. Write what you know. Water at 37 °C: \(\bar v \approx 600\) m/s, number density \(c_w = 3.3\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\); a ligand of radius \(s = 1\) nm presents an area \(\approx 4\pi a_s^2\) to the bath; the bond is \(\Delta U = 21\,k_BT\) (§1.5's worked value for a 0.6 nM affinity).
  2. Kicks per second — kinetic theory's impingement rate, \(\tfrac14 c\bar v\) per area, over the ligand's area. (Legitimate here, and only here: a water molecule's free flight between collisions is about its own size, so each flight is one kick. The proteins of §1.1 do not fly — their arrival is the sum of \(10^{13}\) such kicks a second — but the kicks themselves do.)
    \(k_0 = \tfrac14 c_w\bar v\cdot 4\pi a_s^2 = \tfrac14(3.3\times10^{28})(600)(1.3\times10^{-17}) \approx 6\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  3. Apply eq. 3.1.6 naively.
    \(k_{off} = k_0\,e^{-21} = 6\times10^{13}\times7.6\times10^{-10} \approx 5\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
    — a residence of 20 microseconds. The real receptor holds its ligand for about a minute: \(k_{off} \approx 0.01\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The estimate is off by six orders of magnitude.
  4. Locate the error. Not in the exponential — that is Boltzmann, and it is exact. In \(k_0\): a single kick with \(E > \Delta U\) does not free a ligand buried in a pocket. It has to travel out against the well, through a viscous bath that takes back the energy in the next collision. Most strong kicks are undone before the ligand has moved a fraction of a nanometre. The effective attempt rate is the rate at which the ligand as a whole is carried to the rim — the "rattle rate" \(k_0\) of §1.5, about \(10^{7}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), not \(10^{13}\).
The kick picture gets the exponential exactly right and the prefactor wrong by a million. That is the reason §1.5 stops counting kicks and watches the ligand's position in the well instead: Kramers's problem, in which the bath both kicks and drags. Same \(e^{-\Delta U/k_BT}\); an honest \(k_0\).

1.4 · What sets the depth: shape and charge

So far \(\Delta U\) has been a slider. What is it, physically? A bound ligand is held by a great many weak contacts between two surfaces, and a contact exists only where the two surfaces actually touch — within a van der Waals distance, three or four ångströms. Immunologists have names for the two surfaces: the patch of the target that is bound is the epitope, and the antibody's surface that binds it is the paratope (Jerne coined both words in 1960). For a receptor and its ligand the same two surfaces are simply the ligand and the binding site. The depth of the well is a ledger of what those two surfaces do to each other, and two things dominate the ledger.

Shape. Where a bump on one surface fills a dip on the other, the atoms come close enough to touch, water is squeezed out from between them, and the pair gains roughly half a \(k_BT\) per small patch of buried area. Where the shapes do not match, the surfaces are held apart and no contact forms — worse, a bump that lands on a bump lifts the whole ligand and spoils every other contact at once. Charge. Amino-acid side chains carry charges and polar groups; a positive residue facing a negative one (a salt bridge, or a hydrogen bond) adds one to three \(k_BT\), and a positive facing a positive subtracts as much. Below, a ligand of eight patches docks in a binding site of eight dips. You control how many of its bumps fit the dips and how many of its three charges face their opposites; the sim adds up the ledger, builds the well that results — its actual shape, along the direction the ligand can slide — and measures how long the ligand stays in it. The water is drawn as well, at something like its real density — a water molecule is about half a patch across and the liquid is packed, so the ligand is never not being hit — and slowed so the hits are visible, because the jostling is the point. Notice that the ligand's exposed side is not flat either: real proteins are lumpy, and the water uses the lumps. A molecule striking the flank of a lobe shoves the ligand sideways along its site; one that gets under an overhang pries it upward. Every such hit rattles the ligand in its well, and only a run of them, piled up against a depth the fit decided, knocks it off.

  1. Start from the perfect fit. Every dip is filled and every charge faces its opposite: eight contacts and three pairs, ΔU ≈ 7.6 kBT. The landscape on the right is a single deep well; the stays are long. Watch the water work at the ligand's lumpy top — shoving its lobes sideways, prying under its overhangs — thousands of hits per stay, none of them alone enough.
  2. Keep the shape and flip the charges. The contacts are unchanged, but three like charges now face each other — the ligand will not sit in the aligned pose at all. It slides to a shifted pose, holds on by a couple of kBT on its high points, and is gone within a few time units.
  3. Restore the charges and spoil the shape. Same three pairs, five patches that no longer touch: the well loses 3 kBT and the stay shortens tenfold. Then walk the shape slider from 8 to 0 one patch at a time and watch the deposits climb — roughly a factor e per kBT, with scatter, because each fit is also a different shape of well.

The sim measured a ledger. Count the patches that touch, count the charge pairs that face an opposite, subtract the pairs that face a like charge, and the depth of the well is, to a useful approximation, a sum:

eq. 3.1.7 $$\Delta U \;\approx\; N_c\,\varepsilon_c \;+\; \big(N_{+-} - N_{++}\big)\,\varepsilon_q$$

Here \(N_c\) is the number of patches in van der Waals contact and \(\varepsilon_c\) the energy each is worth — about \(0.5\,k_BT\) net per patch of \(\sim 30\) Å² (the raw hydrophobic gain is nearer \(1.3\,k_BT\), some 25 cal/mol per Å² of buried area, of which the debits in the 566 box below take back about half); \(N_{+-}\) and \(N_{++}\) count the charge pairs facing an opposite and a like charge, and \(\varepsilon_q \approx 1\text{–}3\,k_BT\) is the worth of one such pair. The sim uses \(\varepsilon_c = 0.5\) and \(\varepsilon_q = 1.2\), so its perfect fit is \(8(0.5) + 3(1.2) = 7.6\,k_BT\). Two rules hide in the sum. A contact only counts where the surfaces touch — so the shape term is not "how many bumps the ligand has" but how many land in a dip, and one bump in the wrong place can lift the ligand off every other contact. And the charge term has a sign — a mismatched charge is not a missing contribution, it is a penalty, which is why the ligand with the right shape and the wrong charges refused the aligned pose and slid to a worse-fitting one where the charges no longer faced each other.

Now feed the ledger to eq. 3.1.6. Every entry in the sum becomes a factor in the residence: each patch that fits multiplies the stay by \(e^{0.5} \approx 1.6\), each salt bridge by \(e^{1.2} \approx 3.3\), each like-charge clash divides by the same. Eight patches and three pairs against two patches and one pair is a well 6 \(k_BT\) deeper and a stay \(e^{6} \approx 400\) times longer — with the same atoms, differently arranged. That is what the two surfaces are for: not to hold on hard, but to hold on selectively, so that the right partner stays for a minute and a stranger is gone in a microsecond.

Alignment is the whole game. The energy of a bond is not a property of the ligand or of the receptor; it is a property of how well the two surfaces match, contact by contact and charge by charge. Shift the ligand by one patch and its own atoms become a stranger's. The well is deep because the fit is good, and the stay is long because the well is deep — the sim's deposits climb by roughly a factor \(e\) per \(k_BT\), with scatter, because a different fit is also a different shape of well, and the shape sets the attempt rate. That prefactor is §1.5's business.
Note from Dr. McGhee. The picture is old and has kept getting sharper. Emil Fischer, in 1894, said an enzyme and its substrate must fit "like lock and key" to explain why enzymes discriminate between sugars that differ by a single flipped hydroxyl. Daniel Koshland, in 1958, added that the lock can close around the key — induced fit — which is why the "static profiles" in the sim are a cartoon of surfaces that in fact flex a little as they meet. Niels Jerne coined epitope and paratope in 1960 while speculating about how the immune system could possibly hold a matching surface for every foreign molecule it might meet. And the crystal structures, once they came, said the lock and key were even tighter than Fischer imagined: the atoms buried at an interface are packed as densely as the inside of a protein (Lo Conte, Chothia & Janin 1999) — there is no room in a good fit for a wrong bump.
Deeper (566): the debit side of the ledger — water, motion, and the charges that steer

Eq. 3.1.7 is a net ledger, and the exponent in eq. 3.1.6 takes a free energy, not a raw contact energy. Three debits sit against the credits. First, the hydrophobic gain is not an attraction between the surfaces at all: it is the entropy of the water released when two nonpolar patches meet, which is why it scales with buried area (Chothia 1974) and why Chothia and Janin (1975) concluded that hydrophobicity is the main stabiliser of protein–protein association while complementarity decides who may associate. Second, a charged or polar group that binds must first give up the water it was hydrogen-bonded to, and the new pair only pays if it is worth more than the old ones — Fersht and colleagues (1985) measured that leaving a hydrogen-bond partner unpaired costs 0.5–1.5 kcal/mol if it is uncharged and roughly 3 kcal/mol more if it is charged, so a charged group with no partner is the most expensive mistake a surface can make. Third, a bound ligand has lost most of its freedom to tumble and rattle — an entropy cost of several \(k_BT\) that every bond pays before the first contact counts. Finally, charge works on both dials of this tab: long-range electrostatics steer a ligand into the correct pose before it touches, raising the sticking probability of §1.2. Schreiber and Fersht (1996) found the barnase–barstar association rate rises from a basal \(10^5\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}\) to over \(5\times10^{9}\) — the diffusive ceiling — by electrostatic attraction alone.

An antibody's ledger — and what one wrong residue costs
  1. Write what you know. A "standard-size" protein–protein interface buries about \(1600 \pm 400\) Å² in total, roughly 800 Å² on each side (Lo Conte, Chothia & Janin 1999, 75 complexes including 19 antibody–antigen pairs). Take a patch as 30 Å² at a net \(\varepsilon_c = 0.5\,k_BT\), and suppose three charged or polar pairs face their opposites at \(\varepsilon_q = 2\,k_BT\). Body temperature, \(k_BT = 0.616\) kcal/mol. The attempt rate is §1.5's \(k_0 \approx 10^7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\).
  2. The shape term.
    \(N_c \approx \dfrac{800\ \text{Å}^2}{30\ \text{Å}^2} \approx 27, \qquad N_c\,\varepsilon_c \approx 27\times0.5 = 13\,k_BT\)
  3. The charge term and the total — eq. 3.1.7:
    \(\Delta U \approx 13 + 3\times 2 = 19\,k_BT \approx 12\ \mathrm{kcal/mol}\)
    As an affinity (the well-depth step of §1.5's worked example, run backwards): \(K_D = c^\circ e^{-19} \approx 6\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm M = 6\) nM — squarely in the range of a good antibody.
  4. The residence — eq. 3.1.6 with \(k_0 = 10^7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\):
    \(\langle t_{\text{bound}}\rangle = \dfrac{e^{19}}{10^{7}} \approx \dfrac{1.8\times10^{8}}{10^{7}} \approx 18\ \mathrm s\)
  5. Now mutate one epitope residue so that a charged partner on the paratope is left unpaired. Fersht's measurement: 0.5–1.5 kcal/mol for the lost hydrogen bond plus \(\approx 3\) kcal/mol for the stranded charge, call it 4 kcal/mol:
    \(\Delta(\Delta U) \approx \dfrac{4}{0.616} \approx 6.5\,k_BT, \qquad \dfrac{k_{off}'}{k_{off}} = e^{6.5} \approx 700\)
    The stay drops from 18 s to about 25 ms, and \(K_D\) rises from 6 nM to about 4 µM.
Twenty-seven patches and three pairs make a well about 19 \(k_BT\) deep — a nanomolar antibody with an eighteen-second grip. Strand a single charged partner and the grip is seven hundred times shorter. That is why one amino-acid change in a viral surface protein can defeat an antibody that took weeks to make, and why the immune system keeps making new ones.

1.5 · A bond is a well the bath kicks you out of

Real biological bonds are not one-way. Almost every receptor–ligand pair, every antibody, every transcription factor on its site, lets go — and the reason is the same bath that delivered the molecule in the first place. A bond is an energy well: the ligand sits in a dip of depth \(\Delta U\) below the free state. The bath, meanwhile, keeps kicking it with energy of order \(k_BT\) (Week 1, Tab 1). Most kicks rattle it around the bottom. Occasionally a run of kicks piles up enough energy to carry it over the rim, and the bond is gone.

§1.4 built the well out of contacts and charges. Now forget the ledger and keep only its total, \(\Delta U\). Below is exactly that: one tracer (or a thousand) in a well, driven by the coarse-grained bath of Week 1 — the same \(D\) and \(\mu\) that Einstein tied together in eq. 1.3.9. No chemistry is in this box. Watch what the well produces on its own: a residence time that is random, memoryless, and exquisitely sensitive to how deep the well is.

  1. Run one particle at a shallow well (2–3 kBT). It escapes, is put back, escapes again. No two stays are alike.
  2. Switch to a thousand wells. The survival curve of the cohort is a straight line on the log axis — exponential — and the histogram of stays has spread ≈ mean, the signature of a memoryless process.
  3. Step the depth from 2 to 8 kBT and let each depth log a point. The mean stay climbs as eΔU/kBT: a straight line of slope ≈ 1 on the right-hand chart. Two extra kBT buys a stay 7× longer.

Now the names. The sim's first measurement — a memoryless stay — is the promise §1.3 made, kept: in every short interval \(dt\) a bound molecule leaves with the same probability \(k_{off}\,dt\), regardless of how long it has already been there. The fraction of a cohort still bound after time \(t\), and the average stay, follow at once:

eq. 3.1.8 $$P(\text{still bound at } t) = e^{-k_{off}t}, \qquad \langle t_{\text{bound}}\rangle = \frac{1}{k_{off}}$$

The sim's second measurement is the depth law. The chance of finding the tracer up near the rim, where one more kick frees it, is Boltzmann's — Week 1's eq. 1.2.7, the same tilted cloud that set the sedimentation column:

eq. 1.2.7 again — the odds of sitting a height \(\Delta U\) above the bottom $$\frac{P(\text{rim})}{P(\text{bottom})} = e^{-\Delta U/k_BT}$$

Multiply that by how often the tracer attempts the rim — a rattle frequency \(k_0\) set by the well's width and the bath's friction, not by the raw count of water collisions — and you have §1.3's law again, with the honest prefactor the kick-counting picture could not give:

eq. 3.1.6 again — the kick law, from the continuous picture: \(k_0\) is now the rattle rate, not the raw kick count $$k_{off} = k_0\,e^{-\Delta U/k_BT}$$

This is why residence times in biology span twelve orders of magnitude — from the microsecond stays of a substrate on an enzyme to the effectively permanent bonds of a covalent link — while bond energies span only a factor of ten or so. The exponent does the spreading. It is also why "irreversible" is a statement about your clock, not the bond's: the one-way one-way sites of §1.2 are simply wells deep enough that nobody leaves during the experiment.

Two dials, one bath. \(k_{on}\) is about arriving — it scales with \(D\), with the size of the target, and with the concentration outside. \(k_{off}\) is about leaving — it depends on the well and the temperature and on nothing outside the cell at all. The concentration a cell is trying to read enters only through the first dial. Everything in Tab 2 follows from keeping those two facts separate.
Deeper (566): what \(k_0\) really is — Kramers, and why force changes it

The sim's rattle frequency is a cartoon. Kramers (1940) solved this exact problem — an overdamped particle escaping a well under thermal noise — and found \(k_{off} = \dfrac{\mu\sqrt{U''_{\text{well}}\,|U''_{\text{barrier}}|}}{2\pi}\,e^{-\Delta U/k_BT}\): the prefactor is set by the curvatures of the well and the barrier and by the mobility, so the same bath that sets \(D\) sets the attempt rate. The exponential is untouched. Two consequences the course will use later: pulling on a bond with force \(F\) lowers the barrier by roughly \(F\cdot x^\ddagger\) (the distance to the rim), so \(k_{off}(F) = k_{off}(0)\,e^{F x^\ddagger/k_BT}\) — Bell's law, the reason a cell's grip on its matrix is a race between binding and force (Week 7). And the sim's deposits will sit slightly below a pure exponential at shallow depths, where the well is only a couple of \(k_BT\) deep and "rattle, then escape" is not a clean separation — Kramers assumes \(\Delta U \gg k_BT\).

From a measured affinity to a bond energy — and back to a residence time
  1. Write what you know. Tab 4's neutrophil receptor binds its peptide with an equilibrium constant that, as biochemists quote it, is \(K_D = 1/K_{eq} \approx 0.6\) nM, and its bound ligand has a half-life of a minute or so. Body temperature.
    \(K_D = 0.6\ \mathrm{nM} = 6\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{M}, \qquad t_{1/2} \approx 60\ \mathrm s, \qquad k_BT = 4.28\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J\)
  2. Turn the affinity into an energy. An equilibrium constant is a Boltzmann factor in disguise: the binding free energy relative to a standard concentration \(c^\circ = 1\) M is
    \(\Delta G^\circ = k_BT\ln\!\dfrac{c^\circ}{K_D} = k_BT\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{6\times10^{-10}}\right) = 21.2\,k_BT \approx 9.1\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm J \approx 13\ \mathrm{kcal/mol}\)
    That is the depth of the well measured from the free state — about the energy of a handful of hydrogen bonds and a buried hydrophobic patch, which is what a peptide pocket is.
  3. Read \(k_{off}\) from the residence, not from the depth. Eq. 3.1.8:
    \(k_{off} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}} = \dfrac{0.693}{60\ \mathrm s} \approx 0.012\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad \langle t_{\text{bound}}\rangle = 1/k_{off} \approx 87\ \mathrm s\)
  4. Now ask what eq. 3.1.6 says the attempt rate must be.
    \(k_0 = k_{off}\,e^{\Delta U/k_BT} = 0.012\times e^{21.2} \approx 1.9\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
    — a rattle every 50 ns. A ligand exploring a nanometre pocket with \(D \approx 500\ \mathrm{\mu m^2/s}\) crosses it in \(\ell^2/2D \approx 1\) ns, so a prefactor of tens of nanoseconds is the right neighbourhood for a barrier that is mostly the well itself. The orders of magnitude agree; the exact number is Kramers's business, not ours.
A 0.6 nM affinity is a well about 21 \(k_BT\) deep, and a one-minute residence means the ligand tries the rim some \(10^7\) times a second and succeeds once. Deepen the well by a single \(k_BT\) — one more hydrogen bond — and the stay lengthens by \(e \approx 2.7\)×. That sensitivity is the whole reason evolution can tune receptors so finely.

1.6 · One setup for every model

Tab 1 has now bought the three rates every binding model is built from, and this section fixes the way they are written down so that nothing in the rest of the course has to change its mind. Name the players once. A free site is \(s\); a free protein — the ligand — is \(p\); a bound pair is \(sp\); if a site converts what it holds, as an enzyme does, the product is \(q\). Two dials, no memory

Week 2 ended on a single receptor blinking — bound, free, bound — and asked you to notice that raising the concentration shortened the gaps without touching the stays. You now own both halves of that sentence in the symbols above. The gaps are governed by \(k_{on}C\): the site waits for a delivery, and deliveries arrive at a rate proportional to \(C\) (eq. 3.1.4). The stays are governed by \(k_{off}\): a bond survives its bath for a memoryless time of mean \(1/k_{off}\) (eq. 3.1.8), and the bath does not know what the concentration outside is.

Both processes are memoryless. A receptor that has been free for a long time is not "due" a ligand; a bond that has lasted a minute is not "tired." This is not a modelling convenience — it is what a thermal bath does, and it is the same statistics that gave Week 1 its random walk and Week 2 its Poisson arrivals. It also means a single receptor is a terrible instrument: its telegraph of stays and gaps is pure noise, one letter at a time. What it can do is be one of many.

Check yourself (cold)

(i) A receptor's ligand has \(k_{off} = 0.01\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). You watch one bound complex for 100 s. What is the probability it is still bound? (ii) You double the ligand concentration. What happens to the mean stay? To the mean gap? (iii) Two receptors bind the same ligand; receptor B's well is 3 \(k_BT\) deeper. By what factor does its ligand stay longer?

Answer

(i) \(e^{-k_{off}t} = e^{-1} = 0.37\). Not zero — a third of one-minute bonds outlast the minute; that is what "mean" means for an exponential.

(ii) The stay does not change at all — \(k_{off}\) has no \(C\) in it. The gap halves: arrivals come at \(k_{on}C\).

(iii) \(e^{3} \approx 20\)×, from eq. 3.1.6, if the attempt rates are alike. Three thermal-energy units is one good hydrogen bond, and it buys a twenty-fold longer residence.

  • A diffusing protein reaches a surface at the rate diffusion refills the layer the surface has emptied, \(J = DC/\delta\) (eq. 3.1.1) — proportional to \(C\) and \(D\), not to speed. An arrival binds only if it meets an open site and sticks; the intrinsic site rate \(k_r\) and the diffusive supply \(4\pi D a_s\) (eq. 1.5.1) stand in series to make \(k_{on}\) (eq. 3.1.4). Leaving is the bath's business too: a bound molecule is outwaited by a rare kick, \(k_{off} = k_0 e^{-\Delta U/k_BT}\) (eq. 3.1.6), made honest by the well (§1.5). Keep arriving and leaving in separate columns.
  • A bond is a memoryless residence: survival \(e^{-k_{off}t}\), mean stay \(1/k_{off}\) (eq. 3.1.8). "Irreversible" means deep on your clock.
  • Residence times are exponentially sensitive to depth: one \(k_BT\) is a factor of \(e\).
  • One notation: sites \(s\), proteins \(p\), pairs \(sp\); \(k_s\) arrive, \(P\) stick, \(k_{on} = Pk_s\), \(k_{off}\) leave, \(k_{cat}\) convert. One master equation (eq. 3.1.9); every model in Tabs 2–3 is a setting of its switches.
  • A one-way surface fills and then reports nothing. A surface that lets go can report — that is Tab 2.

2.1 · Same surface, three rules

Tab 1 built two dials and one door. Now put two hundred doors on a surface, hold the concentration outside steady, and ask the only question a cell can ask: what fraction of my doors is occupied? The answer depends entirely on the rule a landing molecule obeys, and there are three worth knowing. One-way doors: it binds and never leaves (Tab 1.2). Revolving doors: it binds, stays a memoryless while, and leaves at \(k_{off}\) (Tab 1.5). Stacking: it may land on a molecule that is already bound, and molecules in the upper layers leave at the rate a liquid of the same stuff would evaporate — a pile, not a monolayer.

Run all three before any of them is derived. Two charts: the occupancy \(\theta(t)\) after the concentration is switched on, and — the one that matters — the steady occupancy each rule reaches, deposited against the concentration that produced it. That second chart is a surface's reading of the world. One of the three rules gives a reading with no information in it. One gives a reading that runs away. One is how your cells work.

  1. One-way doors at 1 nM, then at 30 nM. Both climb to θ = 1; the second just gets there sooner. Look at the deposited points: a flat line at 1. This surface reads nothing.
  2. Revolving doors: sweep c from 0.3 to 300 nM, letting each settle. The deposits trace a curve that is linear at low c, bends, and saturates. Find the c where θ = ½ and compare it with koff/kon.
  3. Stacking: sweep c upward. The pile passes θ = 1 and, near the saturation concentration, refuses to stop growing. Then drop the site count to 1 or 24 and watch what a fraction looks like with too few doors to average over.

Before the three rules get their equations, the quantity they share. Of \(\Gamma_s\) free sites and \(\Gamma_{sp}\) occupied ones (per area, or as counts), the fractional occupancy is

eq. 3.2.1 $$\theta = \frac{\Gamma_{sp}}{\Gamma_s + \Gamma_{sp}} = \frac{\Gamma_{sp}}{\Gamma_{max}} \qquad (0 \le \theta \le 1 \text{ for a monolayer})$$

2.2 · One-way doors: a surface that fills and forgets

Tab 1.2's rule, written for a fraction. Molecules arrive at each open site at the rate \(k_{on}C\) (eq. 3.1.4, now per site), a fraction \((1-\theta)\) of sites are open, and nothing ever leaves:

Start from the master equation (eq. 3.1.9) and throw the switches for this rule: a sticking probability \(P\) (so \(k_{on} = Pk_s\)), no release (\(k_{off} = 0\)), no conversion (\(k_{cat} = 0\)), and the concentration at the surface equal to the bulk (\(C_s = C\)):

eq. 3.2.2 $$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = k_{on}\,C\,(1-\theta)$$

Separate and integrate from an empty surface, \(\theta(0)=0\):

eq. 3.2.3 $$\theta(t) = 1 - e^{-k_{on}C\,t}$$

Read it the way the sim showed it. The concentration appears only in the exponent — in the clock. Every one-way surface ends at \(\theta = 1\); a higher \(C\) gets it there faster, and that is all \(C\) can do. If you want to know the concentration from a one-way surface you must catch it mid-fill and time it, which is a legitimate measurement (a chemisorption gauge, a covalent capture assay, an antibody so tight it never lets go during your experiment) but it is not how a cell can live. The cell needs a steady reading that moves when \(C\) moves.

When to use it. Any time the observation is short compared with \(1/k_{off}\): covalent bonds, very high-affinity capture (nM antibodies on a 10-minute assay), irreversible adsorption of proteins onto a bare plastic surface. Test: is \(k_{off}\,t_{\text{obs}} \ll 1\)? Then the doors are one-way on your clock and eq. 3.2.3 is the right model.
The only thing a one-way surface can measure: how long to 90 %
  1. Write what you know. A sensor chip carries capture antibodies that, on the assay's timescale, never release. Per-site \(k_{on} = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{\mu m^3/s}\) (a typical antibody, well below the 6 µm³/s diffusion ceiling of §1.1). Two samples: 1 nM and 10 nM.
    \(k_{on} = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{\mu m^3/s}, \qquad C_1 = 0.602\ \mathrm{\mu m^{-3}}, \qquad C_2 = 6.02\ \mathrm{\mu m^{-3}}\)
  2. Invert eq. 3.2.3 for the time to a target occupancy.
    \(\theta = 1 - e^{-k_{on}Ct} \;\Rightarrow\; t_{90} = \dfrac{-\ln(1-0.9)}{k_{on}C} = \dfrac{2.30}{k_{on}C}\)
  3. Plug in both samples.
    \(t_{90}(1\ \mathrm{nM}) = \dfrac{2.30}{10^{-3}\times0.602} = 3.8\times10^{3}\ \mathrm s \approx 64\ \mathrm{min}, \qquad t_{90}(10\ \mathrm{nM}) \approx 6.4\ \mathrm{min}\)
Both chips end fully covered. The concentration lives in the time — ten times the ligand, one-tenth the wait — so a one-way surface reads concentration only if you watch the clock. Endpoint assays with irreversible capture therefore report "present", not "how much", unless they stop early and time it.

2.3 · Revolving doors: Langmuir

Add the reverse arrow of eq. 3.1.5. Sites still fill at \(k_{on}C\) per open site; now each occupied site also empties at \(k_{off}\), memorylessly (eq. 3.1.8). Impingement times the probability a site is open, minus departures times the probability it is occupied:

The master equation again (eq. 3.1.9), one switch flipped — the bond lets go, \(k_{off} > 0\) — with \(k_{cat} = 0\) and \(C_s = C\) still:

eq. 3.2.4 $$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = k_{on}\,C\,(1-\theta) \;-\; k_{off}\,\theta$$

This is Langmuir's rate equation, and it is the sentence the sim's revolving-door chip was speaking. It is linear in \(\theta\) — the sum of a constant and a term proportional to \(\theta\) — so it relaxes exponentially toward a balance point. Earn the solution yourself before reading it:

Derive eq. 3.2.4 → the occupancy at any time, and where it ends
  1. Collect the \(\theta\) terms. Write \(k = k_{on}C + k_{off}\) for the total rate at which a site changes its mind.
    \(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = k_{on}C - (k_{on}C + k_{off})\,\theta = k_{on}C - k\,\theta\)
  2. Find the fixed point. Set \(d\theta/dt = 0\):
    \(\theta^\ast = \dfrac{k_{on}C}{k_{on}C + k_{off}}\)
  3. Substitute \(\phi = \theta - \theta^\ast\). The constant drops out and what is left is the simplest ODE there is:
    \(\dfrac{d\phi}{dt} = -k\,\phi \;\Rightarrow\; \phi(t) = \phi(0)\,e^{-kt}\)
  4. Restore \(\theta\), with the initial occupancy \(\theta_0\).
    \(\theta(t) = \theta^\ast + (\theta_0 - \theta^\ast)\,e^{-(k_{on}C + k_{off})t}\)
The occupancy relaxes exponentially, at rate \(k_{on}C + k_{off}\), from wherever it started to \(\theta^\ast\) — the same rate the sim measured for its revolving-door chip. Notice the rate is faster at higher \(C\): more arrivals means the balance is struck sooner.
This assumes \(C\) at the surface is constant and equal to the bulk value — i.e. that delivery keeps up with binding. Tab 3 is about when that fails.
eq. 3.2.5 $$\theta(t) = c_1\,e^{-(k_{on}C + k_{off})\,t} + \frac{k_{on}C}{k_{on}C + k_{off}}$$
eq. 3.2.6 $$c_1 = \theta_0 - \frac{k_{on}C}{k_{on}C + k_{off}} \qquad\text{(from } \theta(0) = \theta_0\text{, with } C \text{ held constant)}$$

Let \(t \to \infty\) and the exponential dies. What survives is the steady occupancy, and it is cleaner if the two rate constants are folded into one equilibrium constant:

eq. 3.2.7 $$K_{eq} = \frac{k_{on}}{k_{off}} \qquad\text{(units of volume; } 1/K_{eq} \text{ is a concentration — biochemists call it } K_D\text{)}$$
eq. 3.2.8 $$\theta_{eq} = \frac{K_{eq}\,C}{1 + K_{eq}\,C}$$

This is the Langmuir isotherm — the curve your revolving-door deposits traced, and Week 2's Tab 5 teaser finally named. Read its three regimes off the chart: at \(K_{eq}C \ll 1\) it is a straight line, \(\theta \approx K_{eq}C\) (delivery-limited: every arrival finds a door); at \(K_{eq}C = 1\) — that is, \(C = 1/K_{eq} = K_D\) — exactly half the doors are full; at \(K_{eq}C \gg 1\) it saturates toward 1 (door-limited: arrivals queue for the few open sites). The midpoint is set by the ratio of the two dials. A tighter bond (smaller \(k_{off}\)) or a faster delivery (larger \(k_{on}\)) shifts the whole reading to lower concentrations.

Nobody has infinite time. The transient is mostly over at the half-time — the time for \(\theta\) to close half the gap to \(\theta_{eq}\) — which from eq. 3.2.5 is:

eq. 3.2.9 $$\tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k_{on}C + k_{off}}$$

Two half-times gets you to 75 % of the way, three to 88 %; "the transient is done" usually means three or four of them.

Note from Dr. McGhee. Irving Langmuir, working at GE's research lab, put surface chemistry on a quantitative footing in the 1910s. Building on Freundlich's empirical 1909 isotherm and Gibbs's thermodynamics of surfaces, he proposed the kinetic picture you just derived — molecules adsorbing onto discrete, equivalent sites and desorbing back to the fluid, reaching a dynamic equilibrium — and got both the rate law (eq. 3.2.4) and the saturation isotherm (eq. 3.2.8) out of it. His definitive paper, The adsorption of gases on plane surfaces of glass, mica and platinum, appeared in JACS in 1918; the Nobel Prize in Chemistry followed in 1932. The same saturating form is Michaelis–Menten's (1913) for enzymes — §2.7 makes the connection exact — which is why a biochemist and a surface chemist can read each other's plots without translation.

2.4 · Stacking: BET, or what happens when the doors are not the limit

Langmuir's picture has one assumption the stacking chip broke on purpose: a bound molecule blocks its site. Suppose instead that a molecule can land on top of one already there, and another on top of that — the way a vapour condenses onto a surface it likes. Brunauer, Emmett and Teller (1938) kept Langmuir's kinetics and added one idea per layer: the first layer binds to the surface with its own \(k_{off,1}\); every layer above it is a molecule sitting on molecules of its own kind, so it leaves at the rate the bulk liquid would, \(k_{off,L}\). Two energies, therefore two rates, and their ratio is the whole model: \(c_B = k_{off,L}/k_{off,1} = e^{(E_1 - E_L)/k_BT}\) says how much more the surface is liked than the liquid.

Balancing arrivals and departures layer by layer (each layer is a Langmuir balance whose "surface" is the layer below) gives a geometric series, and the total coverage — now in monolayer equivalents, free to exceed 1 — comes out as:

In the master equation's terms (eq. 3.1.9): Langmuir's balance for the first layer, and one more balance for each layer stacked on a bound pair, \(sp + p \rightleftharpoons spp\), with the liquid's own release rate \(k_{off,L}\). Summing the layers gives

eq. 3.2.10 $$\theta_{tot} = \frac{c_B\,x}{(1-x)\,\bigl(1 + (c_B - 1)\,x\bigr)}, \qquad x = \frac{C}{C_{sat}}, \quad C_{sat} = \frac{k_{off,L}}{k_{on}}$$

\(C_{sat}\) is the concentration at which arrivals onto the pile exactly balance departures from it, \(k_{on}C_{sat} = k_{off,L}\); above it the pile grows without limit — condensation. That is the \((1-x)\) in the denominator, and the runaway your deposits showed.

Derive (566) eq. 3.2.10 from a layer-by-layer Langmuir balance
  1. Name the layers. Let \(s_0, s_1, s_2, \dots\) be the areas covered by exactly \(0,1,2,\dots\) layers, and \(A = \sum s_i\) the total area.
  2. Balance the first layer. Bare area fills at \(k_{on}C\,s_0\); singly covered area is uncovered at \(k_{off,1}s_1\):
    \(k_{on}C\,s_0 = k_{off,1}\,s_1 \;\Rightarrow\; s_1 = c_B\,x\,s_0, \qquad x \equiv k_{on}C/k_{off,L},\ c_B \equiv k_{off,L}/k_{off,1}\)
  3. Balance every layer above it — the same, with the liquid's rate:
    \(k_{on}C\,s_{i-1} = k_{off,L}\,s_i \;\Rightarrow\; s_i = x\,s_{i-1} = c_B\,x^{i}\,s_0 \quad (i \ge 1)\)
  4. Sum the two geometric series (both need \(x<1\)):
    \(A = s_0\Bigl[1 + c_B\sum_{i\ge1}x^i\Bigr] = s_0\Bigl[1 + \dfrac{c_B x}{1-x}\Bigr], \qquad N_{\text{ads}} = s_0\,c_B\sum_{i\ge1} i\,x^i = s_0\,\dfrac{c_B\,x}{(1-x)^2}\)
  5. Divide. Monolayer equivalents are molecules per site, \(\theta_{tot} = N_{\text{ads}}/A\):
    \(\theta_{tot} = \dfrac{c_B x/(1-x)^2}{1 + c_B x/(1-x)} = \dfrac{c_B\,x}{(1-x)(1 + (c_B-1)x)}\)
Eq. 3.2.10. For \(x \ll 1\) the \((1-x)\) factors are 1 and \(\theta_{tot} \approx c_B x/(1 + c_B x)\) — Langmuir's isotherm with \(K_{eq} = c_B/C_{sat} = k_{on}/k_{off,1}\). BET contains Langmuir as its dilute limit; it adds only the pile.
The same \(k_{on}\) is assumed for landing on bare surface and on a pile, and the layers are treated as ideal stacks with no lateral interaction. Both are cartoons; the model's real job is measuring surface area, and there it works remarkably well.
When to use it — and when to refuse. BET was built for gases condensing on powders; it is the standard way to measure a porous material's surface area (nitrogen at 77 K, \(x\) swept from 0.05 to 0.3). In biology it describes non-specific layering: proteins piling onto a bare implant surface, a "corona" forming on a nanoparticle, a coating that thickens with concentration. It is not a model of receptor–ligand binding — a receptor's pocket holds one ligand, and there is no liquid of ligand for a second one to condense into. It sits on this ladder so you recognise it and reject it when someone fits a saturating receptor curve with a runaway equation. Knowing which model does not apply is the skill.
A stack, a number, and the Langmuir hiding inside it
  1. Write what you know. A surface likes the adsorbate 50× more than the adsorbate likes itself (\(c_B = 50\)); the fluid is at half its saturation concentration.
    \(c_B = 50, \qquad x = C/C_{sat} = 0.5\)
  2. Assemble eq. 3.2.10.
    \(\theta_{tot} = \dfrac{c_B x}{(1-x)(1+(c_B-1)x)} = \dfrac{50\times0.5}{0.5\times(1 + 49\times0.5)} = \dfrac{25}{0.5\times25.5} = 1.96\)
  3. Compare with what Langmuir would have said for the same first-layer chemistry, \(K_{eq}C = c_B x = 25\):
    \(\theta_{\text{Langmuir}} = \dfrac{25}{1+25} = 0.96\)
  4. Now go dilute, \(x = 0.01\): BET gives \(\dfrac{0.5}{0.99\times1.49} = 0.339\); Langmuir with \(K_{eq}C = 0.5\) gives \(0.333\).
    \(\text{difference} \approx 2\% \text{ at } x = 0.01; \quad \text{a factor of 2 at } x = 0.5\)
At half-saturation the surface carries two monolayers' worth — the first is 96 % full (Langmuir), and a second one is piling on top. Dilute, the two models agree to 2 %: BET's first layer is Langmuir. If your receptor data ever demanded the extra layer, you would be measuring something sticking to something other than the receptor.

2.5 · Which model reads a concentration? — the isotherm as a ruler

Put the three steady readings on one axis and ask the cell's question of each. A reading is a steady value of \(\theta\) that changes when \(C\) changes — its usefulness is the slope \(d\theta/d\ln C\), how much the needle moves for a given fractional change in concentration. One-way doors: \(\theta_{eq} = 1\) always, slope zero, no reading. Stacking: a reading, but one that only becomes sensitive as \(x \to 1\), where the surface is drowning in adsorbate — and it is the wrong physics for a pocket that holds one ligand. Langmuir: a reading with a window. Slide the concentration and watch where the needle moves and where it is pinned.

  1. Set the concentration to 1/Keq (that is, to KD). The Langmuir slope is at its maximum: ¼ per e-fold of c. This is where a receptor is a good instrument.
  2. Go to 0.03 KD and to 30 KD. Both readings are pinned — near 0 and near 1 — and a factor-of-two change in c barely moves them. The ruler has a range of roughly two decades, centred on KD.
  3. Change KD. The whole window slides. Evolution does not tune a receptor to be "sensitive"; it tunes KD to sit where the concentrations that matter actually are.

A receptor population responds to good news faster than to bad, and the asymmetry is exactly the concentration term in \(k\).

2.6 · When sites talk to each other — Hill

Langmuir's sites are strangers: what happens at one has no effect on the next. Many real receptors are not like that. Haemoglobin's four oxygen sites, dimeric and tetrameric receptors, receptor clusters in a membrane — an occupied site can make its neighbour stickier, by shifting a shared conformation or by simply holding a partner in place. Below is the smallest honest version: a ring of sites where each occupied neighbour deepens a bound molecule's well by \(J\), so its \(k_{off}\) drops by \(e^{-J/k_BT}\) per occupied neighbour. 2.7 · The door that eats — Michaelis–Menten

Every door so far gave the molecule back. An enzyme's does not. It holds a substrate for a while and then, instead of releasing it, changes it — the site opens and a product leaves. That is one more arrow on Langmuir's scheme, and it changes what the surface reports. A receptor reports an occupancy. An enzyme reports a rate — products per second — and a cell reads a good deal of its world through how fast such factories run.

Run it before it is derived. Same two hundred sites, same \(k_{on}\) and \(k_{off}\); the third dial, \(k_{cat}\), is how fast a bound substrate is converted. Watch for two things the Langmuir surface never showed: a rate that saturates, and an occupancy that sits below Langmuir's for the very same bond.

  2. Fast turnover (kcat ≫ koff): sweep again. The occupancy trace now sits under Langmuir's dashed line at every C, and the deposits' midpoint has slid right, to KM. The enzyme empties its own sites by working.
  3. Read Vmax off the plateau in the stats: it is kcat × 200 sites and contains nothing about the bond. Then double kcat. Vmax doubles and KM moves — one dial, two effects.

The scheme is the master equation of §1.6 with its third arrow switched on (case 4a). An enzyme's site \(s\) binds a substrate molecule \(p\) into the complex \(sp\); the complex either falls apart the way it formed or goes forward to a product \(q\), freeing the site:

eq. 3.2.13 $$s + p \;\underset{k_{off}}{\overset{k_{on}}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; sp \;\xrightarrow{\;k_{cat}\;}\; s + q$$

The book-keeping for the fraction of sites that are busy is eq. 3.1.9 with \(k_{cat} > 0\) — Langmuir's rate equation (eq. 3.2.4) with one more way to empty a site — a bound substrate leaves either as itself, at \(k_{off}\), or as product, at \(k_{cat}\). The substrate concentration is \(C\), held constant:

eq. 3.2.14 $$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = k_{on}\,C\,(1-\theta) \;-\; (k_{off} + k_{cat})\,\theta$$

What the factory delivers is the number of busy sites times the rate each one works: \(v = k_{cat}N_s\,\theta\), with \(N_s\) the total number of enzyme sites (our 200). Set \(d\theta/dt = 0\), exactly as in §2.3:

  2. Divide top and bottom by \(k_{on}\) and name the concentration that appears:
    \(\theta_{ss} = \dfrac{C}{C + K_M}, \qquad K_M \equiv \dfrac{k_{off}+k_{cat}}{k_{on}}\)

Two assumptions are hiding here. Steady state — \(d\theta/dt = 0\) while \(C\) is still what it was — is Briggs and Haldane's (1925) reading of the scheme; Michaelis and Menten (1913) assumed instead that binding stays at equilibrium, which is the \(k_{cat} \ll k_{off}\) limit where \(K_M \to K_D\). And the sim holds \(S\) constant, a reservoir — the initial-rate condition. In a real assay the enzyme eats its own substrate, \(S\) falls, and \(v\) falls with it; Michaelis and Menten did both — initial rates first, then whole time courses with product inhibition — and the single constant their global fit returned was \(V_{max}/K_M\), not \(K_M\).
eq. 3.2.15 $$v = k_{cat}N_s\,\theta_{ss} = \frac{V_{max}\,C}{K_M + C}, \qquad K_M \equiv \frac{k_{off}+k_{cat}}{k_{on}}, \quad V_{max} \equiv k_{cat}N_s$$

This is the Michaelis–Menten equation. Put it beside eq. 3.2.8 and read it the same way: linear at \(C \ll K_M\) (every substrate that arrives finds a free enzyme), half of the maximum at \(C = K_M\), flat at \(V_{max}\) when every site is busy and the factory is limited by \(k_{cat}\) alone. Same curve, two differences. The midpoint has moved: \(K_M \ge K_D\), because a working enzyme keeps emptying its own sites and needs more substrate to keep half of them busy. And the plateau, \(V_{max} = k_{cat}N_s\), does not contain the bond at all — it is a site count times a speed.

Same curve, two questions. Langmuir's midpoint is a fact about a bond. Michaelis–Menten's midpoint is a fact about the bond and the turnover, and it reads as an affinity only when \(k_{cat} \ll k_{off}\). 3.1 · What the cell reads

A cell never sees a concentration. It sees how many of its \(N_s\) receptors are occupied right now — a count, \(N_s\theta\) — and the count flickers as ligands come and go. Whatever the cell does next (secrete, transcribe, crawl) is some function of that count. Below is one cell. Sweep the concentration and watch two things: the reading, and how much it flickers.

  2. Sweep C across three decades and let the deposits trace the curve. It is gentle: 81-fold in C from a tenth to nine-tenths occupied.
  3. Change Ns. The flicker shrinks as 1/√Ns: ten thousand receptors read four times more steadily than a thousand.

3.2 · Deciding yes or no: a threshold

Most fate decisions are all-or-none. Divide or not; differentiate or not; start crawling or not. The simplest rule a cell can use is a threshold: commit when the occupancy exceeds \(\theta^*\). 3.3 · Deciding a direction — and what saturates it

The neutrophil's real decision is not yes or no but which way. It compares the occupancy of its front with that of its back and crawls toward the fuller side. In a field that changes by a fraction \(\Delta C/C\) across the cell, the two readings differ by \(\theta(1-\theta)\,\Delta C/C\) — the slope of the ruler again — and that factor \(\theta(1-\theta)\) is the whole story of what saturates the signal. It is largest at \(C = K_D\). Far below, almost no receptor is occupied on either side; far above, every receptor on both sides is full. Either way the difference vanishes and the cell cannot steer.

  1. At C = KD the arrow points steadily toward the front: the two halves read 0.525 against 0.475.
  2. Raise C to 100 KD. Both halves read 0.99 and the arrow wanders: the signal has saturated. Too much of the very factor the cell is chasing blinds it.
  3. Lower C to KD/100. Lost again — now neither half reads anything. The cell can steer only in the decade or so around KD.
  4. Steepen the gradient, or add receptors: the signal-to-noise ratio in the stats is what a single glance can resolve.

3.4 · From decision to behaviour: signalling sharpens the reading

The function \(g\) in eq. 3.3.1 is not a metaphor. It is a chain of enzymes — each a Michaelis–Menten factory (§2.7) — and that is what cell signalling is from this week's side. In the migrating neutrophil the chain runs from the occupied receptor through a G protein to a lipid kinase, and the lipid it makes (PIP3) piles up at the front of the cell far more steeply than the receptor occupancy that caused it: Servant and colleagues (2000) watched this amplification live. There it recruits the machinery that pushes the membrane forward. The receptors supply a gentle, saturating difference (§3.3); the cascade sharpens it into a decision; the decision is behaviour.

How much can a cascade sharpen? Langmuir's reading needs an 81-fold change in \(C\) to go from a tenth to nine-tenths. A response with Hill coefficient \(n\) — from cooperative receptors (§2.6) or from feedback in the cascade — needs only

eq. 3.3.5 $$\frac{C_{90}}{C_{10}} = 81^{1/n} \qquad (81 \text{ for } n = 1;\ 3 \text{ for } n = 4;\ 1.13 \text{ for } n = 35)$$

Ferrell and Machleder (1998) measured \(n \ge 35\) for the cascade that decides whether a frog oocyte matures: a 13 % change in hormone flips the cell. The price of a switch is information — a cell that decides sharply at \(C^*\) can no longer tell \(0.5\,C^*\) from \(0.1\,C^*\). Whether a cell wants a dimmer or a switch depends on whether it needs to measure its environment (a neutrophil steering) or to commit (an oocyte maturing).

How steep must a switch be to commit within ±10 % of its threshold?
  1. Write what you know. "Off" at \(0.9\,C^*\), "on" at \(1.1\,C^*\), taking 10 % and 90 % of maximal response as off and on.
    \(C_{90}/C_{10} = 1.1/0.9 = 1.22\)
  2. Solve eq. 3.3.5 for \(n\).
    \(n = \dfrac{\ln 81}{\ln 1.22} \approx 22\)
About 22 — far beyond binding cooperativity (haemoglobin's is 2.8) and squarely in the range only feedback-amplified cascades reach. Fate switches are built from cascades, not from receptors.

3.5 · Position from concentration: the French flag

Put the threshold rule in a gradient and you have the oldest idea in developmental biology in this week's symbols. Wolpert (1969) proposed that a cell reads its position from the local concentration of a morphogen — a factor emitted at one end and cleared as it spreads — and picks its fate by thresholds: one fate above the first threshold, another between, a third below. A tricolour: the French flag. The field is Week 2's steady source with clearance, in one dimension an exponential \(C(x) = C_0 e^{-x/\lambda}\); the reading is eq. 3.3.1; the thresholds are eq. 3.3.2; the sharpness of each stripe boundary is eq. 3.3.3.

Watch where the boundaries fall, how far they move when the source is doubled, and how ragged they are when the cells have few receptors.

  3. Drop Ns to 30: the boundaries become ragged and cells near them flicker between fates. Read the measured jitter against λ·δC/C — the decision band of eq. 3.3.3, turned into a length by the slope of the gradient.

Three consequences, all visible in the sim. 3.6 · Fine print: is the reading honest, and how fast is it?

Every rule above took \(C\) to be the concentration the cell is bathed in. Two things can spoil that, and a tab about fate has to own both. First, a surface can consume faster than diffusion feeds it, so the concentration at the receptors is lower than the concentration in the environment — the cell then reads a number that is not the environment's. Second, the reading takes time to catch up when the environment changes. The first is refereed by one dimensionless number; the second by one clock. Neither changes the rules of §3.1–3.5; both say when the rules may be applied with the bulk concentration.

One flux, two ways to write it

In steady operation the molecules crossing the film per area per second are the molecules the surface is taking up per area per second — there is nowhere else for them to go. Write the same flux twice, once as delivery and once as reaction, with \(\Gamma_{sp}\) the occupied sites per area and \(\Gamma_{max}\) the site density (so \(\theta = \Gamma_{sp}/\Gamma_{max}\)):

eq. 3.3.7 $$J = k_m\,(C - C_s) \;=\; k_{on}\,C_s\,(\Gamma_{max} - \Gamma_{sp}) \;-\; k_{off}\,\Gamma_{sp}$$

The right-hand side is Langmuir's rate equation (eq. 3.2.4) multiplied through by \(\Gamma_{max}\) and evaluated, honestly this time, at the surface concentration \(C_s\). The left-hand side is new only in its packaging. Delivery across a film of thickness \(\delta\) is Fick's first law — Week 1's eq. 1.3.5, the current down a gradient:

eq. 1.3.5 again — Fick I across the film $$J = -D\,\frac{dC}{dx} \approx D\,\frac{C - C_s}{\delta}$$

and everything about the geometry of delivery is swept into one mass-transfer coefficient, with the units of a speed — how fast the bath can replace what the surface removes:

eq. 3.3.8 $$k_m = \frac{D}{\delta} \qquad\text{(a film)}; \qquad k_m = \frac{D}{a} \qquad\text{(a sphere of radius } a\text{, from eq. 2.3.5)}$$

The spherical version is worth a moment: Week 2's flux at contact was \(J_a = DC/a\) (eq. 2.3.5), so a sphere's \(k_m\) is \(D/a\), and \(k_m \times 4\pi a^2 \times C = 4\pi D a\,C\) — Smoluchowski's rate, eq. 1.5.1, recovered from the delivery side of the ledger alone. A perfect absorber is what the relay looks like when the surface always wins the race: \(C_s = 0\) and the flux is pure delivery, \(J = k_m C\). Langmuir with the bulk concentration is what it looks like when delivery always wins: \(C_s = C\). The referee below shows you both, and the whole continuum in between.

Prove it: the number that referees

The cleanest case is a fresh surface — \(\Gamma_{sp} \approx 0\), the moment after the ligand appears, which is also the moment a cell most needs a reading. Earn the referee from eq. 3.3.7:

Derive the surface concentration and flux of a fresh surface
  1. Set \(\Gamma_{sp} = 0\) in eq. 3.3.7. The departure term vanishes and the reaction side is linear in \(C_s\):
    \(k_m\,(C - C_s) = k_{on}\,\Gamma_{max}\,C_s\)
  2. Solve for \(C_s\).
    \(k_m C = C_s\,(k_m + k_{on}\Gamma_{max}) \;\Rightarrow\; C_s = \dfrac{k_m}{k_m + k_{on}\Gamma_{max}}\,C\)
  3. Name the ratio. Divide numerator and denominator by \(k_m\):
    \(\dfrac{C_s}{C} = \dfrac{1}{1 + \mathrm{Da}}, \qquad \mathrm{Da} \equiv \dfrac{k_{on}\Gamma_{max}}{k_m}\)
  4. Substitute back for the flux.
    \(J = k_m(C - C_s) = k_m C\Bigl(1 - \dfrac{1}{1+\mathrm{Da}}\Bigr) = k_m C\,\dfrac{\mathrm{Da}}{1+\mathrm{Da}}\)
    — which for \(\mathrm{Da} \gg 1\) is \(k_m C\) (pure delivery) and for \(\mathrm{Da} \ll 1\) is \(k_{on}\Gamma_{max}C\) (pure reaction, at the bulk concentration).
\(C_s/C = 1/(1+\mathrm{Da})\): the sim's readout, predicted from the two speeds alone. A tie (\(C_s/C = \tfrac12\)) is \(\mathrm{Da} = 1\) exactly.
"Fresh" matters. As \(\Gamma\) grows, the reaction side's effective speed is \(k_{on}(\Gamma_{max}-\Gamma) = k_{on}\Gamma_{max}(1-\theta)\), so the working Damköhler number is \(\mathrm{Da}(1-\theta)\) and shrinks toward zero as the surface fills — the recovery of \(C_s\) you watched in the hungry chip. A finite surface is only ever transport-limited for a while.
eq. 3.3.9 $$\mathrm{Da} = \frac{k_{on}\,\Gamma_{max}}{k_m} \quad\begin{cases} \ll 1 & \text{reaction-limited: doors are slow, } C_s \approx C\text{, Langmuir kinetics (eq. 3.2.4) apply as written} \\[4pt] \gg 1 & \text{transport-limited: the surface is hungry, } C_s \ll C\text{, flux saturates at } J \approx k_m C \end{cases}$$

The surface Damköhler number: the rate at which the surface could take up molecules if it were held at the bulk concentration, over the rate at which delivery can replace them. Both are speeds (m/s), so Da is a pure number — and a pure number is exactly what you want a referee to be, because it does not care whether you are talking about a bacterium, a biosensor chip or a catalyst pellet. For the fresh surface:

eq. 3.3.10 $$C_s = \frac{k_m}{k_m + k_{on}\Gamma_{max}}\,C = \frac{C}{1 + \mathrm{Da}}$$
eq. 3.3.11 $$J = \frac{k_m\,C}{1 + \mathrm{Da}}\cdot\mathrm{Da} \;=\; \frac{k_{on}\Gamma_{max}\,C}{1 + \mathrm{Da}}$$

Read eq. 3.3.11 as the reaction rate you would have had, \(k_{on}\Gamma_{max}C\), knocked down by \((1+\mathrm{Da})\). A surface with \(\mathrm{Da} = 9\) binds at one-tenth of its chemistry's pace; making its receptors stickier would change nothing, because it is not the receptors that are slow. That is the decision the referee makes: which knob is worth turning.

Note from Dr. McGhee. Gerhard Damköhler, a German chemist, introduced these ratios in the 1930s while working out how to scale up chemical reactors — nondimensionalising the governing equations and finding the groups that compare reaction rates with transport rates. The idea became a backbone of combustion and reaction engineering, and it is equally central today in porous media and bioreactors, for the same reason it is central here: it cleanly separates a kinetics-limited world (\(\mathrm{Da} \ll 1\)) from a transport-limited one (\(\mathrm{Da} \gg 1\)), and tells you which physics you are allowed to ignore.
A dimensionless number ranks; it does not rate. Da tells you which runner is slower. It does not tell you whether either of them is fast enough. A surface with \(\mathrm{Da} = 10^{-3}\) is reaction-limited whether its reaction takes a millisecond or a month. Always check the absolute clocks — \(1/(k_{on}C)\) for the doors, \(\delta^2/D\) or \(a^2/D\) for the delivery — before you let the ratio tell you what matters. The review module's stalled escalator had a Péclet number of ten thousand and cleared nothing; the same trap is waiting here.

The second piece of fine print: the reading lags

A cell's occupancy does not track its environment instantly. From eq. 3.2.9, a step in \(C\) is followed with a half-time \(\tau_{1/2} = \ln 2/(k_{on}C + k_{off})\): at low concentration this is set by \(k_{off}\) alone, so a receptor whose ligand stays a minute cannot report a change faster than about a minute, however sharp its threshold. This is the clock behind Tab 4's ledger — the neutrophil's reading takes minutes; its crawl takes tens of minutes — and it is the reason a cell that needs a fast answer uses a receptor with a fast \(k_{off}\), paying for it (eq. 3.2.7) with a weaker bond.

3.7 · The counting limit — how good can any reading be?

Every rule in this tab was one glance at \(N_s\) receptors, and eq. 3.3.3 priced the glance. But a glance is not a measurement; a cell can look for a while. How much does looking longer buy — and is there a floor that no cell, however it is built, can read below? Berg and Purcell (1977) answered with the simplest instrument imaginable: a sphere of radius \(a\) that swallows every molecule touching it and counts. In a time \(t_{avg}\) it counts \(N = k_s C\,t_{avg}\) molecules (eq. 1.5.1 again, \(k_s = 4\pi D a\) — the delivery Week 2 computed). Molecules arrive one at a time and independently — a steady stream of single shots — so the count wobbles by \(\sqrt N\) (Week 2's census noise, the flicker §2.1 promised would "collect properly"), and the concentration inferred from it, \(\hat C = N/(k_s t_{avg})\), inherits that fraction:

eq. 3.3.12 — the Berg–Purcell limit $$\frac{\delta C}{C} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt N} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D a\,C\,t_{avg}}}$$

This is the floor. Nothing about how a cell is wired appears in it — only its size, the ligand's diffusion, the concentration and how long it is willing to wait — because the best any device can do is count each arriving molecule once, and this one does. (The constant in front is model-dependent: Berg and Purcell's exact treatment of a sphere that watches its whole interior gives \(3/(5\pi)\) in place of \(1/(4\pi)\). The scaling is not.)

A real cell does not swallow. Its \(N_s\) receptors are §1.5's telegraph — bound, free, bound — so the instantaneous occupancy has the one-glance flicker of eq. 3.3.3, and the sim below averages it over windows of length \(t_{avg}\) and inverts each average through the isotherm (eq. 3.2.8). The catch is that the flicker is correlated: a receptor that is bound now is probably still bound a second from now, so two consecutive glances are the same glance until the population has turned over. The turnover clock is the population's correlation time:

eq. 3.3.13 $$\tau_c = \frac{1}{k_{on}C + k_{off}} = \frac{1-\theta}{k_{off}}$$

— about 50 s for the neutrophil's receptor at \(C = K_D\). Averaging for \(t_{avg} \gg \tau_c\) buys \(t_{avg}/2\tau_c\) independent looks, so the receptor reading's error is the one-glance band divided by the square root of that number:

eq. 3.3.14 — the receptor reading (Berg & Purcell's eq. 51, for \(N_s\) receptors) $$\Bigl(\frac{\delta C}{C}\Bigr)^2 \approx \frac{2\tau_c}{t_{avg}}\cdot\frac{1}{N_s\,\theta(1-\theta)} = \frac{2}{N_s\,k_{off}\,\theta\,t_{avg}} \qquad (t_{avg} \gg \tau_c)$$

The right-hand form says what a cell can do about it: more receptors, a faster \(k_{off}\) — paid for, through eq. 3.2.7, with a weaker bond — or a longer look. Nothing else. A third exit (§2.7's \(k_{cat}\)) shortens \(\tau_c\) exactly as a larger \(k_{off}\) would; the cell still counts only what sticks. Berg and Purcell put it more sharply: once a molecule has occupied a receptor, its later visits carry no information — only new molecules count, and eq. 3.3.14 is just \(2/(\text{new molecules captured})\).

Derive the receptor reading, and why it is a count
  1. One receptor is a telegraph. Bound with probability \(\theta\), it flips at total rate \(k_{on}C + k_{off} = 1/\tau_c\); its occupancy autocorrelation decays as \(e^{-|\tau|/\tau_c}\) (Berg & Purcell's eq. 49).
  2. Average it for a window \(t_{avg}\). The variance of a window average of a process with variance \(\theta(1-\theta)\) and correlation time \(\tau_c\) is exactly
    \(\mathrm{var}(\bar\theta) = \theta(1-\theta)\,g(u),\quad g(u) = \dfrac{2}{u}\Bigl(1 - \dfrac{1-e^{-u}}{u}\Bigr),\quad u = \dfrac{t_{avg}}{\tau_c}\)
    \(g \to 1\) for \(u \ll 1\) (one glance) and \(g \to 2/u\) for \(u \gg 1\) — the sim draws the exact \(g\); the box keeps the long-window limit.
  3. \(N_s\) independent receptors divide the variance by \(N_s\); the isotherm's slope \(d\theta/d\ln C = \theta(1-\theta)\) (§3.2's derive) converts it to concentration:
    \(\Bigl(\dfrac{\delta C}{C}\Bigr)^2 = \dfrac{\theta(1-\theta)\,g}{N_s\,\theta^2(1-\theta)^2} \;\xrightarrow{u\gg1}\; \dfrac{2\tau_c}{t_{avg}\,N_s\,\theta(1-\theta)}\)
  4. Make it a count. At equilibrium \(k_{on}C = k_{off}\,\theta/(1-\theta)\), so \(\tau_c = (1-\theta)/k_{off}\) and
    \(\Bigl(\dfrac{\delta C}{C}\Bigr)^2 = \dfrac{2}{N_s\,k_{off}\,\theta\,t_{avg}} = \dfrac{2}{N_s\,k_{on}C\,(1-\theta)\,t_{avg}} = \dfrac{2}{\text{molecules newly captured in } t_{avg}}\)
    \(N_s k_{on}C(1-\theta)\) is the rate at which empty receptors catch new molecules. The reading is a count after all — of captures, not arrivals.
Eq. 3.3.14. For \(N_s = 1\) it is Berg & Purcell's eq. 51, \(\Delta c/\bar c = (2\tau_b/T\bar p)^{1/2}\), with \(\tau_b = 1/k_{off}\) and \(\bar p = \theta\).
The receptors are treated as independent. A molecule released by one receptor is well placed to find a neighbour, which correlates them; Berg & Purcell handle this by counting new molecules to the whole cell (their eq. 55), Bialek & Setayeshgar (2005) by adding the diffusive return explicitly. Both leave the scaling and the floor intact.

Now put eq. 3.3.14 over eq. 3.3.12. The concentration and the time cancel, and what is left is a number this tab already owns:

eq. 3.3.15 — the gap to the floor $$\frac{(\delta C/C)^2_{\text{receptors}}}{(\delta C/C)^2_{\text{absorber}}} = \frac{2\,k_s}{N_s\,k_{on}\,(1-\theta)} = \frac{2}{(1-\theta)\,\mathrm{Da}}, \qquad \mathrm{Da} = \frac{N_s k_{on}}{4\pi D a}$$

— with \(\mathrm{Da}\) the §3.6 referee written for a sphere (eq. 3.3.9 with \(k_m = D/a\) and \(N_s = 4\pi a^2\Gamma_{max}\)). The gap between a receptor reading and the floor is the reciprocal of the Damköhler number: a cell reads at the counting limit only if it captures nearly every arrival, \(\mathrm{Da} \gtrsim 1\) — and §3.6 showed that a surface which does that depletes its own neighbourhood and no longer reads the bulk concentration. A cell cannot have both an honest reading and a perfect one. The neutrophil, at \(\mathrm{Da} \approx 0.03\) and \(\theta = \tfrac12\), sits \(\sqrt{2/(0.5 \times 0.03)} \approx 11\) times above the floor — and does not care, because §3.3 showed one glance already tells its front from its back.

A reading is a count. The floor is \(1/\sqrt{\text{arrivals}}\) (eq. 3.3.12); a receptor reading sits \(\sqrt{2/((1-\theta)\mathrm{Da})}\) above it (eq. 3.3.15) and falls as \(1/\sqrt{t_{avg}}\) only once \(t_{avg}\) exceeds \(\tau_c\) (eq. 3.3.13–3.3.14). Sensing costs time, and the price is set by \(N_s\), \(k_{off}\) and Da.
  1. Default chip, read with receptors. Let the windows fill. The measured error is flat for tavg shorter than τc (eq. 3.3.13), then falls along a slope of −½: four times the averaging time, half the error (eq. 3.3.14).
  2. Switch to count every arrival. The points now land on the orange floor (eq. 3.3.12) — and the floor has no plateau: every arriving molecule is a new one. Read the gap between the two runs at 100 s against √(2/((1−θ)Da)) in the stats (eq. 3.3.15).
  3. Back to receptors. Raise koff tenfold: τc shrinks, the plateau ends sooner, the long-window error drops by √10 — and KD has risen tenfold with it. Speed is bought with affinity.

Three rules, from one inequality

Rearranged, eq. 3.3.12 reads \(t_{avg} \ge 1/\bigl(k_s C\,(\delta C/C)^2\bigr)\), and Berg and Purcell's three design rules fall out of it:

  • Integration time for a target precision. Halving the error costs four times the wait; below \(\tau_c\) (eq. 3.3.13) waiting buys nothing at all. A cell that needs a fast answer needs a fast \(k_{off}\).
  • Geometry and transport set the ceiling. A bigger collector (\(a\)), a faster ligand (\(D\)), a higher \(C\) all raise the count; and receptors need not tile the surface — \(N_s \approx \pi a/a_s\) of them, a fraction of a percent of the area, capture half of what a perfect absorber would (their eq. 55–56; Week 2's eq. 2.4.7). Sparse is nearly optimal, provided each capture is of a new molecule.
  • An upper bound on information. No downstream biochemistry can add what the count did not carry. Cascades sharpen a decision (eq. 3.3.5) and amplify a signal; they cannot narrow the band. This is the front-end constraint everything else must respect — Week 4's capstone spends it.

The neutrophil resolves its gradient in a glance; the bacterium would need to hold still for ~17 s to resolve the difference across its own body, and it never holds still for more than one. So it does not compare front with back — it compares now with a second ago, along a run (Tab 4.3). Berg and Purcell's paper ends on exactly this point.
  • A cell's input is a count, \(N_s\theta\); its response is a function of it (eq. 3.3.1). Graded, threshold and cooperative responses are the three shapes that function takes.
  • The receptor's \(K_D\) sets where on the concentration axis a fate flips (eq. 3.3.2, \(C^* = K_D\theta^*/(1-\theta^*)\)); the receptor count sets how reliably: one glance is undecided within \(\delta C/C = 1/\sqrt{N_s\theta(1-\theta)}\) of \(C^*\) (eq. 3.3.3).
  • The direction a migrating cell reads is \(\Delta\theta = \theta(1-\theta)\,\Delta C/C\) (eq. 3.3.4): it peaks at \(K_D\) and saturates to nothing far above or below it. Signalling cascades sharpen the reading, \(C_{90}/C_{10} = 81^{1/n}\) (eq. 3.3.5), at the price of measuring nothing far from \(C^*\).
  • In a gradient, thresholds become positions: \(x_i = \lambda\ln(C_0/C_i^*)\), with a precision \(\lambda\,\delta C/C\) (eq. 3.3.6). Doubling the source moves every boundary by \(\lambda\ln 2\); the pattern scales with \(\lambda\).
  • Fine print: the rules use the bulk \(C\) only when \(\mathrm{Da} = k_{on}\Gamma_{max}/k_m \ll 1\) (eq. 3.3.9–3.3.10), and the reading follows the world no faster than \(1/(k_{on}C + k_{off})\) (eq. 3.2.9). A dimensionless number ranks; it does not rate — check the clocks.
  • Every reading has a floor, \(\delta C/C = 1/\sqrt{4\pi D a C t_{avg}}\) (eq. 3.3.12): count every arrival and you cannot do better. A receptor reading sits \(\sqrt{2/((1-\theta)\mathrm{Da})}\) above it (eq. 3.3.15) and improves only as \(1/\sqrt{t_{avg}}\) once \(t_{avg} > \tau_c\) (eq. 3.3.13–3.3.14). Honest readings are never perfect ones.

4.2 · Reading one: the neutrophil's front and back

Tab 3 issued the licence: with \(\mathrm{Da} \approx 0.03\), the neutrophil may use Langmuir's isotherm with the concentration it sits in. Its front, facing the colony, sits in a slightly higher concentration than its back, 15 µm away. Each side reads the field with eq. 3.2.8, and the cell's information is the difference:

eq. 3.4.2 $$\Delta\theta = \theta_{eq}(C_{\text{front}}) - \theta_{eq}(C_{\text{back}}) \;\approx\; \frac{d\theta_{eq}}{dC}\,\Bigl|\frac{dC}{dr}\Bigr|\,2a \;=\; \frac{K_{eq}}{(1+K_{eq}C)^{2}}\cdot C\Bigl(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\Bigr)\cdot 2a$$

Since \(K_{eq}C/(1+K_{eq}C)^2 = \theta(1-\theta)\), eq. 3.4.2 is really \(\Delta\theta = \theta(1-\theta)\times\Delta\ln C\): the ruler's slope (Tab 2.5 — largest at \(C = K_D\), pinned near 0 and 1) times the fractional change in concentration across the cell, \((1/r + 1/\lambda)\,2a\). The second factor is the field's doing and grows as \(1/r\) toward the colony; the first is the receptor's doing and is a hump centred on \(K_D\). In a \(1/r\) field the steepness wins: the reading keeps growing as the cell closes in, and saturation only slows it. Where \(K_D\) earns its keep is the far field, where the fractional gradient is shallow and the cell can only read at all if its ruler is on scale there. Place the cell, and watch the two factors trade.

  1. Slide the cell in from 500 µm. Far out, the field is shallow and the ruler is pinned low: Δθ is a fraction of a percent. Where the field crosses KD it is ~5 %; closer still it keeps growing, because the fractional gradient steepens as 1/r faster than saturation flattens the ruler.
  2. Park the cell at 400 µm and lower KD tenfold. The far-field reading jumps several-fold — a receptor tuned to the concentrations out there lets the cell start reading from far away. That is what affinity buys: where the reading begins, not how large it gets.
  3. Check the stats: τ½ at this concentration and Da. The reading is licensed (Da ≪ 1) and it settles in a minute or so — faster than the cell can crawl its own diameter.

Deeper (566): 2,000 receptors at 13 nM, or 53,000 at 0.6 nM? Both papers are right about something

Williams et al. (1977), with a tritiated tripeptide and a filtration assay, found about 2,000 sites per cell with \(K_D\) = 12–14 nM. Sklar et al. (1984), watching a fluorescent hexapeptide bind in real time on a flow cytometer, found 53,000 ± 13,000 sites at 0.6 ± 0.2 nM — and also found that bound receptors are internalised at ~24 % per minute after a 20–30 s latency, and that the complex's affinity changes with time bound. A filtration assay that washes cells for minutes at 37 °C loses fast-dissociating complexes and internalises the rest; a different ligand has a different \(K_D\); and a receptor population whose affinity drifts during the measurement does not have a single \(K_D\) at all. The lesson for this module: Langmuir's two constants are a model of a surface. A living cell moves its doors indoors while you are counting them — one of the "what the model cannot say" items below.

4.3 · Reading two: the bacterium can't, so it remembers

Run eq. 3.4.2 for E. coli. Its baseline is \(2a = 1\) µm instead of 15, so where the field crosses its own \(K_D\) the front–back difference is fifteen times smaller than the neutrophil's — about a third of a percent. Its ~1,200 aspartate receptors, blinking at random, flicker by roughly \(1/\sqrt{1200\,\theta(1-\theta)} \approx 6\) % from one instant to the next. A signal of 0.3 % against a flicker of 6 % is not a reading. A bacterium cannot sense a spatial gradient across its body, and it does not try.

What it does instead is trade space for time. It swims in a straight run for about a second, covering ~20 µm — twenty body lengths — then tumbles for a tenth of a second and sets off in a new random direction. During a run it compares the occupancy of its receptors now with a running memory of what it was a few seconds ago. If occupancy is rising, it postpones the tumble; if falling, it tumbles sooner. That is the whole algorithm, and it turns a random walk into a biased random walk up the gradient without the cell ever knowing which way "up" is. What the model cannot say

  • How close to the floor the reading is. §3.7 priced it: 53,000 receptors resolve the 5 % in one glance, but sit a factor of ~11 above the Berg–Purcell floor (eq. 3.3.15) — the price of an honest, reaction-limited reading.

  • Internalisation. Sklar's cells took bound receptors indoors at ~24 %/min. Langmuir's steady state assumes the doors stay on the surface; a live cell's do not, so \(\theta_{eq}\) is a target the cell approaches and then spoils.

  • No immune system beyond one cell. Complement, cytokines, the endothelium the neutrophil had to cross: none of it is in this ledger.

5.1 · Next week's headline, measured today — the surface that senses is a thermal object

Every reader in this module was a rigid sphere with its receptors nailed on. The real surface is a lipid bilayer five nanometres thick — two layers of molecules held together by nothing but their dislike of water — and it is not rigid, and it is not still. The same bath that shook Tab 1's tracer out of its well shakes the membrane into a spectrum of ripples, each mode carrying \(\tfrac12 k_BT\) (Week 1's equipartition, one more time). Two numbers govern how much it moves: a tension \(\sigma\), the energy per area it costs to stretch the sheet, and a bending stiffness \(\kappa\), the energy it costs to curve it. Next week earns both from the molecules up; today only measures what they do to the surface the receptors ride. The sim below is the smallest honest preview of the third row's last clause. A bare vesicle — one bilayer, ten microns across, nothing inside but salt water — is moved into a bath a few percent more dilute than its interior. Water follows the salt (Week 1's diffusion, one more time), the vesicle swells — which dilutes its inside — and the sheet stretches. It can stretch about three percent, which is four and a half percent of volume. Watch how little dilution that turns out to allow. No equation is minted here: next week gives the stretch its law (a stiffness, \(K_A\)), the swelling its pressure (Laplace), and the tear its barrier — and then asks how your own cells, which survive far worse, do it.

  1. Isotonic: nothing happens, and that is the baseline — a resting bilayer carries almost no tension.
  2. Mildly hypotonic (6 mOsm — two percent of the bath's 300): the vesicle swells to a new size and stops, its inside now almost as dilute as the bath. Read the area strain and the tension it implies. The dashed line is where next week's equations say it will stop.
  3. Hypotonic (16 mOsm, five percent): it never gets there. Log several bursts; the strain at rupture scatters around 3 % — the scatter is not a bug, and next week explains it (a tear is a thermal escape, like Tab 1's well). Then drag the radius: size barely matters — the budget is the sheet's three percent.

  • A cell reads concentration through occupancy, and occupancy flickers: the error falls as \(1/\sqrt{t_{avg}}\) past \(\tau_c\), and eq. 3.3.12 is the floor under it. Sensing costs time, and §3.7 priced it.
  • The surface carrying the receptors is a thermal object: tension flattens it, bending stiffness sets its short-wavelength cutoff, and eq. 3.5.1 — previewed, not yet earned — says how much of each. Both get their molecules next week.
  • A bare bilayer stretches about three percent and then tears; a bath diluted by four percent is enough to burst a vesicle of any size, because swelling dilutes the inside and three percent of area is all the volume it can add. Your cells survive several times that — how is Week 4's question.
  • Reading is the input. Deciding (Hill), resolving (Berg–Purcell) and moving (Weeks 5–7) are what the cell does with it — and all of it happens on a sheet five nanometres thick.
