1.1 · The air is a mosh pit

Start with the room you are sitting in. Every cubic meter of the air holds about \(2.5\times10^{25}\) molecules, each flying at roughly the speed of a rifle bullet (~500 m/s), each colliding with a neighbor a few billion times per second, and traveling only ~70 nm — a thousandth of a hair's width — between hits. In water it is more crowded still: a protein sitting in the fluid of your mosquito bite is struck about \(10^{13}\) times every second.

Nothing at the molecular scale is ever still. "Stillness" is a macroscopic illusion — what you get when you average trillions of violent little collisions that almost, but not quite, cancel. Daniel Bernoulli guessed in 1738 that gas pressure was nothing but this bombardment — molecular impacts drumming on the walls — about 120 years before most physicists were willing to believe molecules existed at all.

1.2 · \(k_BT\) — the currency of the molecular world

How hard are those kicks? Temperature is the bookkeeping device. The Boltzmann constant \(k_B\) is the exchange rate between the temperature you measure with a thermometer and the energy each molecule carries. Two facts — which the box below exhibits, and which we will derive in §1.4:

eq. 1.1.1 $$\tfrac{3}{2}k_BT \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle$$
eq. 1.1.2 $$v_{rms} \;=\; \sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle} \;=\; \sqrt{3k_BT/m}$$

At body temperature, \(k_BT = 1.38\times10^{-23}\times310 = 4.28\times10^{-21}\) J. That number looks absurdly small until you write it in the units the cell actually works in: \(k_BT \approx 4.3\ \mathrm{pN\cdot nm}\) — piconewtons of force acting over nanometers of distance. That is precisely the scale of molecular motors, protein folding, and receptor binding, and it is why we call \(k_BT\) the currency of molecular-scale energy in the cell. Every process in this course either pays thermal noise its \(k_BT\) toll or gets randomized by it.

Worked example— how fast is the O₂ you're breathing?
  1. State the question as physics. "How fast" for a gas molecule means the typical thermal speed \(v_{rms}\) at body temperature, \(T = 310\) K. We need the molecule's mass and a law connecting temperature to speed.
  2. Write the tool. Equipartition (eq. 1.1.1) prices motion at \(\tfrac{1}{2}k_BT\) per direction — three directions in 3D:
    $$\tfrac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac{3}{2}k_BT$$
  3. Solve for the speed symbolically first. Rearranging before plugging in numbers is what keeps the units honest:
    $$v_{rms} = \sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} \quad\text{(eq. 1.1.2)}$$
  4. Get the mass in kg. One O₂ is one mole's worth divided by Avogadro:
    $$m_{O_2} = \frac{0.032\ \mathrm{kg/mol}}{6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}} = 5.31\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$$
  5. Plug in.
    $$v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3(1.381\times10^{-23})(310)}{5.31\times10^{-26}}} = \sqrt{2.42\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}} \approx 492\ \mathrm{m/s}$$
≈ 492 m/s — about Mach 1.4. Every breath is a swarm of supersonic projectiles, and you feel it only as gentle pressure because the kicks come from all sides at once.
Deeper: one temperature, a whole distribution of speeds

\(v_{rms}\) is not "the average speed" — it is the square root of the average squared speed, and those differ. Maxwell (1860) and Boltzmann (1868–72) worked out the full speed distribution that a colliding gas settles into. In 3D the mean speed is \(\bar v = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m} = 0.92\,v_{rms}\) (≈453 m/s for O₂ at 310 K) and the most-probable speed is \(0.82\,v_{rms}\). Keep the distinction — the end-of-lecture question "what is the average speed of an O₂ molecule?" is testing exactly this.

1.3 · The law the 1600s measured

Long before anyone believed in molecules, careful people with pistons and mercury columns found that gases obey a stunningly simple rule:

eq. 1.1.3 $$PV \;=\; nRT \;=\; N k_B T$$

It was assembled piecewise: Boyle (1662) — pressure and volume are inversely related at fixed temperature \((P\propto 1/V)\); Charles (1787) — volume grows linearly with temperature at fixed pressure \((V\propto T)\); Avogadro (1811) — equal volumes at equal \(T, P\) hold equal numbers of molecules \((n\propto V)\). The two forms in eq. 1.1.3 are the same law counted differently: \(R = k_B N_A\) is just the per-mole price of what \(k_B\) prices per molecule.

The law assumes an ideal gas:

  1. Gas molecules occupy negligible volume compared to the container.
  2. Molecules move randomly and continuously in straight lines.
  3. Collisions between molecules and walls are perfectly elastic.
  4. No intermolecular forces act except during collisions.
  5. The average kinetic energy of molecules depends only on temperature.
Worked example— lecture Q1: how many O₂ molecules in one breath?
  1. Gather the knowns. One tidal breath: \(V \approx 0.5\ \mathrm{L} = 5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^3}\), at atmospheric pressure \(P = 101{,}325\) Pa and body temperature \(T = 310\) K.
  2. Write the tool and solve it for what you want. The ideal gas law (eq. 1.1.3) counts molecules if you rearrange it for \(N\):
    $$PV = Nk_BT \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad N = \frac{PV}{k_BT}$$
  3. Evaluate the thermal energy once.
    $$k_BT = (1.381\times10^{-23})(310) = 4.28\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J}$$
  4. Plug in.
    $$N = \frac{(101{,}325)(5\times10^{-4})}{4.28\times10^{-21}} \approx 1.2\times10^{22}\ \text{molecules of air}$$
  5. Take the O₂ fraction. Air is 21% O₂ by count: \(N_{O_2} = 0.21 \times 1.2\times10^{22}\).
≈ 2.5×10²¹ oxygen molecules per breath — about four hundred billion times more molecules than there are people on Earth. This is why "average" behavior (pressure, temperature) is so smooth: the statistics are unimaginably deep.

1.4 · Why the law is true — one molecule at a time

By the mid-1800s, Maxwell and Boltzmann showed the empirical gas law follows from almost nothing: the kinetic molecular theory (KMT). Its assumptions are the ideal-gas list recast as mechanics — (1) particles are point masses, (2) in constant random straight-line motion, (3) colliding elastically, (4) with negligible intermolecular forces, and (5) with temperature proportional to their average kinetic energy.

Here is the whole derivation. One molecule of mass \(m\) in a cubic box of side \(L\), bouncing off the wall perpendicular to \(x\) with velocity \(v_x\). Each elastic bounce delivers an impulse, so the average force during a round trip is:

eq. 1.1.4 $$F_x = \frac{\Delta (m v_x)}{t} = \frac{2m v_x}{t}, \qquad \langle t\rangle = \frac{2L}{v_x}$$
eq. 1.1.5 $$\langle F_x\rangle = \frac{2m v_x}{\langle t\rangle} = \frac{m v_x^2}{L}, \qquad \langle P_x\rangle = \frac{\langle F_x\rangle}{L^2} = \frac{m v_x^2}{V}$$

Real molecules move in 3D with no preferred direction, so on average each direction carries a third of the squared speed:

eq. 1.1.6 $$\langle v_x^2\rangle = \langle v_y^2\rangle = \langle v_z^2\rangle = \tfrac{1}{3}\langle v^2\rangle \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; P = \frac{1}{3}\frac{m\langle v^2\rangle}{V} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; P = \frac{N}{V}\frac{m\langle v^2\rangle}{3}$$

Now set eq. 1.1.6 against the measured law, eq. 1.1.3:

eq. 1.1.7 $$PV = N\frac{m\langle v^2\rangle}{3} = Nk_BT \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; k_BT = \frac{m\langle v^2\rangle}{3} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \tfrac{3}{2}k_BT = \tfrac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle$$

— which is exactly the pair of facts we asserted in §1.2, now derived. Temperature has a microscopic meaning: it is the average kinetic energy per molecule, read in different units. The loop is closed: bouncing molecules (§1.1) → measurable law (§1.3) → and the bridge between them is \(k_BT\) (§1.2).

Deeper: where the brackets matter

\(\langle v_x^2\rangle\) is not \(\langle v_x\rangle^2\) — the second is zero (as many molecules move left as right), the first is what pressure is made of. It is common to drop the \(\langle\,\rangle\) brackets partway through a derivation like this for readability; keep them in your head. Averages of squares, not squares of averages, is the single most reused move in this course — it returns as the mean-squared displacement in Tab 2.

Answer

Q3. Cellular energies are quoted in \(k_BT\). Rank: a hydrogen bond (~2–5 \(k_BT\)), ATP hydrolysis (~20–25 \(k_BT\)), a covalent C–C bond (~150 \(k_BT\)). Which can thermal motion break spontaneously?

Answer

Hydrogen bonds sit only a few \(k_BT\) up, so thermal kicks break them constantly — which is exactly why they are biology's fastener of choice (bonds that must be reversible). ATP's ~20 \(k_BT\) is safely above noise (that is what makes it a usable fuel packet), and covalent bonds at ~150 \(k_BT\) essentially never break thermally — that is what makes a molecule a molecule.

2.1 · What Brown actually saw

In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown watched pollen granules in water dance erratically under his microscope. His crucial move was the control: he repeated the observation with definitely-dead matter — ground rock dust — and the dance persisted. Not life. He had no mechanism, but he had defined the phenomenon that now carries his name: Brownian motion.

Put yourself in his position. You can see the mote. You cannot see what is hitting it — air and water molecules are far below the resolution of any light microscope, then or now. All you observe is a speck of dust that will not sit still, with nothing touching it.

The simulation below lets you cheat. It is a mote of dust in air, and the show/hide molecules button is the thing Brown never had. Hide them and you have his 1827 view: unexplained motion. Show them and the explanation is obvious — the mote is being shot at from every side, thousands of times a second, and the kicks do not quite cancel.

Scale warning, so the picture stays honest. A real speck of dust is ~10⁹ times the mass of an air molecule and is struck ~10¹⁶ times a second; simulated faithfully it would sit motionless on your screen forever. The mote here is only tens to thousands of times heavier than the bath, so the imbalance is visible in real time. The mechanism is exact; the mass ratio is compressed.
  1. Start with the molecules hidden. Watch the mote for ten seconds. Could you explain that motion in 1827?
  2. Show the molecules. Every kink in the trail now has a visible cause.
  3. Grow the mote. It takes more hits per second, yet jiggles less — because the imbalance between opposite sides grows only as √N while the number of hits grows as N. Averaging beats the noise.
  4. Thin the air. Fewer, lumpier kicks — and the mote's path gets visibly more erratic.

2.2 · Why the pollen jiggles — and the sand grain doesn't

So thermal kicks move small things. Why, then, does a grain of sand sit on the bottom of a glass instead of dancing? When is a particle "small" enough to be Brownian?

The criterion is an energy balance: thermal kicks (\(k_BT\)) versus gravity. Do the bookkeeping carefully, because the idea is about to become one of the most reused in the course. Sitting at height \(h\) costs potential energy:

eq. 1.2.1 $$U(h) = \Delta\rho\,V g\,h \qquad\text{(buoyant weight × height)}$$

A settled population does not all lie at the energy minimum — thermal kicks keep lofting particles back up, so the pile reaches a standoff instead of collapsing onto the floor. The balance point is the height at which the climb costs exactly one thermal unit: gravity's toll equals the kicks' budget. That height is the sedimentation length:

eq. 1.2.2 $$U(\ell_g) = k_BT \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ell_g \;=\; \frac{k_BT}{\Delta\rho\, V g}$$

If \(\ell_g\) is much larger than the particle itself, gravity is a rounding error and the particle is fully Brownian; if \(\ell_g\) is smaller than the particle, it sits on the bottom like furniture.

Now watch what the sim's height histogram actually does. It does not merely thin out with height — it decays exponentially, with a 1/e height of exactly \(\ell_g\). That exponential is not a quirk of gravity; it is the shape any energy landscape stamps on a thermal population, and it is the most reused distribution in this course. Naming it properly takes the language of probability distributions — which is exactly what §2.3 and §2.4 are about to build — so we come back and name it in §2.4. Tab 3 then squeezes the Stokes–Einstein relation out of it.

Worked example— bead vs sand grain: who jiggles, who sinks?
  1. Set up the contest. Gravity charges an energy toll to climb; thermal energy pays it. The height at which the toll equals the budget is the sedimentation length — above it, gravity wins. Lifting a particle of buoyant density \(\Delta\rho\) and volume \(V\) by \(h\) costs \(U = \Delta\rho V g\,h\).
  2. Combine the two energies. Set the climbing cost equal to the thermal budget \(k_BT\) and solve for the height:
    $$\Delta\rho V g\,\ell_g = k_BT \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ell_g = \frac{k_BT}{\Delta\rho V g}$$
    The verdict rule: \(\ell_g \gg\) particle size → Brownian; \(\ell_g \ll\) size → it sediments.
  3. The bead's numbers. A 1-µm polystyrene bead: radius 0.5 µm, \(\Delta\rho \approx 50\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\):
    $$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi(0.5\times10^{-6})^3 = 5.2\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{m^3}, \quad \Delta\rho V g = (50)(5.2\times10^{-19})(9.8) = 2.6\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{N}$$
  4. The bead's verdict.
    $$\ell_g = \frac{4.28\times10^{-21}}{2.6\times10^{-16}} \approx 17\ \mu\mathrm{m} \;\gg\; 1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$$
    Thermal energy can lift it 17 of its own diameters — fully Brownian.
  5. The sand grain, same machine. 1 mm radius, \(\Delta\rho \approx 1650\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\): the volume is \(10^9\)× larger and the density mismatch 33× larger, so \(\ell_g\) collapses:
    $$\ell_g \sim 5\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{m} \;\lll\; 1\ \mathrm{mm}$$
The bead jiggles (ℓg = 17 µm ≫ its size); the sand grain gets less than a proton's width of thermal exploration and sinks like a stone. Brown would have needed a shovel. Everything cell-sized in water lives on the Brownian side.

Everything cell-sized or smaller in water sits far on the Brownian side. Cell biology has no choice about thermal motion — it lives entirely inside it.

2.3 · Pearson's drunkard and the law of \(\langle x^2\rangle\)

Summer 1905: the statistician Karl Pearson writes to Nature asking for help with "the problem of the random walk" — a man takes N steps, each in a random direction; where is he? Lord Rayleigh replied within weeks (he had solved it decades earlier in another disguise), and the answer entered folklore as the drunkard's walk: the most probable place to find the walker is near where he started — yet he unmistakably spreads. Einstein's 1905 diffusion theory is the molecular-scale drunkard's walk written in the language of physics.

The whole law fits in three lines. A walker on a line takes steps of size \(\delta\), left or right with equal probability, every \(\tau\) seconds. After step \(N\): \(x_N = x_{N-1} \pm \delta\). Square it and average over many walkers: the cross-term \(\pm 2\delta x_{N-1}\) averages to zero (that is what "random" means), leaving

eq. 1.2.3 $$\langle x_N^2\rangle = \langle x_{N-1}^2\rangle + \delta^2 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \langle x^2\rangle = N\delta^2 = \frac{\delta^2}{\tau}\,t$$

Stop and look at what multiplies \(t\): the combination \(\delta^2/\tau\) — step size squared per step time. Every microscopic detail of the walk enters through this one number. A walker taking a million tiny, rapid steps and one taking a few large, slow steps spread identically if their \(\delta^2/\tau\) match — at any magnification you can actually watch, the microscopic details are unrecoverable and only this combination survives. A quantity that (i) is all that matters and (ii) hides its origins deserves its own name and symbol. This — finally — is the diffusion coefficient you have been using since the constants box:

eq. 1.2.4 — definition $$D \;\equiv\; \frac{\delta^2}{2\tau} \qquad \left[\frac{\mathrm{m^2}}{\mathrm{s}}\right]$$

Two honest remarks about the definition. The units are area per time, not a speed — a diffuser has no speed (2.4 makes this precise); it has a rate of covering territory. And the factor of 2 is pure bookkeeping convention: physics fixed \(\delta^2/\tau\); we split off a 2 so that this \(D\) will match, with no stray factors, the \(D\) that appears in Fick's flux law next week. With the name in place, the law of the walk reads:

eq. 1.2.5 $$\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt \qquad\text{(one dimension)}$$

2.4 · Where is the particle? What \(\sqrt{2Dt}\) actually tells you

Section 2.3 handed you a formula, and it is dangerously easy to misread. Write it the usual way, \(x_{rms} = \sqrt{N}\,\delta = \sqrt{2Dt}\), and it looks like a position — plug in a time, get out a distance, done. It is not a position. You cannot compute where the particle ended up, because the walk is random: two identical particles released together end up in different places, every single time. So what is \(x_{rms}\)? It is the width of a probability cloud — the standard deviation of all the places the particle could be.

That forces two different readings, and you must know which one you are using:

  • One particle: "where is it?" has no answer. The honest question is "with what probability is it within a distance \(r\) of the start?" — and that the theory answers exactly. On a 2D microscope image the answer is \(P(r \le \sqrt{4Dt}) = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 63\%\) (in 1D, \(\pm\sqrt{2Dt}\) holds ≈ 68%). The ring is a betting line, not a leash — watch the single particle below wander out of it and back.
  • Many particles: probability becomes a census. Release 1000 molecules from the bite and you cannot name any one molecule's position — but you can say with confidence that about 630 of them are inside the \(\sqrt{4Dt}\) ring right now. Individually lawless, collectively bookkeepable: this is the same many-particle magic that turned wall drumming into steady pressure in Tab 1.

And the bridge between the two readings is the deepest fact in this tab: one particle re-run many times builds exactly the cloud that many particles build at once. The third mode below deposits a dot wherever the lone particle happens to be at each restart — and dot by dot, Pearson's cloud assembles itself. When HW1 hands you a handful of single-particle tracks, this is why averaging them is legitimate.

Deeper: two "most probable" statements that sound contradictory

Pearson's answer — "the most probable place to find the walker is where he started" — is about position: the 2D cloud is densest at the origin. But the most probable distance from the origin is not zero: there is vanishing area within tiny radii, so the ring histogram below peaks at \(r = \sqrt{2Dt}\) (the Rayleigh peak), not at \(r=0\). Densest at the center, yet typically found a ring away — both true, and the radial histogram shows them at once.

  2. a thousand: the census view. Read the live fraction inside the ring — ~63% at every instant, forever, while every individual particle disobeys.
  3. one particle, restarted: let the endpoints accumulate. The lone particle's history converges onto the thousand-particle cloud — the ensemble IS the replay.

Tilt the cloud: the Boltzmann distribution

Everything above described a free cloud. Nothing pulls the walkers, so no direction is cheaper than any other and the probability spreads out symmetrically. Now ask the question §2.2 deliberately left open: what happens to that probability distribution when the landscape is not flat — when moving one way costs energy?

Two tempting answers are both wrong. The cloud does not collapse into the energy minimum — kicks keep lofting particles back up, exactly as §2.2's column showed. And it does not ignore the energy either — the column is visibly denser at the bottom. What settles is a compromise, and statistical mechanics prices it exactly: each position's probability is discounted by an exponential in its energy cost, with \(k_BT\) as the exchange rate (the same currency Tab 1 established):

eq. 1.2.7 $$C(h) = C_0\, e^{-U(h)/k_BT}$$

This is the Boltzmann distribution, and read it the way this section has trained you to read everything: as a probability statement. \(C(h)/C_0\) is the relative likelihood of finding a particle where the energy cost is \(U(h)\) — never a promise about any one particle, exactly like the ring. One \(k_BT\) of climb makes a state \(e \approx 2.7\)× rarer; two \(k_BT\), 7.4× rarer; ten \(k_BT\), 22,000× rarer. That steepness is why a molecular event costing tens of \(k_BT\) essentially never happens by accident, and why a cell that needs such an event builds an enzyme to pay the bill instead of waiting for luck.

And now §2.2's histogram explains itself. There \(U = \Delta\rho V g h\) is linear in height, so \(e^{-U/k_BT}\) is a plain exponential in \(h\) whose 1/e height is \(k_BT/\Delta\rho V g\) — precisely the sedimentation length you measured. The sinking column was showing you eq. 1.2.7 all along; you just did not have the language for it yet.

Two distributions, one tab. The Gaussian cloud of §2.3–2.4 is what randomness does with no force: it spreads, \(\sqrt{2Dt}\) wide. Boltzmann's exponential is what randomness does against a force: it settles, \(k_BT/F\) tall. Keep eq. 1.2.7 in your pocket — Tab 3 puts spreading and settling in the same box and makes them fight, and that standoff is where the Stokes–Einstein relation comes from.

2.6 · The drunkard goes home

We have been asking "how far does a random walker get?" Biology usually asks the opposite question, and it is much harder: how long until the walker finds one particular place? A transcription factor must find one 10-base-pair site in a whole genome. A neurotransmitter must find one receptor. An antibody must find one epitope. None of them can steer.

Pearson's drunkard makes the question concrete. He leaves the bar with no sense of direction, staggers along the street grid, and keeps going until he happens to stumble onto his own front door. He is not lost in the sense of being far away — he is usually quite close. He simply has no way to aim.

3.1 · The bargain, watched — one bath, two jobs

Tabs 1 and 2 leave you one step short. You can price molecular events in \(k_BT\), and you can turn a diffusion coefficient into a travel time — but nobody has told you where \(D\) itself comes from. Every number you used was handed to you from a table. This tab replaces the table with a formula, and the whole formula grows out of one picture. Here it is.

Put a big particle back into Tab 1's mosh pit and watch what the collisions do to it. If the particle is sitting still, the hammering arrives evenly from all sides: the kicks nearly cancel, and the leftover imbalance is the jiggle of Tab 2, measured by \(D\). Now push it. Nothing about the bath changes — but a moving particle runs into more molecules on its front face than its back, and harder. The same kicks, re-counted from a moving seat, sum to a net backward force: drag.

The kicks ARE the resistance. There are not two forces in this tab — there is one hail of collisions and two ways of adding it up. Parked, the sum wanders around zero: fluctuation. Moving, the sum points backward: dissipation. This identity is the tab's entire working assumption, and it holds because inertia is out of the game: at the cell scale \(Re \sim 10^{-9}\) and a particle's momentum memory dies in nanoseconds (3.2), so the collision bookkeeping IS the whole force story, instant by instant. Think of honey — what makes it hard to stir (molecules constantly in your way) is exactly what calms the jiggling of anything suspended in it. One cause, two ledgers: each must be computable from the other.

First, the tab's central character: mobility

When you push something at this scale it does not accelerate and coast — it instantly moves at a speed proportional to the push (section 3.2 shows why inertia is out of the game). That proportionality constant is the mobility \(\mu\): how much speed you get per unit of force.

eq. 1.3.1 $$v_{drift} = \mu F \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mu \equiv \frac{v_{drift}}{F} \qquad \left(\ \zeta \equiv \frac{1}{\mu}\ \text{is the same fact stated as a price}\right)$$

Everyday version: a shopping cart has high mobility — a small push produces real speed. A sofa on carpet has low mobility. For a molecule, \(\mu\) is measured exactly the way it is defined: grab it (an optical tweezer really does this — HW1 hands you one), pull with a known force \(F\), read the drift speed \(v\), divide. Big or sticky → low \(\mu\). Small or slippery → high \(\mu\). Nothing about randomness yet — mobility is a plain, deterministic property, like stiffness.

Then, the claim to test

The jiggle you already own from Tab 2: \(D\), read from the slope of \(\langle r^2\rangle\). Einstein's 1905 claim — the bargain — is that because the kicks and the resistance are the same collisions, thermal energy must convert one into the other:

eq. 1.3.2 — the claim, proved in 3.3 $$D \;\overset{?}{=}\; \mu\,k_BT$$

Read it as a sentence: the wandering a particle does for free equals its willingness to move when pushed (\(\mu\)), times the push the bath itself supplies (\(k_BT\)). If true, it means you can predict a particle's spontaneous wandering from a purely mechanical pulling experiment — two things that look unrelated.

Meet the teams

The box below is Tab 1's bath plus two teams of big tracer particles — identical in every way except what we do to them:

  • The purple team is left alone. Pure Tab-2 physics: the bath kicks them, we log \(\langle r^2\rangle\), and the slope gives \(D\). They are the "free wander" measurement.
  • The orange team gets pulled with a tiny steady force — half to the right and half to the left, so the box as a whole feels no net shove. Each settles at a steady drift, and \(\mu = v_{drift}/F\). They are the "pushed" measurement.

Same bath, same size, same everything — the only difference between the teams is the force. That is what makes this one clean experiment: any difference in their behavior is purely the push's doing.

How to read the check stat

The starred readout, \(D/(\mu\,k_BT)\), divides what the purple team did (measured wander) by what the orange team says they should have done (willingness × thermal budget). The units cancel — it is a pure number — and it is the entire experiment in one figure: 1.00 means the kicks and the drag really are one phenomenon. Any other stable value would mean jiggle and drag were separate pieces of physics that merely coexist. Watch it wander toward 1 with honest ±20% noise; nothing in the code enforces the answer.

Fine print for the honest: tracers that leave one side re-enter on the other (so a pull can run forever — the bookkeeping keeps their true path); the bath is gently re-thermostatted because a steady pull does work on the box; tracers ignore each other and collide only with the bath. And the convergence is slow and wobbly on purpose — these are single-particle statistics, exactly section 2.4's lesson.

  1. In both mode, read the right-hand chart: the free team's \(\langle r^2\rangle\) climbs as a diffusion curve while the pulled team's \(\langle x\rangle\) climbs as a straight line. Same bath, two clean experiments.
  2. Watch the check stat \(D/(\mu k_BT)\) wander toward 1 (±20% is honest — section 2.4 again). No formula told the box to do this.
  3. Heat it up. The jiggle strengthens and the hotter bath drags harder — both measurements move, the ratio stays at 1. That refusal to budge is what "the same collisions" means.
  4. Crank the pull force. Too hard a pull outruns the thermal picture (you are towing, not probing) and the ratio sags — a real experimental lesson: probe gently.

3.2 · Mobility has a law: \(\mu = 1/6\pi\eta a\)

Section 3.1's box measured a mobility. To use the bargain on a real molecule you need to know what sets \(\mu\) in a real fluid — and the answer starts with how strange the cell-scale world is. Inertia, the thing that makes a thrown ball keep going, is completely irrelevant there. The Reynolds number \(Re = \rho v a/\eta\) compares inertia to viscosity: for you swimming, \(Re \sim 10^{6}\); for a 2-nm protein drifting at 1 µm/s, \(Re \approx 2\times10^{-9}\). E. M. Purcell's famous 1977 lecture Life at low Reynolds number runs the numbers for a swimming bacterium: turn the flagellum off and it coasts about a tenth of an ångström — less than one atom — before viscosity eats all its momentum. No gliding, no follow-through: velocity exists only while a force is applied, which is precisely why "speed per unit force" is the right variable at this scale.

For a sphere at low \(Re\), Stokes (1851) solved the resistance exactly. It is linear — a genuine mobility — and it depends on just two things, the fluid's viscosity and the sphere's radius:

eq. 1.3.3 $$v = \mu F, \quad \mu = \frac{1}{6\pi\eta a} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F_{drag} = \zeta v, \quad \zeta = 6\pi\eta a$$

Two things deserve a stare. First, the resistance grows like \(a\), not like the cross-sectional area \(a^2\) — at low \(Re\) the sphere drags along a halo of fluid much bigger than itself, and the halo scales with radius. Second, mass appears nowhere: the race below is pure geometry and viscosity. (And one honesty note: 3.1's toy bath resists by discrete collisions; a real liquid's mobility law is this hydrodynamic one — same physics, smoother bookkeeping.)

Worked example— lecture Q5, first half: what force does a vesicle cost?
  1. Gather the knowns. A motor protein hauls a vesicle of radius \(a = 100\ \mathrm{nm} = 10^{-7}\) m through water (\(\eta = 1.0\ \mathrm{mPa\,s} = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}\)) at \(v = 1\ \mu\mathrm{m/s} = 10^{-6}\) m/s. Wanted: the force \(F\) the motor must supply just to beat drag.
  2. Chain the two tools. Mobility relates speed to force (3.1); Stokes tells us the mobility from geometry (eq. 1.3.3). Combine them symbolically before any numbers:
    $$v = \mu F \quad\text{and}\quad \mu = \frac{1}{6\pi\eta a} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F = \frac{v}{\mu} = 6\pi\eta a\, v$$
  3. Evaluate the drag price ζ = 1/μ.
    $$\zeta = 6\pi\eta a = 6\pi\,(10^{-3})(10^{-7}) = 1.9\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{N\,s/m}$$
  4. Multiply by the speed.
    $$F = \zeta v = (1.9\times10^{-9})(10^{-6}) = 1.9\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{N} \approx 0.002\ \mathrm{pN}$$
  5. Compare to something real. A kinesin motor stalls near 5–6 pN — about 2500× more than this job needs.

Deeper: how long does "no inertia" take to become true?

3.3 · Why the bargain is forced: \(D = \mu k_BT\)

You have measured the bargain (3.1) and you have the law for mobility (3.2). What is left is the best part: showing that equilibrium forces \(D = \mu k_BT\) — no other value is even possible. The argument is Einstein's in 1905, it is three steps long, and every ingredient is already yours.

Step 1 — demand equilibrium. Go back to section 2.2's water column — you already own this experiment. A steady force \(F\) points down, so sitting at height \(h\) costs potential energy \(U(h) = F h\), exactly the \(U = \Delta\rho V g\,h\) of the sedimentation column with the force generalized.

Generalized to what? To every constant force biology and the lab actually use — you have met each one. Gravity, for free: Perrin won a Nobel Prize watching resin beads settle into this exact profile. A centrifuge: gravity turned up a thousandfold, which is how even small proteins are made to "pile up." An electric field on anything charged: electrophoresis — every gel you will ever run is this experiment. An optical tweezer pulling one bead at constant force: HW1's tweezer experiment is literally this section. The sim below keeps the force pointing down, like 2.2, because it IS 2.2 — the same settling column, revisited now that you own \(\mu\) and \(D\).

And you already know what the settled state must look like — you measured it in 2.2 and named it in 2.4. States that cost energy are exponentially rarer, at exchange rate \(k_BT\):

eq. 1.2.7 again — same law, general U $$C(h) = C_0\, e^{-U(h)/k_BT}$$

Step 2 — name the two currents. The force settles particles downward at the mobility speed; the pile at the floor leaks diffusively back up (this leak term is Fick's first law, borrowed on credit — next week it gets its own derivation):

eq. 1.3.4 $$J_{drift} = -\,C\,\mu\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial h}$$
eq. 1.3.5 $$J_{diff} = -\,D\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial h}$$

Step 3 — make them cancel, line by line. Equilibrium means zero net current everywhere: \(J_{drift} + J_{diff} = 0\). Watch how eq. 1.2.7 and eq. 1.3.4–1.3.5 combine. First differentiate eq. 1.2.7 — chain rule, with \(C_0\) and \(k_BT\) constants:

eq. 1.3.6 $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial h} = C_0\,e^{-U/k_BT}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{k_BT}\frac{\partial U}{\partial h}\right) = -\,\frac{C}{k_BT}\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial h}$$

Put that into the diffusion current:

eq. 1.3.7 $$J_{diff} = -\,D\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial h} = +\,\frac{D}{k_BT}\,C\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial h}$$

Add the two currents and demand zero:

eq. 1.3.8 $$J_{drift}+J_{diff} = \left(\frac{D}{k_BT} - \mu\right) C\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial h} \;=\; 0$$

\(C\) is not zero, and the tilt \(\partial U/\partial h\) is not zero — so the bracket must be. Nothing survives except the bargain, and Stokes's mobility from 3.2 finishes it:

eq. 1.3.9 $$D = \mu\,k_BT \;=\; \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta a}$$

This is the Stokes–Einstein relation — the whole of Week 1 in one line. \(k_BT\) on top: everything diffuses, because thermal energy is free and universal. \(6\pi\eta a\) underneath: size and stickiness set the price. It returns for chemoreception (Week 3), oxygen delivery (Week 11), and the tumor microenvironment (Week 12). And note what it explains: 3.1's box landed on \(D/(\mu k_BT) = 1\) not by luck but because no equilibrium bath can do otherwise. The widget below lets you try to build one that does.

Before you throw the switch: what "breaking Einstein" would mean

The sim below has a control reality does not offer: a Break Einstein toggle that unslaves \(D\) from \(\mu k_BT\). The particles keep their honest mobility — they still settle at \(\mu F\) — but they diffuse with whatever \(D\) you dial in. What happens? The column still settles (any drift-vs-diffusion contest finds a standoff), but into the wrong exponential: the pile's 1/e height becomes \(\zeta D/F\) instead of \(k_BT/F\). Read that height as a thermometer and the profile claims the box sits at \(T_{\mathrm{eff}} = \zeta D/k_B\) — while the molecular speeds still say \(T\). One box, two thermometers, two readings: you could run an engine off the disagreement forever, and the second law forbids it. That contradiction is the toggle's whole point — the only value of \(D\) that makes the two thermometers agree is \(\mu k_BT\). When you flip the switch, watch the effective-temperature strip above the histogram split away from the bath.

  4. Slide the radius \(a\) up (with Einstein intact): \(\mu\) falls, so \(D\) falls with it — and the profile does not change, because eq. 1.2.7 has no \(a\) in it. Only the time to settle changes.

Fick's laws — you have already used one

Encounters — Smoluchowski's question

Marian Smoluchowski (1916–17) asked: if two particles diffuse randomly, how long until they meet? His answer is the diffusion-limited rate constant, and it sets the ceiling on how fast any reaction in a cell can run — enzyme–substrate, transcription factor–DNA, neurotransmitter–receptor:

eq. 1.5.1 $$k_s = 4\pi (D_1 + D_2)(a_1 + a_2)$$

Two riddles to bring with you: why do the diffusion coefficients add? And why do the radii add? Both have one-sentence answers that will feel obvious once you see them — come Tuesday.
