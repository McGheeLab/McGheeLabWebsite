The Microscope — one rig, three slides, one afternoon

The neutrophil in the infected bite depends on three molecular parts: a receptor that reads the chemokine, an actin that builds the front, a myosin that pulls the rear. Before asking whether the cell can find the wound (the Problems), characterize the parts. Your lab has one TIRF microscope and three slides in the box: the receptor slide (the neutrophil's chemokine receptor in a supported membrane, and a vendor's proprietary chemokine analogue whose binding rates are undisclosed), the actin slide (one filament growing from a seed; actin from a proprietary expression line, kinetics undisclosed) and the myosin slide (filaments gliding over a lawn of the same lab's myosin II; its ATP dependence undisclosed).

The instrument. A TIRF microscope (total internal reflection fluorescence) sends its light along the coverslip at a glancing angle, so that only a sheet about 100 nm thick right at the glass is illuminated and everything above it stays dark. Fluorescent molecules that are bound to the surface glow; the same molecules a micron away in solution do not. That is why it can watch single ligands land on a membrane, single filaments grow, and single motors move — dynamics that an ordinary microscope drowns in out-of-focus glow.

Every slide is run the same way.

  1. Set one dial — a concentration.
  2. Run — the instrument records until the event you can see happen (a plateau, a length change, a finish line) and hands you the raw numbers for that run.
  3. Keep the run if you judge it usable (tag it) — it goes into your table.
  4. Repeat at new settings until the strip is lit: five usable runs, four different settings, with the feature bracketed — the strip says which side you still need.
  5. Answer the two questions under the strip, from your sweep.
The instrument never reports a rate constant, a KD, a critical concentration, a KM or a maximum speed. It reports what a camera can see — an occupancy, a time to a crossing, a length per second, a speed. Turning a sweep of those into the quantity asked for, including which relation justifies it, is yours to find ( Notes lists every candidate). Your sweep plot lets you choose the axes; a straight line on the right axes is the discovery.

THE PROBLEMS

Challenge — the walk to the wound graded for 566 · everyone should attempt

The situation. A splinter has carried bacteria into the dermis, and they have founded a colony — a patch of dividing cells whose number doubles every \(t_{double}\) (printed below). The nearest capillary runs a distance \(L_c\) away. Neutrophils leave the capillary there and set off through the tissue. Watch one of them. It crawls at a steady speed \(v_{cell}\), but it does not hold a heading: averaged over many such cells, the velocity relaxes toward zero as \(d\langle\mathbf v\rangle/dt = -\langle\mathbf v\rangle/\tau_p\), so a cell remembers its direction for about \(\tau_p\) and then wanders. There is no gradient to steer it in this problem.

The question: how long does a wandering neutrophil take to reach the colony — and does it get there before the colony has doubled?

Work it entirely by hand, in your written solutions: nothing on this tab is recorded, and your exported results file carries no challenge fields. Let \(G(t) = \langle r^2\rangle\) be the cell’s mean-squared displacement from the point where it left the capillary, and let \(H(t) = \langle \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\rangle\). Your written solution must do three things:

  1. Set up the differential equation that \(G\) obeys. A way in: write \(dG/dt\) in terms of \(H\); then write \(dH/dt\) with the product rule, using \(\langle\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v\rangle = v_{cell}^2\) and the relaxation law above (the random part of the cell’s turning is uncorrelated with where it is); eliminate \(H\). You should arrive at a second-order linear equation for \(G\) with constant coefficients. Then decide, and justify from the physics, the conditions at \(t = 0\) that pin down its solution.
  2. Solve it, and show what \(G\) becomes for \(t \ll \tau_p\) and for \(t \gg \tau_p\). Name the quantity that multiplies \(t\) in the second limit.
  3. Estimate the time at which the cell’s root-mean-square displacement first equals \(L_c\). Report it in minutes, check which limit it falls in, and compare it with the doubling time and with the time a straight crawl at \(v_{cell}\) would take.

Notes — equations & when they apply

Notes — every law of Weeks 1–4 this homework can use, and when each one applies

This sheet is your field guide. No question in this homework names its equation — you match the situation to the law. For each entry: what it says, the assumptions it stands on, and what breaks it. Nothing here is graded and nothing is hidden. Equation numbers are the weekly modules' (module.tab.order), so you can open the derivation any time.

How to search this sheet. Every law here is one door — a molecule arrives at a rate proportional to concentration, sticks with some probability, and leaves at a rate that does not care about concentration — dressed for a different stage. Ask three questions: what is arriving, what is leaving, and what does the stage count (occupancy? length? distance? power?). Then read the "when it applies" lines against your answers. When two laws give the same quantity two ways and agree, that agreement is your confidence — no answer key needed.

Notation (the course's, LEARNING-MODULE-SPEC §16): \(C\) concentration (number per volume unless a unit says otherwise) · \(a\) radius of a particle or cell · \(a_s\) radius of a site or receptor pocket · \(k_s\) encounter (arrival) rate constant · \(P\) sticking probability per arrival · \(k_{on} \equiv P\,k_s\) association rate constant · \(k_{off}\) dissociation rate · \(k_{cat}\) turnover rate · \(N_s\) number of sites · \(\theta\) fractional occupancy · \(K_{eq} = k_{on}/k_{off}\) · \(t_{avg}\) averaging time · \(\delta\) subunit rise · \(\tau_p\) persistence time.

Moving through fluid — Stokes drag and Einstein's bargain (eq. 1.3.3, 1.3.9)

$$\zeta = 6\pi\eta\,a \qquad\quad v = \frac{F}{\zeta} \qquad\quad D = \frac{k_BT}{\zeta} = \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta\,a}$$
  • Symbolsζ = drag coefficient · η = viscosity (Pa·s) · a = hydrodynamic radius of the moving object · kBT = the thermal energy (constants below).
  • What it saysDrag is linear in speed and scales with the object's radius; the same ζ that resists pulling sets how far thermal kicks scatter it. Runs forward (size → D) or backward (D → size).
  • When it appliesLow Reynolds number, a rigid sphere, a Newtonian fluid, far from walls. a viscosity given as “N times as viscous as water” means N times water’s viscosity at your temperature.
  • Fails whenThe radius you feed it is not the size of the thing that is swimming — a lattice spacing, a step length or a diameter is not a hydrodynamic radius. Work in SI (D in m²/s, η in Pa·s, a in m) and convert at the end; a nm dropped into a µm slot is a factor of 1000.

Random walks — the mean-squared displacement (eq. 1.2.6, 1.2.8)

$$\langle r^2\rangle = 2\,d\,D\,t \qquad (d = \text{dimensions the walker explores: } 2Dt,\ 4Dt,\ 6Dt) \qquad\qquad t \approx \frac{L^2}{2dD}$$
  • What it saysA diffusing thing covers area, not distance, at a steady rate: typical reach grows like √t, and the time to cover a distance L grows like L².
  • When it appliesFree, unbiased steps that have forgotten their direction; times long compared with whatever sets one step. Count the dimensions the walker actually explores — a cell on a surface has two, a molecule in cytoplasm three.
  • Fails whenSuccessive steps remember each other — a walker that keeps its heading is ballistic, not diffusive, until it forgets (the persistent random walk, below). The 4-vs-6 prefactor is the quietest expensive slip in this unit.

Encounters — the diffusion-limited ceiling (eq. 1.5.1)

$$k_s = 4\pi\,(D_1{+}D_2)\,(a_1{+}a_2) \qquad \text{arrivals per second} = k_s\,C \qquad \text{(one partner fixed: } k_s = 4\pi D a\text{)}$$
  • SymbolsD1, D2 = the two partners' diffusion coefficients · a1, a2 = their radii · C = concentration in molecules per volume (the bridge, below).
  • What it saysThe fastest any sticky target can be found, set by how quickly diffusion delivers searchers — proportional to radius, not area. It is the ceiling on every rate constant in this sheet: nothing binds faster than it is delivered. Comparing a measured kon with ks tells you what fraction of arrivals stick.
  • When it appliesSteady state, dilute searchers, every arrival captured — a deliberate upper bound. Counting is Poisson: N arrivals carry √N of noise.
  • Fails whenA surface is only partly sticky (the patchy-sphere factor, below) or the searchers deplete each other. Use the radius of the searcher, not the step it makes when it lands; and the units are volume per time — µm³/s — so C must be in molecules per µm³ before the two are multiplied.

The concentration bridge — nM and µM into molecules per µm³

$$1\ \mathrm{nM} = 0.6022\ \mathrm{molecules/\mu m^3} \qquad 1\ \mathrm{\mu M} = 602.2\ \mathrm{molecules/\mu m^3} \qquad 1\ \mathrm{\mu M^{-1}s^{-1}} = \frac{1}{602.2}\ \mathrm{\mu m^3/s} = 1.66\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{\mu m^3/s}$$
  • What it saysDerive it once: 10⁻⁹ mol/L × 6.022 × 10²³ molecules/mol ÷ 10¹⁵ µm³ per liter. Every rate constant with a concentration in its units crosses this bridge — a kon in µm³/s and a kon in µM⁻¹s⁻¹ are the same number in two currencies, and a ratio koff/kon in molecules/µm³ is a concentration you can quote in nM.
  • When it appliesWhenever a law wants molecules per volume and your data speaks molar — which is always.
  • Fails whenCrossed backwards. The fingerprints: an answer off by exactly ×0.60 or ×1.66 crossed once the wrong way; ×0.36 or ×2.76 crossed twice; ×602 or ×1/602 forgot the µM bridge entirely. Check the fingerprint before you check the physics.

Fick's first law, and the arrival flux at a surface (eq. 1.3.5, 3.1.1, 2.3.5)

$$J = -D\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x} \qquad\qquad J_{arr} = \frac{D\,C}{\delta}\ \ \text{(a wall behind a depleted layer } \delta\text{)} \qquad\qquad J(a) = \frac{D\,C_\infty}{a}\ \ \text{(the surface of an adsorbing sphere)}$$
  • SymbolsJ = flux (molecules per area per time) · δ = thickness of the layer the surface has emptied · C∞ = concentration far away. D/δ and D/a are effective delivery speeds (the mass-transfer coefficient, eq. 3.3.8).
  • What it saysA held difference across a layer drives a steady, linear delivery. Multiply the sphere's flux by its area 4πa² and you recover 4πDaC — eq. 1.5.1 from the delivery side.
  • When it appliesSteady state (the profile has relaxed, ~L²/D), no reactions inside the layer, D constant across it.
  • Fails whenThe surface does not consume what it receives — then the layer is not depleted and there is no gradient to drive J. Units are inherited from whatever you feed it: commit to one system first.

What an association rate constant is made of — kon ≡ P ks (eq. 3.1.4)

$$s + p \;\xrightarrow{\;k_{on}\;}\; sp, \qquad k_{on} = \frac{k_r\,k_s}{k_r + k_s} = P\,k_s, \qquad k_s = 4\pi D a_s,\quad P = \frac{k_r}{k_r + k_s}$$
  • Symbolskr = the pocket's intrinsic (chemical) rate · ks = the delivery to one pocket of radius as · P = the fraction of arrivals that stick.
  • What it saysArrive × stick. The units are volume per time — the rate at which one site clears ligand out of the volume around it, delivery already discounted. Fast chemistry (kr ≫ ks): P → 1, kon → ks, diffusion-limited. Slow chemistry: kon → kr, reaction-limited.
  • When it appliesAn isolated pocket in a dilute, well-mixed bath; the tabulated kon of biochemistry is this number.
  • Fails whenPockets are packed so densely that they compete for the same depleted layer (then the whole surface, not the pocket, is the adsorber — see the patchy sphere and the Damköhler number below).

The Langmuir door — the rate law, Keq, and the isotherm (eq. 3.2.4, 3.2.7, 3.2.8)

$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = k_{on}\,C\,(1-\theta) - k_{off}\,\theta \qquad\quad K_{eq} = \frac{k_{on}}{k_{off}}\ \ (\text{a volume; its reciprocal is a concentration — biochemists call that } K_D) \qquad\quad \theta_{eq} = \frac{K_{eq}\,C}{1 + K_{eq}\,C}$$
  • Symbolsθ = fraction of sites occupied · C = free-ligand concentration at the sites · Keq in µm³ when kon is in µm³/s and koff in s⁻¹.
  • What it saysArrivals fill open sites at konC; bound molecules leave at koff. At equilibrium the two balance and the isotherm follows: linear at KeqC ≪ 1, exactly half full where KeqC = 1 — that is, at C = 1/Keq — and saturating beyond. The midpoint is the ratio of the two rates, so the same midpoint can be reached by a slow door or a fast, leaky one.
  • When it appliesIdentical, independent sites; one ligand per site; reversible binding; ligand dilute enough that binding does not deplete it; long enough for the transient to die (below). A reading is useful within about a decade either side of the midpoint.
  • Fails whenSites talk to each other (cooperativity — Hill, below) or the bound state has a second exit (Michaelis–Menten, below). And Keq is not the half-coverage concentration — its reciprocal is; reporting one for the other is the commonest inversion in this unit.

How fast the door settles — the relaxation time (eq. 3.2.5, 3.2.9, 3.3.13)

$$\theta(t) = \theta_{eq} + (\theta_0 - \theta_{eq})\,e^{-(k_{on}C + k_{off})\,t} \qquad\quad \tau = \frac{1}{k_{on}C + k_{off}} = \frac{1-\theta_{eq}}{k_{off}} \qquad\quad \tau_{1/2} = \tau\ln 2$$
  • What it saysFilling and emptying share one clock: the sum of the two rates. Filling toward a plateau reaches 63 % of it at t = τ; emptying into ligand-free medium falls to 37 % at t = 1/koff — the wash knows only koff, the rise knows both. Plot 1/τ against C and you have a straight line whose slope and intercept are the two rate constants.
  • When it appliesA step in C held constant afterwards; sites independent; C not depleted by the binding.
  • Fails whenτ is read as 1/koff at finite C — it is shorter by the factor 1 + KeqC. τ is not the half-time (that is τ ln 2), and "the transient is done" means three or four τ, not one.

Cooperative doors — the Hill isotherm (eq. 3.2.11, 3.2.12, 3.3.5)

$$\theta = \frac{(K_{eq}C)^{\,n}}{1 + (K_{eq}C)^{\,n}} \qquad\quad \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta} = n\log C + n\log K_{eq} \qquad\quad \frac{C_{90}}{C_{10}} = 81^{1/n}$$
  • What it saysn = 1 is Langmuir. n > 1 is a steeper switch centred at the same midpoint; the Hill plot's slope is n. Going from 10 % to 90 % of a saturating response takes an 81-fold change in C for a plain door, less for a cooperative one.
  • When it appliesSites whose occupancy changes their neighbours' affinity; n is an empirical steepness, not a count of sites.
  • Fails whenForced onto data that a plain hyperbola already explains — an n ≠ 1 fitted to one door is noise dressed as mechanism. Read the 81-fold for what it is: the range of a plain switch, not a rule about half-maximal points.

The door with a third exit — Michaelis–Menten and KM (eq. 3.2.13–3.2.15)

$$s + p \rightleftharpoons sp \xrightarrow{\;k_{cat}\;} s + q \qquad\quad v = \frac{V_{max}\,C}{K_M + C}, \qquad K_M \equiv \frac{k_{off} + k_{cat}}{k_{on}}, \quad V_{max} \equiv k_{cat}N_s$$
  • What it saysLangmuir with one more way to empty a site. The rate saturates like the isotherm: linear at C ≪ KM, half of maximum at C = KM, flat at Vmax. Any saturating rate — an enzyme's turnover, a motor's cycle rate on its fuel, a gliding speed against ATP — reads the same way, and its midpoint is the substrate concentration at exactly half the plateau. Reaching a fraction f of the plateau needs C = KM·f/(1 − f): 90 % needs 9 KM, not 0.9 KM.
  • When it appliesSteady state of the bound fraction while the substrate is held (initial-rate conditions); the plateau is set by kcat alone and knows nothing about the bond. Linearizations (1/v vs 1/C; C/v vs C) turn a sweep into a straight line whose slope and intercept are the two parameters.
  • Fails whenSubstrate is being eaten during the measurement, or product inhibits. KM ≥ 1/Keq always — the half-rate point sits above the half-occupancy point by the ratio of the two exits.

Reading a concentration — the decision band and the counting floor (eq. 3.3.3, 3.3.12–3.3.15, 2.4.7)

$$\frac{\delta C}{C} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_s\,\theta(1-\theta)}}\ \ \text{(one glance)} \qquad\qquad \frac{\delta C}{C} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D a\,C\,t_{avg}}}\ \ \text{(a perfect adsorber, counting)}$$
$$\Bigl(\frac{\delta C}{C}\Bigr)^2 \approx \frac{2\tau_c}{t_{avg}}\cdot\frac{1}{N_s\,\theta(1-\theta)} = \frac{2}{N_s\,k_{off}\,\theta\,t_{avg}}, \qquad \tau_c = \frac{1}{k_{on}C + k_{off}} = \frac{1-\theta}{k_{off}} \qquad (t_{avg} \gg \tau_c)$$
$$\frac{(\delta C/C)^2_{receptors}}{(\delta C/C)^2_{adsorber}} = \frac{2}{(1-\theta)\,\mathrm{Da}},\quad \mathrm{Da} = \frac{N_s k_{on}}{4\pi D a} \qquad\qquad k_s = 4\pi D a\cdot\frac{N_{rec}\,a_s}{N_{rec}\,a_s + \pi a}\ \ \text{(a sphere with } N_{rec}\text{ receptors of radius } a_s\text{)}$$
  • SymbolsNs, Nrec = receptors on the cell · θ = their equilibrium occupancy at C (the isotherm) · tavg = how long the cell averages · τc = the population's correlation time · Da = the surface Damköhler number (eq. 3.3.9), capture rate over delivery rate.
  • What it saysOne glance at Ns receptors cannot resolve a relative change smaller than the first line. Looking longer buys tavg/2τc independent looks — but never better than the adsorber that counts every arrival, and a receptor reading sits above that floor by the reciprocal of Da. The patchy-sphere factor says why sparse receptors nearly suffice: a molecule that misses a receptor rattles in the depleted layer and tries again, so the correction is a ratio of lengths, not of areas — Nrec ≈ πa/as receptors already capture half of what a perfect adsorber would (Berg & Purcell’s papers say absorber; for a surface that binds, adsorber is the word, and the one this course uses).
  • When it appliesIndependent receptors at equilibrium with a steady C; the cell reads occupancy through the isotherm; tavg longer than τc for the averaged form. To resolve a fractional step ε, set δC/C equal to ε and solve for the time or the count.
  • Fails whenThe θ(1−θ) is dropped (the band is widest at saturation and at zero, narrowest at half coverage); a fraction is entered as a percent (ε² is then off by 10⁴); the adsorber floor is used for a cell that has receptors, or the reverse; as in nm is mixed with a in µm inside the patchy-sphere factor. The prefactors are model-dependent (Berg & Purcell's exact sphere gives 3/5π in place of 1/4π); the scalings are not.

A filament end is a door — growth speed and the critical concentration (eq. 4.1.1, 4.1.3)

$$v = \delta\,(k_{on}C - k_{off}) \qquad\qquad C_c = \frac{k_{off}}{k_{on}} = \frac{1}{K_{eq}} \qquad\qquad D_L = \frac{\delta^2(k_{on}C + k_{off})}{2}\ \ (\text{eq. 4.1.2, the length's wobble})$$
  • Symbolsδ = the rise per subunit — how much longer the filament gets when one subunit joins (constants below) · C = free monomer · v in length per time, or in subunits per time if δ is left out.
  • What it saysSubunits arrive at konC and leave at koff; the end grows when arrivals win. Speed against C is a straight line: its slope is kon, its intercept on the C axis is Cc, and below Cc the end shrinks. Each end of a filament has its own line.
  • When it appliesOne end, a well-mixed monomer pool at constant C, no load on the tip, subunits of one nucleotide state.
  • Fails whenThe rate at ONE concentration is called kon (it is konC − koff); the wrong length is used for δ — the two strands of the actin helix put subunits 5.5 nm apart along one strand, but the filament lengthens by 2.7 nm per subunit; the tip is loaded (then the arrival term carries a Boltzmann factor, eq. 4.5.1).

Two doors on one filament — the treadmilling balance, Css and J (eq. 4.1.4, 4.1.5)

$$\frac{dL}{dt} = \delta\bigl[(k_{on}^B + k_{on}^P)\,C - (k_{off}^B + k_{off}^P)\bigr] \qquad C_c^B = \frac{k_{off}^B}{k_{on}^B},\quad C_c^P = \frac{k_{off}^P}{k_{on}^P}$$
$$C_{ss} = \frac{k_{off}^B + k_{off}^P}{k_{on}^B + k_{on}^P}, \qquad J = k_{on}^B\,C_{ss} - k_{off}^B = k_{off}^P - k_{on}^P\,C_{ss}$$
  • SymbolsB, P = the two ends (barbed and pointed) · Css = the monomer concentration at which the whole filament holds its length · J = the number of subunits per second passing through it, joining at one end and leaving at the other (the subunit flux); J·δ is the speed of the belt.
  • What it saysSet the total length rate to zero and one concentration falls out, between the two ends' thresholds; there the barbed end grows exactly as fast as the pointed end shrinks. Anything that changes one end's rates — a protein that raises koffP, say — moves Css and J together, by this same balance.
  • When it appliesTwo ends sharing one free-monomer pool; CcP > CcB (which needs a nucleotide clock — without it the two thresholds coincide and J = 0).
  • Fails whenOne end's threshold is used where the balance point belongs, or the two ends' rates are added with the wrong signs. Write the balance symbolically before any number.

The ceiling on a tip — how many arrivals stick (eq. 1.5.1 with eq. 3.1.4)

$$k_s = 4\pi D_G\,a_G \quad(\mathrm{\mu m^3/s}) \;\times\; 602.2\ \mathrm{\mu m^{-3}\,\mu M^{-1}} \;\to\; \mathrm{\mu M^{-1}s^{-1}} \qquad\qquad P = \frac{k_{on}}{k_s}$$
  • What it saysThe same encounter ceiling as any binding site, with the monomer's own hydrodynamic radius aG and diffusion coefficient DG in the medium it swims in. The measured kon divided by the ceiling is the fraction of arrivals that stick — a ratio near 1 says delivery-limited, a ratio far below 1 says most arrivals bounce off.
  • When it appliesOne tip in a dilute monomer bath; the tip as an absorbing patch of about the monomer's size. Adding the tip's own radius to aG roughly doubles the ceiling — quote the order of magnitude, not the digit.
  • Fails whenkon and ks are in different currencies (one in µM⁻¹s⁻¹, the other in µm³/s — a factor 602); DG is taken in water when the monomer lives in cytoplasm; the subunit rise δ is used as the monomer's radius.

Motors — duty ratio, the work of a stroke, the power of one head, the load line (eq. 4.2.1–4.2.4)

$$r = \frac{t_{attached}}{t_{cycle}} \qquad\quad F_{step}\,d \le \Delta\mu_{ATP} \qquad\quad P = \frac{F\,d}{t_{cycle}} = F\,v \qquad\quad v = v_0\Bigl(1 - \frac{F}{F_{stall}}\Bigr),\ \ v_0 = d\,k_{cycle}$$
  • Symbolsr = fraction of the cycle a head spends attached · Fstep, d = the unitary force and working stroke · ΔµATP = free energy of one ATP hydrolysis (constants below) · kcycle = 1/tcycle, the head's turnover, which saturates with ATP exactly as eq. 3.2.15 says · Fstall = the load at which v → 0.
  • What it saysEach cycle banks F·d of work out of one ATP; multiply by cycles per second and you have the power of one head. Below saturating ATP the cycle rate — and so the unloaded speed and the power — falls by the Michaelis–Menten fraction [ATP]/(KM + [ATP]). Under load the speed falls linearly to zero at Fstall.
  • When it appliesOne head, cycling steadily; the load line is a one-spring stand-in (kinesin's measured line is not straight, eq. 4.3.5). A sliding filament over a lawn of heads reports the cycle rate directly when the duty ratio is small and the lawn is dense enough for continuous motion.
  • Fails whenpN·nm/s is not converted to watts (1 pN·nm/s = 10⁻²¹ W = 10⁻³ aW); the cycle rate at saturating ATP is used at an ATP concentration that is not saturating; single-molecule numbers for the stroke and force from different laboratories are mixed without noticing they disagree.

Road or diffusion — the Péclet number (eq. 4.1.8)

$$\mathrm{Pe} = \frac{v\,L}{D} = \frac{L^2/D}{L/v} = \frac{\text{time to diffuse } L}{\text{time to be carried } L}$$
  • What it saysA dimensionless referee for any transport question at scale L with a carrier speed v: Pe ≪ 1 and diffusion delivers first, the road is a luxury; Pe ≫ 1 and diffusion is a footnote, the road pays. It is a ratio of two transit times — write both down.
  • When it appliesThe cargo's own D (its radius, in the medium it sits in) against a carrier speed over a stated L. Pe is scale-dependent: quote the L you used.
  • Fails whenD is taken in water for a cargo that lives in cytoplasm; a diameter is used where a radius belongs (a factor 2 in D, and so in Pe); L is the cell when the trip is a fraction of it.

A cell as a walker — the persistent random walk and Dcell (eq. 4.3.6)

$$\langle r^2\rangle = 2v^2\tau_p^2\left(\frac{t}{\tau_p} - 1 + e^{-t/\tau_p}\right) \;\xrightarrow{\,t \ll \tau_p\,}\; v^2t^2 \qquad\quad \xrightarrow{\,t \gg \tau_p\,}\; 4D_{cell}\,t, \qquad D_{cell} = \frac{v^2\tau_p}{2}\ \ \text{(two dimensions)}$$
  • Symbolsv = speed along the path · τp = persistence time, the time over which the velocity direction decorrelates (exponentially) · vτp = the persistence length · rrms = √⟨r²⟩.
  • What it saysA walker that keeps its heading for τp is ballistic at short times (distance ∝ t) and diffusive at long times (distance ∝ √t), with a diffusion coefficient built from its speed and its persistence — eq. 1.2.6 for a "particle" the size of a cell. The regime at any time t is decided by t/τp; the regime at any distance L by L/(vτp).
  • When it appliesAn unbiased walker in two dimensions (a cell on a surface), speed constant, heading memory exponential. Add a bias and the mean displacement grows as v·CI·t with a chemotactic index CI between 0 and 1.
  • Fails whenThe long-time form is used at t ≲ τp (or the short-time form at t ≫ τp) — check the regime first; a speed in µm/min is multiplied by a time in seconds; the 2 in v²τp/2 is dropped, or 6Dt is used for a walker confined to a plane.

Statistics toolbox — how to treat your sweep

  • The line, not one runA rate constant is the slope of a sweep, never one run divided by its setting. Keep runs on both sides of the feature you are after — a midpoint, a zero crossing, a half-maximum — because a line fitted to one side of it extrapolates, and extrapolations are where the errors hide.
  • Average raw, then convert⟨f(x)⟩ ≠ f(⟨x⟩): average the raw readings at each setting first, then push the average through the law. Converting run-by-run and averaging after inflates skewed quantities (a mean of reciprocals is not the reciprocal of a mean).
  • Tag honestlyA run that barely left the noise, or ended before its plateau, is data you keep and mark — not data you delete. The judgment is part of the measurement; only the runs you call usable feed the line.
  • Uncertainty is spread you can defendReport how much your answer moves across defensible choices (which runs you call usable, which axes you draw the line on) — that spread is your error bar, and pretending it is zero is the only wrong answer.
  • Agreement is the real answer keyThe same quantity measured two independent ways must agree: an off-rate from the wash and from an intercept; a midpoint from a ratio of rates and from where a curve crosses half; a threshold from a line's intercept and from where a run stops growing. When they agree, trust both; when they do not, one chain has a slip — find it before exporting.
