Bench 1 · Track it — watch your conjugate wander

A vendor sells your lab a fluorescently tagged antibody. The datasheet gives the parts — antibody 150 kDa, each tag 30 kDa — but not how many tags are attached. That number is proprietary, and it sets the conjugate's size. You will measure it. Every problem in this homework builds on the numbers you earn at these two benches.

The microscope records one thing: where each glowing dot is, frame after frame, for 12 s. That is enough. The solvent constantly kicks the conjugates around, and how far they wander in a given time reflects both the particle and the medium. Each tracking session condenses that motion into one curve: mean-squared displacement (MSD) vs lag time, log–log. Work the bench in this order:

  1. Run a tracking session. The MSD curve appears on the right.
  2. Slide the fit window onto the clean middle of the curve. Avoid both ends: short lags sit on the camera's noise floor (orange line); the longest lags are starved of data and scatter. The verdict box tells you when the window is clean.
  3. Add the fit to your table — copy the slope and MSD at τ = 1 s from the result card. Record ≥ 4 windows you trust (≥ 3 dilute). The spread between windows is your uncertainty.
  4. Switch to crowded and record ≥ 2 more windows. Same conjugate, now in a cytoplasm-like medium. Watch what the slope settles on — Problem 2 uses that number.
This bench never reports D. It gives you a slope and an MSD@1s; finding the relation that turns those into D is part of the homework ( Notes lists every candidate). Remember: the camera sees motion in only two dimensions.

Bench 2 · Bleach it — the same conjugate, a different instrument

Your conjugate glows, so a laser can bleach every copy inside a spot of radius \(w\) that you choose. Unbleached neighbors wander in and the dark spot refills. The run stops at 50 % recovery and reports one number: the half-recovery time \(\tau_{1/2}\). Work the bench in this order:

  1. Pick a spot radius and bleach. The run stops itself at the 50 % crossing.
  2. Add the row — copy \(w\) and \(\tau_{1/2}\) from the result card. Bleach ≥ 4 different sizes, ≥ 5 rows total; repeat one size to see the noise.
  3. Read your own log–log plot. How does \(\tau_{1/2}\) grow with \(w\)? Let your data claim the exponent before you convert anything.
This bench never reports D either. Turning your τ½-vs-w table into a second, independent D is your job — the relation is a citable classic of the photobleaching literature ( Notes has it). Problem 5 compares your two D's: agreement between two instruments that share nothing is the best evidence a measurement can get.

Challenge — one cell, sealed in a droplet graded for 566 · everyone should attempt

Droplet microfluidics seals a single living cell of radius \(a\) inside a droplet of radius \(R_{drop}\) — a sealed pantry stocked with one nutrient at \(C_0\). Every nutrient molecule that reaches the cell is consumed. Nothing else enters or leaves. Your dataset prints \(R_{drop}\), the cell radius \(a\), and the nutrient's molecular radius \(r_{nut}\) — a far smaller molecule than your conjugate, in the same medium.

The question: how long until 90 % of the nutrient is gone — and was the model you used even legitimate?

That is the entire prompt — choosing the physics and carrying it to numbers is what makes this the challenge. Work it entirely by hand, in your PDF: nothing on this tab is recorded, and your exported results file carries no challenge fields. In the PDF:

  1. Set up and solve the governing differential equation for the nutrient — define every symbol, state every assumption — and answer the question.
  2. Get the depletion time constant two independent ways — one route built on a D you measured, one that never touches your measurements — and reconcile them.
  3. Rule on your own well-mixed assumption with a symbolic ratio of timescales.

Notes — equations & when they apply

Notes — every law in this unit, and when each one applies

This sheet is your field guide. No question in this homework names its equation — you match the situation to the law. For each entry: what it says, the assumptions it stands on, and the trap that most often breaks it. Nothing here is graded and nothing is hidden.

How to search this sheet. Every law here is thermal agitation plus a geometry. Ask three questions — what is driving, what is resisting, what shape is the stage? — then read the "when it applies" lines against your answers. And when two laws give the same quantity two ways and agree, that agreement is your confidence — no answer key needed.

The thermal budget — and what gravity can do against it

$$\tfrac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac{3}{2}k_BT \;\Rightarrow\; v_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} \qquad\qquad \ell_g = \frac{k_BT}{\Delta\rho\,V\,g}$$
  • Symbolsm = one molecule's mass (kg) · kBT ≈ 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J at 300 K · Δρ = density excess over the fluid (kg/m³) · V = particle volume · ℓg = gravitational length (how high thermal energy can hold the particle).
  • What they tell youLeft: temperature is an energy budget shared equally by every molecule — speed is what each mass can buy with it. Right: the same budget measured against gravity's bill; if ℓg exceeds the particle's own size, settling never wins.
  • When they applyThermal equilibrium (left: any gas or solute); a dilute particle in fluid under gravity (right). Both are equilibrium statements — no rates, no viscosity anywhere.
  • Watch outvrms is not the mean speed (≈ 0.92 vrms) and never a transport speed — a molecule at 500 m/s still takes seconds to cross a millimetre, because it keeps colliding. And viscosity sets how fast things settle, never whether.

Random walks & the mean-squared displacement

$$\langle r^2(\tau)\rangle = 2\,d\,D\tau \quad (d=\text{dimensions: } 4D\tau \text{ in a 2-D image, } 6D\tau \text{ in bulk}) \qquad \mathrm{MSD}_{meas} = 4D\tau + 4\sigma_{loc}^2 \;\;\text{(2-D tracking)}$$
  • Symbolsτ = lag time (s) · D = diffusion coefficient (µm²/s) · σloc = localization error per position (µm) · d = the number of dimensions the MEASUREMENT sees, not the world has.
  • What it tells youA diffusing particle covers area, not distance, at a steady rate: typical reach grows like √τ. On log–log axes the law is a line of slope 1 whose height fixes D — the MSD at τ = 1 s is 2dD numerically.
  • When it appliesFree, unobstructed, unbound diffusion; ensemble- or time-averaged; lags above the noise floor and short enough that many independent intervals contribute. Count your dimensions: a camera sees 2, a cell interior is 3.
  • Watch outThe camera floor 4σ² props up short lags and sags a naive fit; and the 4-vs-6 dimensional prefactor is the most quietly expensive slip in this unit — a factor 1.5 that looks like nothing and fails everything.
$$\mathrm{MSD}(\tau) \propto \tau^{\alpha}, \quad \alpha < 1 \qquad \text{(subdiffusion in crowded media)}$$
  • What it tells youIn a crowded or viscoelastic medium the exponent itself drops below 1 — the log–log slope is the measurement (α).
  • When it appliesCytoplasm, gels, membranes — anywhere obstacles or elastic recoil anticorrelate successive steps.
  • Watch outThe prefactor of a subdiffusive fit carries units µm²/sα — not a diffusion coefficient; never compare it with one. Ratios of MSDs at one chosen time are safe — that is how a crowding penalty is honestly stated.

Moving through fluid — drag, and Einstein's bargain

$$\zeta = 6\pi\eta\,a \qquad\quad v=\frac{F}{\zeta} \qquad\quad D = \frac{k_BT}{\zeta} = \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta\,a}$$
  • Symbolsζ = drag coefficient · η = viscosity (Pa·s; water ≈ 0.85 mPa·s at 300 K, 0.69 at 310 K) · a = particle radius · F = applied force.
  • What it tells youDrag is linear in speed; the drag price scales with the particle's radius; and — Einstein's bargain — the same ζ that resists pulling also sets how far thermal kicks scatter the particle. It runs both ways: size → D (prediction) or D → size (an instrument).
  • When it appliesLow Reynolds number (a µm particle in water sits at Re ~ 10⁻⁵), a rigid sphere, a Newtonian fluid, far from walls.
  • Watch outWork in SI (D in m²/s, η in Pa·s) and convert at the end. "× water" must reference water at your temperature. The radius here is hydrodynamic — for a molecule that is not a sphere (few are), Stokes–Einstein defines its hydrodynamic radius: the equivalent sphere with the same D. A hydration shell makes molecules read slightly large.

Molecular weight ↔ hydrodynamic radius

$$R_h \propto M^{1/3}\;\text{(compact molecules)} \qquad\quad \frac{R_{h,2}}{R_{h,1}}=\Big(\frac{M_2}{M_1}\Big)^{1/3} \;\Leftrightarrow\; \frac{M_2}{M_1}=\Big(\frac{R_{h,2}}{R_{h,1}}\Big)^{3}$$
  • SymbolsM = molecular weight · Rh = hydrodynamic radius — the radius of the equivalent sphere, the one that would diffuse the same way (it is what DLS and Stokes–Einstein report). No molecule is literally a sphere; Rh is the honest stand-in.
  • What it tells youCompact molecules share a roughly constant density, so molecular weight fills volume and volume grows as Rh³. Anchor to one KNOWN (M, Rh) pair and it becomes an instrument: a measured radius reads out a molecular weight, a known weight predicts a size. Doubling the mass fattens the radius only 26 % — read backwards, a small radius change is a big mass change.
  • When it appliesFolded (globular) proteins, tight complexes and conjugates — folded proteins share ρ ≈ 1.35–1.40 g/cm³, which is what makes the anchor honest.
  • Watch outFails for extended, unfolded, or branched shapes — a polymer coil is mostly solvent. Compare like-for-like: hydrodynamic radius against hydrodynamic radius, since hydration inflates both.

Steady gradients — flux through a film

$$J = D\,\frac{C_{left}-C_{right}}{L} \qquad \text{(steady state; the profile inside is a straight line)}$$
  • SymbolsJ = flux (crossings per area per time) · L = film thickness · C = concentrations held at the two faces.
  • What it tells youA held difference across a thin layer drives a steady, linear delivery: double the difference, double the flow; halve the thickness, double the flow. It answers "how much flows?" and, rearranged, "how thick may the barrier be?" — same law, different unknown.
  • When it appliesSteady state (faces held constant long enough that the interior profile has relaxed), no reactions inside the film, D constant across it.
  • Watch outUnits discipline: J inherits whatever concentration and length units you feed it — commit to one system before touching numbers. And steady state is an assumption with a clock: it takes ~L²/D to establish.

Filling a bleached hole (FRAP)

$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=D\,\nabla^2 c \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \tau_{1/2} = 0.224\,\frac{w^2}{D} \qquad \text{(Soumpasis 1983)}$$
  • What it tells youHow long diffusion takes to refill a bleached spot of radius w to half its final brightness. The w² is the whole story: a hole twice as wide takes four times as long. Runs forward (w → expected τ½: experiment design) and backward (τ½ → D: measurement).
  • When it appliesA uniform circular bleach spot, recovery by pure diffusion (no binding, no flow), in an effectively 2-D pool. The 0.224 belongs to exactly that geometry.
  • Watch outThe scaling is a diagnostic: recovery limited by unbinding barely depends on w at all. Establish the exponent from your own τ½-vs-w slope first, then invert the confirmed law at each spot size and average — ⟨f(x)⟩ ≠ f(⟨x⟩).
$$t_{traverse} = \frac{R^2}{6D} \qquad\qquad \tau_{search} \approx \frac{R^3}{3\,D\,a_{site}} \qquad (a_{site}\approx 3.4\ \mathrm{nm} = 3.4\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{\mu m})$$
  • What it tells youTwo very different 3-D times: crossing a region of radius R is fast; finding one specific tiny site inside it is slower by roughly R/asite. Wandering is cheap; arriving somewhere particular is expensive.
  • Watch outThe classic failure is a nm value dropped into a µm formula — a factor of 1000. Structural check that survives every parameter: τsearch ÷ ttraverse = 2R/asite.

Encounters — the diffusion-limited ceiling, and the unit bridge

$$k = 4\pi\,(D_1{+}D_2)\,(a_1{+}a_2) \qquad \text{rate} = k\,c \qquad \text{(perfect absorber — proportional to radius, not area)}$$
  • What it tells youThe fastest any sticky target can be found: set by how quickly diffusion delivers searchers, not by how much surface is available — hence radius, not area. One partner stationary? Its D is 0 and the formula collapses to 4πDa.
  • When it appliesSteady state, dilute searchers, every arrival captured (a deliberate upper bound — real chemistry can only be slower). Counting is Poisson: N events carry √N of noise.
  • Watch out1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³ (derive it: 10⁻⁹ mol/L × 6.022×10²³ ÷ 10¹⁵ µm³ per liter). The formula needs c in molecules/µm³. Answers off by exactly ×0.60 or ×1.66 are this bridge crossed wrongly — its fingerprints.

First-order loss — when removal is proportional to what remains

$$\frac{dX}{dt} = -\frac{X}{\tau} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; X(t)=X_0\,e^{-t/\tau} \qquad t_{1/2}=\tau\ln 2 \approx 0.693\,\tau \qquad t_{90\%}= \tau\ln 10 \approx 2.303\,\tau$$
  • What it tells youWhenever the removal rate is proportional to the amount left, the amount decays exponentially with one time constant τ — and every landmark (half gone, 90 % gone) is a pure multiple of τ, independent of everything else.
  • When it appliesWell-mixed pools drained by a first-order process: degradation, clearance, uptake by a surface whose capture rate is linear in concentration. "Well-mixed" is an assumption you can test — compare the mixing time to τ itself.
  • Watch outτ is not the half-life (that is τ·ln2) and not the time to empty (an exponential never empties). Where τ comes from is the modelling step: it is whatever combination of your system's constants makes the balance read −X/τ.

Flow vs diffusion — who carries the cargo

$$\mathrm{Pe} = \frac{L\,v}{D} \qquad \text{(Péclet number: flow beats diffusion when } \mathrm{Pe}\gg 1\text{)}$$
  • What it tells youA dimensionless referee for any transport question at scale L with flow v: Pe ≪ 1, stirring is irrelevant; Pe ≫ 1, diffusion is a footnote.
  • Watch outPe is scale-dependent — the same flow that dominates at a millimetre is invisible at a nanometre. Quote the L you used.

Statistics toolbox — how to treat the numbers

  • Average raw, then convert⟨f(x)⟩ ≠ f(⟨x⟩): average the raw measurement at each condition first, then push the average through the law. Converting run-by-run and averaging after inflates skewed quantities.
  • Exponent first, number secondA law earns its use when your own data shows its exponent. Fit the slope on log–log axes across conditions before inverting anything.
  • Uncertainty is spread you can defendReport how much your answer moves across defensible analysis choices (fit windows, repeats) — that spread is your error bar, and pretending it is zero is the only wrong answer.
  • Agreement is the real answer keyThe same quantity measured two independent ways must agree. When it does, trust both; when it does not, one chain has a slip — find it before exporting.
