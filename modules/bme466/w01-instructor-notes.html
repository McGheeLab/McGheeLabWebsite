How to use this

These are board notes, not a handout — what goes on the board and what comes out of your mouth, in order, with the numbers worked live. They are deliberately conceptual: the job of the two lectures is to make students want the equations and believe the picture. The module does the quantitative work.

  • Board = the story, the physical picture, one honest derivation per session, and the order-of-magnitude numbers that make it real.
  • Module = the same content with the algebra written out, plus simulations they turn the knobs on.
  • HW1 (out Week 2) = they measure it themselves.

Don’t reproduce the module at the board. If you’re writing a third line of algebra, stop and point at the module instead.

Markers: ▢ write/draw this · ask the room, wait · work this number live · point at the module.

Session 1 — The bouncing world

Tuesday 25 August · 75 min
One sentence: temperature is motion, and everything else in this course is bookkeeping on that fact.
They should leave believing: the air in this room is violent; pressure is molecular drumming averaged over time; \(k_BT\) is the currency for every process we study.

0 · Cold open — the bite 5 min

Don’t start with a definition. Start with the semester’s recurring scenario.

Tuesday evening, a mosquito bites your ankle. Twenty minutes later it itches and there’s a small raised bump. In that time, mosquito saliva proteins have spread through your skin, your mast cells have dumped histamine into the tissue, and that histamine has reached capillaries a millimetre away and told them to leak.
What carried the histamine that millimetre? Expect “the bloodstream,” “diffusion,” “it spreads.” Push back on each: there is no pump in that tissue, no motor attached to a histamine molecule, and no one aiming it.
Write in the corner of the board and leave it up all week: How fast can a molecule get where it needs to be?

Say the plan out loud: today we work out how molecules move at all; Thursday we find out why that motion is so much slower than it sounds.

1 · The mosh pit 10 min

Draw a box. ~15 dots, arrows in random directions, different lengths.
Board sketch — arrows deliberately different lengths and directions. The point of the picture is that it is violent and going nowhere.
~\(2.5\times10^{25}\) molecules per m³ · typical speed ~500 m/s · ~70 nm between collisions · a protein in water is struck ~\(10^{13}\) times per second.
Is the air in this room moving? Violently — roughly rifle-bullet speed, in every direction at once, with essentially zero net flow. “Still air” is a statement about the average, not the molecules. Flag this average-vs-individual distinction now; it is the most reused idea in the course.

Thirty-second historical beat: Bernoulli, 1738 proposed that gas pressure is just molecular bombardment — about 120 years before most physicists accepted that molecules exist.

Module Tab 1 §1.1 is this box, running, with a wall meter attached.

2 · Pressure is drumming 15 min

This is the one derivation to do on the board today. Do it for a single molecule and stop.

Draw a cube, side \(L\). One molecule, velocity \(v_x\), heading at the right wall. Then write only these three lines: Each bounce reverses it: momentum change \(=2mv_x\) Round trip: \(\Delta t = 2L/v_x\) \(\langle F\rangle = \dfrac{2mv_x}{2L/v_x} = \dfrac{mv_x^2}{L}\)
Board sketch — draw the incoming and outgoing molecule on two levels so the reversal is visible. The dashed path is the round trip that sets Δt.
This one molecule hits the wall a few times a second. Why does a pressure gauge read a steady number instead of a series of thumps? Because there are \(10^{25}\) of them and the gauge averages. Say it explicitly: pressure is not a property of a molecule, it is a property of a crowd.
Board sketch — two quick axes side by side. The spikes don’t get smaller as N grows; there are just so many that the sum stops wobbling (the wobble falls as 1/√N).

Then assert — do not derive — the two steps that finish it:

\(\langle v_x^2\rangle = \tfrac{1}{3}\langle v^2\rangle\) \(PV = \tfrac{1}{3}Nm\langle v^2\rangle\)

Tell them plainly: the algebra between those lines is three more steps and it is all in the module (eq. 2–8). What matters is that nothing went into this except “hard things bouncing.”

Module Tab 1 §1.4 animates exactly this — one molecule, impacts drawn as a spike train, and a slider that adds molecules until the spikes fuse into a flat line. The readout \(\langle F\rangle L/mv_x^2\) converges to 1.00: they watch the derivation be true.

3 · The 1600s already knew the answer 8 min

\(PV = nRT = Nk_BT\)

Three names, one line each: Boyle (1662) \(P\propto 1/V\); Charles (1787) \(V\propto T\); Avogadro (1811) equal volumes hold equal numbers. All empirical. Nobody knew why.

Set the two results side by side: From bouncing: \(PV = \tfrac{1}{3}Nm\langle v^2\rangle\) From experiment: \(PV = Nk_BT\)
What must therefore be true? Let them land it: the two right-hand sides are equal.
This is the moment of the lecture. A law measured with mercury columns and glass tubes, by people who did not believe in atoms, is reproduced exactly by assuming matter is tiny hard things bouncing around. Say that out loud — this is why kinetic theory won.

4 · \(k_BT\), the currency 15 min

Set the two expressions equal and rearrange to the only two equations they must memorise this week:

\(\tfrac{3}{2}k_BT = \tfrac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle\) — temperature is kinetic energy \(v_{rms} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}\)
Work \(k_BT\) live at body temperature. \(k_B = 1.38\times10^{-23}\) J/K, \(T = 310\) K → \(k_BT = 4.3\times10^{-21}\) J \(= 4.3\) pN·nm  ← box it, circle it.

Dwell here. Convert it aloud: piconewtons of force over nanometres of distance. Then say what lives at that scale — motors pull with a few pN, protein domains are a few nm across, receptor bonds break under a few pN. The cell’s entire mechanical world is denominated in \(k_BT\). Every remaining week is a story about something either paying that toll or being randomised by it.

Board sketch — one axis, three dots, and the noise zone shaded. This single picture explains reversible binding, why ATP is the currency, and why molecules don’t fall apart.
Rank these in \(k_BT\): a hydrogen bond, ATP hydrolysis, a covalent C–C bond. Then: which can thermal noise break on its own? ~2–5, ~20–25, ~150. Hydrogen bonds break constantly — which is why biology fastens things with them: they’re meant to come undone. ATP at ~20 \(k_BT\) sits far enough above the noise to be a reliable fuel packet. Covalent bonds never break thermally, which is what makes a molecule a molecule.
How fast is an oxygen molecule? \(m = 0.032/6.022\times10^{23} = 5.3\times10^{-26}\) kg → \(v_{rms} = \sqrt{3(1.38\times10^{-23})(310)/5.3\times10^{-26}} \approx\) 490 m/s. Mach 1.4 — every breath is a swarm of supersonic projectiles, and you feel nothing, because they arrive from every side at once.

Spare minute? Note that \(v_{rms}\) is not the average speed — the distribution (Maxwell 1860, Boltzmann 1868–72) is skewed and \(\bar v = 0.92\,v_{rms}\). Flag it as a real distinction, not pedantry, and move on.

How many O₂ in one breath? \(N = PV/k_BT\) with \(V = 0.5\) L, \(P = 101\) kPa → \(1.2\times10^{22}\) molecules, ~21% O₂ → \(2.5\times10^{21}\) oxygen molecules. More molecules in one breath than there are grains of sand on Earth.
Module Tab 1 §1.2–1.3: a box where collisions alone build the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve, and a piston whose measured points land on Boyle’s hyperbola.

5 · The pivot — set up Thursday 7 min

Now break the lecture on purpose.

Molecules move at 500 m/s. This room is 10 m across. If I open a bottle of perfume at the front, when do you smell it? Let them divide out loud: 10 m ÷ 500 m/s = 0.02 seconds.
Is that what happens? No — it takes minutes, and mostly it arrives because the air is moving, not because the perfume diffused. And the bite took twenty minutes to itch, not twenty microseconds.
Write, and leave up for Thursday: Molecules are fast. Transport is slow. Why?

Give the one-word answer — collisions — and stop. Thursday is the whole answer.

Assign: open the module, Tab 1, play with the pressure box and the piston before Thursday. Ten minutes, no writeup.

Session 2 — The drunkard’s walk

Thursday 27 August · 75 min
One sentence: a molecule with no memory spreads as \(\sqrt{t}\), and that single square root dictates the size of cells, the spacing of capillaries, and why you have a bloodstream.

0 · Answer Tuesday’s cliffhanger 5 min

A molecule in water is struck ~\(10^{13}\) times per second. It travels ~0.3 nm between hits.

So it is moving at hundreds of metres per second — but it never gets to go anywhere, because every fraction of a nanosecond something knocks it off course. It has speed and no direction.

If each kick is random, where does it end up on average? Right where it started — which sounds like nothing happens, and yet the perfume does eventually arrive. Resolving that apparent contradiction is today’s lecture.

1 · Brown, 1827 — and the trap he avoided 8 min

Tell the story properly, because the method is the lesson. Robert Brown, botanist, puts pollen grains in water under his microscope and sees them jittering without pause. The tempting 1827 conclusion — pollen comes from a living plant — is that this is some vital force, the spark of life.

What is the control experiment? Push until someone gets it: repeat with something definitively dead. Brown ground up rock dust, window glass, and — reportedly — a fragment of the Sphinx. Everything jittered. Not life.
Make the epistemological point, because it recurs all semester: he had a phenomenon and no mechanism, and was careful to publish the first without inventing the second. It took Einstein until 1905 to supply the mechanism.
Why couldn’t Brown see what was hitting the pollen? Water molecules are ~0.3 nm; light microscopy bottoms out near 200 nm. He was watching an object visibly shoved by things that are permanently invisible. A resolution problem, not a physics problem.
Board sketch — draw the path first, ask what could possibly be moving it, then add the molecules. The reveal is the lesson.
Module Tab 2 §2.1 is exactly this, with a cheat Brown never had: a show/hide molecules button. Hidden, it is his 1827 view — a speck wandering with nothing touching it. Shown, every impact is drawn as an arrow.

2 · The drunkard — the one derivation today 15 min

Draw a horizontal line, a dot on it, steps of size \(\delta\) left and right.
Board sketch — draw three trajectories, not one: the fan is what makes “⟨x⟩ = 0 but they clearly spread” obvious. Add the dashed √t envelope last.

Summer 1905: Karl Pearson writes to Nature asking for help with “the problem of the random walk” — a man takes \(N\) steps in random directions, where is he? Rayleigh answers within weeks. It enters folklore as the drunkard’s walk.

\(x_N = x_{N-1} \pm \delta\), each sign equally likely
What is \(\langle x_N\rangle\)? Zero — the + and − cancel. So by that measure he never goes anywhere. But he clearly does spread out. The average position is the wrong question.
Square it instead: \(x_N^2 = x_{N-1}^2 \pm 2\delta x_{N-1} + \delta^2\) Average — the cross-term dies → \(\langle x_N^2\rangle = \langle x_{N-1}^2\rangle + \delta^2\) \(\langle x^2\rangle = N\delta^2 = 2Dt\),   \(D \equiv \delta^2/2\tau\)
Then say the punchline slowly, and write it: Distance grows like \(\sqrt{t}\), not \(t\).
What does that do to travel time? Twice as far takes four times as long; ten times as far, a hundred times as long. There is no speed you can quote for diffusion — the effective speed falls as it goes.

Two warnings worth thirty seconds each, because they cost points later:

  • Keep the brackets. \(\langle x^2\rangle\) is an ensemble average. A single trajectory’s \(x^2/2t\) scatters wildly and never settles. D lives in the average, not the individual. (The module lets them set walkers to 1 and watch the law fail to appear.)
  • Mind the dimension. \(\langle r^2\rangle = 2Dt\) (1D), \(4Dt\) (2D), \(6Dt\) (3D) — each axis is an independent walk. A microscope track is 2D; a molecule crossing a cell is 3D. HW1 grades this.

3 · The tyranny of \(L^2\) 18 min

This is the heart of the course. Give it real time.

\(t \approx L^2/6D\)  (3D)
Board sketch — three circles, radii in the ratio 1 : 2 : 3, labelled t, 4t, 9t. Drawing it beats saying it — the crowding of the rings is the √t law.

Take a small molecule, \(D \approx 10^{-9}\) m²/s in water, and build the ladder live — each one is quick and the effect is cumulative:

across a…distancetime
synaptic cleft20 nm~70 ns
bacterium1 µm~0.2 ms
your cell10 µm~17 ms
the mosquito wheal1 mm~3 min
ankle → brain1 m~5 years

Let the last one sit.

What does that number tell you about your own body? That diffusion alone could not possibly run you. You have a circulatory system because of the bottom row — and we do that properly in Weeks 9–10.
And why is a typical cell about 10 µm? Not a coincidence: roughly the largest package diffusion can service on a useful timescale. Cells that must be bigger cheat — neurons use motors on cytoskeletal tracks (Week 5), muscle fibres go multinucleate, large eggs stir themselves.
Bring it back to the bite: the wheal is about a millimetre, the table says minutes, and the itch takes — minutes. You just predicted a physiological timescale from a random walk. Name that explicitly; it’s the first time this course does something predictive.
Module Tab 2 §2.4 has this as a map: a diffusion front expanding from the bite past the cell, the wheal, the knee, with the “equivalent speed” visibly collapsing.

4 · Spreading vs finding 12 min

One more turn of the screw, and it sets up everything in Part I. Everything so far answers how far does it get. Biology usually needs the harder question:

How long until it finds one particular place?

Concrete cases: a transcription factor must find one ~10 bp site in a genome of \(10^9\) bases; a neurotransmitter must find a receptor. None of them can steer.

Our drunkard leaves the bar and his house is four blocks away. He is not lost — he just has no sense of direction. Roughly how many steps to get home? Take guesses; they will lowball badly. For a modest city it is hundreds to thousands of steps for a four-block walk.
Sketch a scribbled path re-crossing itself. He keeps re-walking streets he has already walked. In 2D the mean search time grows roughly like \(N\ln N\) in the number of corners — the logarithm is precisely the cost of revisiting.
Board sketch — draw the green direct route first, then scribble the purple one over it until the class laughs. The visual contrast does the whole argument.
Module Tab 2 §2.5 runs this, with a live histogram of steps-to-home. Measured: 103 / 471 / 1033 steps on 7×7, 15×15, 27×27 grids — doubling the city costs ~4.6×, not 2×. A nudge toward home of 0.2 collapses 471 steps to 59.
The biological moral: a faint sense of direction beats blind search enormously. That is why chemotaxis exists, and it is where Weeks 3–5 go.

5 · What we still cannot do — the bridge 7 min

Close by being honest about the hole in the week.

Every number today came from \(D\). Where did I get \(D\)? From a table. We have not derived it once.
Leave these two up: 1. What sets \(D\)? — Einstein, 1905: \(D = k_BT/6\pi\eta a\) 2. What about gradients and encounters? — Fick & Smoluchowski · Week 2

Preview #1 in words only, because it is beautiful and takes ten seconds: the same collisions that push a particle around also resist it when it tries to move. Diffusion and drag are the same phenomenon seen from two sides, so they cannot be independent — and forcing them to agree gives you \(D\) from nothing but temperature, viscosity and size. Note \(k_BT\) on top (everything diffuses, for free) and size and stickiness underneath (that is the price).

The practical consequence: once you have \(D\), today’s step-counting becomes a stopwatch. The drunkard’s 471 steps become 471 × (seconds per step), and the transcription factor’s search becomes a number you can compare against how fast genes actually switch on.
Assign: module Tabs 2 and 3 before Tuesday. HW1 goes out next week — warn them now that it works differently from any homework they’ve had: nobody gives them \(D\); they measure it, four different ways, from data seeded to their own course ID.

Misconceptions to hunt (both sessions)

  • “Heavier molecules are colder.” No — at the same temperature every species carries the same average energy. Heavy means slow, not cold. Module §1.2 makes this visible: bath and tracer read the same \(\langle KE\rangle/k_BT\) while their speeds differ by an order of magnitude.
  • “Diffusion has a speed.” It does not. Quote D, or a time to cross a stated distance. Any µm/s figure is meaningless without the distance it was measured over.
  • “Brownian motion is caused by currents / vibration / the pump down the hall.” Brown’s rock-dust control is the answer, and it is why the story is worth telling.
  • “⟨x⟩ = 0 means nothing happens.” The mean is zero; the spread is not. Average of the square, not square of the average — the same move that made pressure work on Tuesday.
  • “Molecules aim at their target.” Nothing in Part I steers. Everything is found by accident, and biology’s job is to make the accident likely enough.

  • Session 2: cut the search/drunkard section (§4) and fold one sentence of it into the Week 2 opener. Do not cut the \(L^2\) ladder; it is the payoff of the week.

Provenance & two corrections

Board content follows F25 Lecture 1 pp. 3–8 and keeps its equation numbering where the module uses it. Parameter values are cited in the module’s Sources panel (Einstein 1905, Kestin 1978, Luby-Phelps 2000, Milo & Phillips 2015).

Two corrections to the F25 notes — already applied in the module. Do not re-teach the old versions.
  1. The F25 constants box lists “\(D_{gas} = 10^{-9}\) m²/s.” That is dissolved gas in water. A gas molecule diffusing in air is ~10⁴× faster (~\(2\times10^{-5}\) m²/s). If you use the perfume-across-the-room example, use the air value or the estimate comes out wrong.
  2. F25 eq. 13 writes drag as \(F = \zeta v_{rms}\). Drag opposes whatever velocity the particle actually has: \(F = \zeta v\). \(v_{rms}\) belongs to Tuesday’s energy accounting, not to the drag law. This matters when you reach Stokes–Einstein.
