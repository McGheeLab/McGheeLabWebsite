Escape! — how far does randomness carry you?

A molecule in cytoplasm has no motor and no plan — it is kicked ~10¹³ times a second and takes a tiny random step each time. Below, one particle (your unknown nanoparticle, at your dataset's temperature \(T\)) starts at the center. You draw a circular finish line at distance \(L\); the experiment runs until the particle first crosses it and reports the escape time \(\tau\). That single number is called the first-passage time, and how it grows with \(L\) is the deepest fact about diffusion.

$$\text{If diffusion were like walking: } \tau \propto L. \qquad \text{Is it? Your table will say.}$$

Two warnings from every real lab: single random events scatter — repeat runs at the same \(L\) and expect wildly different \(\tau\); and conclusions come from the trend across many runs, never one run.

How to average — this one matters

Escape times are heavy-tailed: most runs finish quickly, a few take many times longer, and the slow ones carry real weight. That has a sharp consequence:

$$\text{average the } \tau \text{ values at each } L \text{ FIRST, then convert: } \quad \bar\tau(L) \;\longrightarrow\; D. \qquad \textbf{Never average } D \textbf{ or } 1/\tau \textbf{ run-by-run.}$$

Because \(\langle 1/\tau\rangle \neq 1/\langle\tau\rangle\) for a spread-out distribution, converting each run separately and then averaging inflates your answer — by a factor of two or more here. So: repeat each distance at least three times. Your plot below does the right thing automatically — it shows every run faintly and marks the mean τ at each distance.

Two readings, in the right order

Your plot reports two different things, and the order matters:

  1. The free fit's slope — this tests the law. Is the exponent really 2, or something else? That is Question 1's business.
  2. D from the square law — once you believe the exponent, apply \(\bar\tau = L^2/4D\) at each distance and average those. That is Question 2's answer.

Why not simply read D off the free fit's intercept? Because the intercept is the fitted line extrapolated back to \(L=1\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) — far outside your data. A slope that is off by even 0.3 (easy, with this much scatter) throws that extrapolation off by several fold. Testing a law and using it are different jobs; do them in that order.

  • First-passage time = when a random walker first reaches a boundary you set.
  • One run is an anecdote; the trend across averaged repeats is the physics.
  • The scatter is real (not measurement error) — and it biases careless averages.

The Ensemble — from anecdotes to a law

Tab 1 taught you that single escapes scatter wildly. The cure is the ensemble: track many particles at once and average. The mean-squared displacement (MSD) obeys an exact law, \(\langle r^2(\tau)\rangle = 4D\tau\) in 2-D — a straight line of slope 1 on a log–log plot. Here you run tracking sessions (20 fps, 12 s) on the same unknown particle and fit the MSD.

But real tracking has a localization-noise floor: every measured position is off by \(\sigma_{loc}\), which adds a constant \(4\sigma_{loc}^2\) to the MSD. At short lags this floor flattens the curve (slope < 1); at the longest lags statistics collapse. Where you fit is a scientific choice — your table of fit windows makes that choice visible.

$$\mathrm{MSD}(\tau) = 4D\tau + 4\sigma_{loc}^2 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \text{fit the middle, defend the window}$$

Crowding (566)

Real cytoplasm is 20–40 % crowded. Switch the scenario and the law itself changes: \(\mathrm{MSD}\propto\tau^{\alpha}\), \(\alpha<1\) — anomalous subdiffusion. The slope you fit is α.

  • The ensemble average is lawful even though each walker is random.
  • Noise floor at short lags, scatter at long lags — fit the clean middle.
  • Your uncertainty on D is how much the fit moves across defensible windows.

Drag — the fluid pushes back with the same hand that kicks

Diffusion (random kicks) and drag (resistance to being pulled) come from the same molecular collisions. Einstein's relation ties them: \(D = k_BT/\zeta\). Here you trap a calibration bead of known radius \(r_{bead}\) with an optical tweezer in the same cytoplasm, pull with a force you choose, and time it across a 10-µm finish line. Thermal jiggle rides on top of the drift — so even this "clean" measurement scatters.

$$v = \frac{F}{\zeta}, \qquad \zeta = 6\pi\eta\,r_{bead} \qquad \text{(Stokes drag)} \qquad D = \frac{k_BT}{\zeta} \ \text{(Einstein)}$$

Your v–F table should be a straight line through the origin; its slope is \(1/\zeta\). From ζ you get the cytoplasm viscosity η, and from η + your Tab-2 \(D_1\), Stokes–Einstein hands you the unknown particle's radius — its identity.

  • Fluctuation (D) and dissipation (ζ) are two faces of one physics: \(D=k_BT/\zeta\).
  • Linearity of v vs F is itself a result — check it, don't assume it.
  • \(1\ \mathrm{pN\cdot s/\mu m^2} = 1\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s}\) — the unit bridge you need.
An idealization worth naming. We treat this cytoplasm as a simple Newtonian fluid with one viscosity — the same η for a micron-scale bead and a nanometre-scale particle. Real cytoplasm is not that: its effective viscosity depends on probe size, because a small particle threads between crowders that a large bead must push through (Luby-Phelps 2000, in the Sources). Keeping that assumption explicit is part of the answer; 566 students should say what it would change.

Filling the Hole — Fick's second law with a stopwatch

The unknown particle was trackable one at a time. A GFP-tagged transcription factor in a nucleus is not — too small, too fast, too many. So we watch the concentration field instead: a laser bleaches every fluorophore inside a spot whose radius \(w\) you choose; unbleached neighbors diffuse in and the spot recovers. The experiment stops itself the moment recovery crosses 50 % and hands you \(\tau_{1/2}\).

$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=D\,\nabla^2 c \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \tau_{1/2} = 0.224\,\frac{w^2}{D_2} \ \ \text{(Soumpasis 1983)} \qquad \text{— how does } \tau_{1/2} \text{ grow with } w\text{? Your table decides.}$$

Same discipline as Tab 1: several spot sizes, repeat runs, log–log plot, read the exponent — then turn the fit around to extract \(D_2\). Your dataset's own spot (★ on the slider) and your nucleus radius \(R_{nuc}\) are in the readouts.

What your \(D_2\) then buys you

Two classic estimates follow immediately, and the mission asks for both:

$$\underbrace{t_{traverse} = \frac{R_{nuc}^2}{6D_2}}_{\text{crossing the nucleus (3-D)}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\tau_{search} \approx \frac{R_{nuc}^3}{3 D_2 a_{site}}}_{\text{one factor finds one 10-bp site}}, \quad a_{site}\approx 3.4\ \mathrm{nm}$$

The second is the famous one: a target a few nanometres wide inside a nucleus micrometres across. Compare the two numbers — the ratio is roughly \(R_{nuc}/a_{site}\), which is why finding a gene is so much harder than crossing the nucleus.

  • FRAP measures D from the field, not the particle — recovery time \(\sim w^2/D\).
  • The \(w^2\) is Fick's second law speaking; your exponent should say so.
  • Photon noise makes the 50 % crossing a judgment — that judgment is your error bar.

Counting — the speed limit of sensing

A cell "smells" by capturing ligand molecules — here, a signalling ligand that happens to diffuse at the same rate as the transcription factor you just characterized, so your \(D_2\) is the number to use. Even a perfect absorber (every touch captured) cannot beat the rate at which diffusion delivers: Smoluchowski's limit. You control the bath concentration \(c\); the ★ on the slider marks your cell's own environment \(c^\star\). Count captures at several \(c\), and let the line through your table reveal the law.

$$J_{max} = 4\pi D\,a\,c \qquad \text{— note: proportional to the radius } a\text{, not the area. Delivery, not surface, is the bottleneck.}$$

Deliberate unit trap: the slider says nM; the formula wants molecules/µm³. Deriving 1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³ is part of the written homework — and it is the difference between a right answer and one that is off by a factor of 1.66.

Counting takes time

Because arrivals are random, a cell cannot know \(c\) instantly: to resolve a fractional difference \(\delta\) it must average long enough to collect \(\sim 1/\delta^2\) independent counts. Berg & Purcell's estimate (an order-of-magnitude relation, not an exact identity):

$$\tau_{avg} \;\sim\; \frac{1}{D\,a\,c\,\delta^{2}} \qquad (\delta = 0.1 \text{ here — a 10 \% difference})$$

566: switch to the discrete-receptor cell and find how astonishingly few receptors reach half the limit (Berg & Purcell 1977).

  • Diffusion, not binding chemistry, sets the ultimate sensing rate: \(k = 4\pi Dac\).
  • Counting is Poisson — expect \(\sqrt N\) noise; fit the slope, never one point.
  • Berg–Purcell: resolving small concentration differences costs time.

The Map — your numbers, from bench to cell

The map below is drawn from your own answers (\(D_1\) from Tab 2, \(D_2\) from Tab 4): how far diffusion carries each molecule in a given time, against a molecular motor walking at \(v\). Below the crossover \(x_{cross}=4D/v\) diffusion wins — free and instant; beyond it the cell must pay for motors and ATP. This one picture is why metabolites diffuse, vesicles ride motors, and no one smells a banana from across a still room (that's the written Problem 6).

  • Diffusion: \(x\sim\sqrt{4Dt}\). Motors: \(x = vt\). \(\sqrt t\) always loses eventually.
  • The crossover \(x_{cross}=4D/v\) is set by your measured D.
  • Passive where it's cheap, active where it's necessary — the cell's design logic.

Notes — every equation in this unit, and when each one applies

This page is your quick reference: each law used anywhere in this module, what it tells you, the assumptions it stands on, and the trap that most often breaks it. Nothing here is graded and nothing is hidden — this is the same standard sheet the Practice tab's copy-for-chatbot button hands to an AI tutor.

The one idea behind all of it. Every equation on this page is the same physics wearing a different costume: thermal agitation (kBT rations out kicks) plus a geometry (a circle to escape, a window to fit, a line to cross, a hole to fill, a sphere to feed). To use the sheet: decide what is random and what is fixed, pick the geometry that matches your experiment, read the assumptions line, then compute. If two of these laws measure the same quantity two different ways and agree — that agreement is your confidence, no answer key required.

Random walks & the mean-squared displacement — Tab 2 · practice 2, 2b

$$\langle r^2(\tau)\rangle = 4D\tau \quad \text{(2-D)} \qquad\qquad \mathrm{MSD}(\tau) = 4D\tau + 4\sigma_{loc}^2 \quad \text{(what you actually measure)}$$
  • Symbolsτ = lag time (s) · D = diffusion coefficient (µm²/s) · σloc = localization error per position (µm). The 4 is dimensional: 2 per axis × 2 axes — in 1-D it is 2Dτ, in 3-D it is 6Dτ.
  • What it tells youA diffusing particle covers area, not distance, at a steady rate: typical excursion grows like √τ. On log–log axes the law is a straight line of slope 1, and its height fixes D.
  • When it appliesFree (unobstructed, unbound) diffusion; positions averaged over many particles or many time origins; lag times above the noise floor and short enough that many independent intervals contribute.
  • Watch outEvery measured position carries ±σloc, adding the constant 4σ² — at short lags it swamps 4Dτ and inflates a naive fit. Fit the middle window; report how much D moves as you vary the window.
$$\mathrm{MSD}(\tau) \propto \tau^{\alpha}, \quad \alpha < 1 \qquad \text{(subdiffusion in crowded media)}$$
  • What it tells youIn a crowded or viscoelastic medium the exponent itself drops below 1 — the log–log slope is the measurement (α), not a fitting nuisance.
  • When it appliesCytoplasm, gels, membranes — anywhere obstacles or elastic recoil anticorrelate successive steps.
  • Watch outThe prefactor of a subdiffusive fit carries units µm²/sα — it is not a diffusion coefficient and cannot be compared with one. And the noise floor biases α low exactly as it biases slope in the dilute case.

First passage — escape times — Tab 1 · practice 1

$$\bar\tau = \frac{L^2}{4D} \qquad \text{(mean first-passage time from the centre of a circle of radius } L\text{, 2-D)}$$
  • What it tells youHow long randomness takes to first reach a boundary: double the distance, wait four times as long. In 3-D the constant changes (4 → 6 for a sphere) but the L² scaling — the actual lesson — survives.
  • When it appliesTo the mean over many runs, started at the centre, with an absorbing boundary. One run tells you almost nothing.
  • Watch outFirst-passage times are heavy-tailed: their standard deviation is ≈ 0.71 of the mean, so identical runs differing several-fold are physics, not sloppiness. Because \(\langle 1/\tau\rangle \neq 1/\langle\tau\rangle\) (Jensen's inequality), converting each run to a D and averaging is biased high — often ×2. Average the times at each L first, then convert. And extrapolate with the confirmed law, never with a free fit's intercept (that intercept is an extrapolation to L = 1 µm and swings wildly).

Drag, Stokes & Einstein — Tab 3 · practice 3

$$v = \frac{F}{\zeta} \qquad\quad \zeta = 6\pi\eta\,r \qquad\quad D = \frac{k_BT}{\zeta} = \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta\,r}$$
  • SymbolsF = applied force (pN) · v = drift velocity (µm/s) · ζ = drag coefficient (pN·s/µm) · η = viscosity (Pa·s) · r = particle radius · kBT ≈ 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J at 300 K.
  • What it tells youThree laws chained: drag is linear in velocity (Stokes flow), the drag coefficient encodes the fluid's viscosity and the particle's size, and — Einstein's 1905 bargain — the same ζ that resists pulling also sets how hard thermal kicks scatter the particle. Measure any two of {ζ, D, r} and the third follows.
  • When it appliesLow Reynolds number (a µm bead in water sits at Re ~ 10⁻⁵ — inertia is honestly negligible, the bead forgets its velocity in nanoseconds), a rigid sphere, a Newtonian fluid, far from walls.
  • Watch outUnits: pN ÷ (µm/s) = pN·s/µm, and after dividing by 6πr (µm) you land in pN·s/µm² — which is exactly 1 Pa·s. To quote "× water" you must use water's viscosity at your temperature: at 300 K it is ≈ 0.85 mPa·s, not 1.0. A v–F line that misses the origin means a residual force (flow, tilt) — zero force must mean zero average drift.

FRAP — filling a bleached hole — Tab 4 · practice 4

$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=D\,\nabla^2 c \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \tau_{1/2} = 0.224\,\frac{w^2}{D} \qquad \text{(Soumpasis 1983)}$$
  • What it tells youHow long diffusion takes to refill a bleached spot of radius w to half its final brightness. The w² is the whole story: a hole twice as wide takes four times as long, because diffusion covers distance like √t.
  • When it appliesA uniform circular bleach spot, recovery by pure diffusion (no binding, no flow, no directed transport), in an effectively 2-D pool. The 0.224 comes out of the exact solution for precisely that geometry — a different bleach profile changes the constant.
  • Watch outThe scaling is a diagnostic, not just an average: if recovery were limited by unbinding kinetics instead of diffusion, τ½ would barely depend on w at all. Establish the exponent from the slope of τ½ vs w first, then invert the confirmed law (D = 0.224 w²/τ̄½) at each spot size and average.
$$t_{traverse} = \frac{R^2}{6D} \qquad\qquad \tau_{search} \approx \frac{R^3}{3\,D\,a_{site}} \qquad (a_{site}\approx 3.4\ \mathrm{nm} = 3.4\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{\mu m})$$
  • What it tells youTwo very different 3-D times: crossing a nucleus of radius R is fast; finding one specific 10-bp site inside it is slower by roughly R/asite — a factor of ~1000. Wandering is cheap; arriving somewhere particular is expensive.
  • Watch outThe single most common failure is a nm value dropped into a µm formula — a factor of 1000 in the search time. Convert asite to µm first. Structural check: τsearch ÷ ttraverse = 2R/asite, always.

Encounter rates — Smoluchowski & Berg–Purcell — Tab 5 · practice 5, 5b

$$k = 4\pi D\,a\,c \qquad \text{(perfect absorber — note: proportional to the radius } a\text{, not the area)}$$
  • What it tells youThe fastest any cell of radius a can capture molecules at bath concentration c — set by how quickly diffusion delivers them through the surrounding fluid, not by how much membrane is available. That is why the rate scales with a, not a².
  • When it appliesSteady state, dilute ligand, every arrival captured. Counting is Poisson: N captures carry √N of noise, so fit a whole run's slope rather than reading one point.
  • Watch out1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³ — the formula needs c in molecules/µm³. Leave c in nM and your measured-over-predicted ratio lands at ≈ 0.60; see a ratio near 0.60 or 1.66 and check units before anything else.
$$\tau_{avg} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{D\,a\,c\,\delta^{2}} \qquad\qquad k = k_{max}\,\frac{N s}{N s + \pi a}, \quad N_{1/2} = \frac{\pi a}{s}$$
  • What it tells youLeft: how long a cell must average its counts to resolve a fractional concentration difference δ — halve δ and the time quadruples (Berg & Purcell 1977). Right: capture with N receptor patches of size s saturates; half the perfect-absorber rate needs only N½ = πa/s receptors, covering ≈ πs/4a of the surface — a fraction of a percent.
  • Watch outReceptor number has strongly diminishing returns in both directions: at N½, halving N drops the rate only to ⅓ of maximum. A molecule that misses one patch is not lost — diffusion returns it for another try. That is why the k-vs-N curve saturates instead of climbing like a power law.

Statistics toolbox — how to treat the numbers

  • Average firstWhen single measurements scatter several-fold (escape times, half-recoveries), average the raw quantity at each x first, then convert. Converting run-by-run and averaging the converted values is biased whenever the conversion is nonlinear (1/τ, w²/τ): \(\langle f(x)\rangle \neq f(\langle x\rangle)\).
  • Slope vs constantDivision of labour: the log–log slope tests whether the exponent is what the law claims (2 for escape and FRAP, 1 for MSD and k-vs-c). Once confirmed, the physical quantity comes from applying the law to your group means — never from the free fit's intercept.
  • Through the originFit through the origin only when physics demands it (v = 0 at F = 0; k = 0 at c = 0) — and glance at whether a free fit would actually miss the origin, because that is how a bias force or background reveals itself.
  • UncertaintySpread of repeats at one x → sample SD (divide by N−1) → the mean's uncertainty is SD/√N. Just as honest: report how much your answer moves across the fit windows you consider defensible. Quoting three decimals from five scattered runs is fiction.
  • Two-method checkThe same D measured by escape times and by MSD (or ζ measured by drag and implied by D via Einstein) must agree. Independent methods agreeing is the confidence a scientist actually relies on — it needs no answer key.

Worked examples — the full chain, on the calibration standard

Each of these runs the complete analysis on the shared standard (the ★ Practice tab's sample), ending at its certified value. Your graded dataset is a different sample — these show the method, not your answers.

1 · Escape times → D (and the two-estimator trap)

Collected: 5 runs at L = 5 µm with mean τ̄ ≈ 1.9 s; 5 runs at L = 10 µm with mean τ̄ ≈ 7.1 s. Individual runs ranged 0.6–5.2 s at the same L — that spread is heavy-tailed first passage, not error.

Step 1 — test the exponent. slope = log(7.1/1.9)/log(10/5) = log(3.74)/log(2) ≈ 1.9 ≈ 2. The square law stands.

Step 2 — apply the confirmed law at each distance. D = L²/(4τ̄): at 5 µm, 25/(4·1.9) = 3.3; at 10 µm, 100/(4·7.1) = 3.5. Average: D ≈ 3.4 µm²/s — certified 3.43. ✓

The trap, run on the same data: converting each run to its own D and averaging those gave ≈ 6–7 µm²/s — biased high by ~2× because ⟨1/τ⟩ ≠ 1/⟨τ⟩. Same data, wrong order of operations, answer off by a factor of two.

2 · Ensemble MSD → the same D by an independent method

A tracking session's MSD flattens below τ ≈ 0.2 s (noise floor 4σ² ≈ 0.44 µm², i.e. σloc ≈ 0.33 µm) and scatters above τ ≈ 3 s (few independent intervals).

Step 1 — fit the middle. Window 0.35–2.65 s: slope 0.98 ≈ 1. Ordinary diffusion confirmed.

Step 2 — read the height. MSD(1 s) ≈ 13.7 µm² = 4D·(1 s) → D ≈ 3.4 µm²/s — the same particle as the escape experiment, by a completely independent method. That agreement is the real result.

Step 3 — defend the window. Nudging both edges moved D by ~±8 %. Report D = 3.4 ± 0.3 µm²/s, not D = 3.43000.

3 · Drag → viscosity → particle radius (the Einstein chain)

Four forces, four transits of the 10 µm line: v–F is a straight line through the origin with slope 12.4 µm/(s·pN).

ζ = 1/slope = 0.0805 pN·s/µm. η = ζ/(6πrbead) = 0.0805/(6π·1.0) = 4.27 × 10⁻³ pN·s/µm² = 4.27 mPa·s (1 pN·s/µm² = 1 Pa·s).

× water: at 300 K water is 0.854 mPa·s, so η_rel = 4.27/0.854 = 5.00× water — certified. ✓

Close the chain with Einstein: the tracked particle's D = 3.43 µm²/s in this same fluid gives r = k_BT/(6πηD) = 4.14×10⁻²¹/(6π · 4.27×10⁻³ · 3.43×10⁻¹²) ≈ 1.5×10⁻⁸ m = 15 nm. Drag gave the fluid; Einstein then sizes the particle.

4 · FRAP → D₂ from the hole-filling law

Half-recovery times: w = 1 µm → 0.015 s; w = 2 µm → 0.060 s; w = 4 µm → 0.24 s.

Step 1 — exponent. log–log slope of τ½ vs w = log(0.24/0.015)/log(4) = 2.0. Diffusive recovery confirmed (a binding-limited recovery would barely depend on w).

Step 2 — invert the confirmed law at each w. D = 0.224 w²/τ̄½ = 0.224/0.015 ≈ 14.9; 0.896/0.060 ≈ 14.9; 3.58/0.24 ≈ 14.9 → D₂ ≈ 15 µm²/s — certified 15.0. ✓

5 · Counting → the Smoluchowski slope (and the 0.6022 step)

Capture rates at several bath concentrations fall on a straight line through the origin: slope ≈ 340 s⁻¹ per nM.

Predict it: k = 4πDac needs c in molecules/µm³, so per nM: 4π · 15 · 3.0 · 0.6022 = 340.5 s⁻¹/nM. ✓ Measured and predicted agree — units handled.

The slip, shown: skip the 0.6022 and the "prediction" is 4πDa = 565.5 per nM; the measured/predicted ratio then reads 340.5/565.5 = 0.60 — the unmistakable signature of leaving c in nM.

Berg–Purcell coda: at c = 5.0 nM this cell captures k = 1703 s⁻¹, and averaging for τ ≈ 1/(D a c δ²) = 100/(15·3·3.011) ≈ 0.74 s resolves a 10 % concentration difference.
