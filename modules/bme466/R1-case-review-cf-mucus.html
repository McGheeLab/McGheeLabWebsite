1.1 · The case

A 19-year-old with cystic fibrosis has grown Pseudomonas aeruginosa from every sputum culture for six years. She inhales 300 mg of tobramycin twice a day, 28 days on and 28 days off. Her sputum drug levels come back around 10³ µg/g — roughly a thousand times the concentration that kills this organism in a test tube, and about a hundred times what an intravenous dose could safely put in her airway. Her exacerbations get less frequent on treatment. The cultures never turn negative.

The question this module answers: the drug is sprayed directly onto the infection at a thousand times its killing concentration. Why does it suppress the infection instead of curing it?

The pathology behind that question is a transport failure, and it starts one molecule at a time. CFTR is an anion channel in the apical membrane of airway epithelium; losing it means chloride and bicarbonate are not secreted while sodium absorption continues unopposed. Water follows the salt. The airway surface liquid — the thin film your cilia beat inside — dehydrates: mucus solids climb from roughly 2 % to 5–8 %, the mucin mesh tightens, the film thickens and stiffens, the periciliary layer collapses, and the cilia end up embedded in a gel instead of swimming under one. Mucus stops moving. Bacteria that would have been swept out in minutes stay for decades.

Everything in that paragraph is a length, a viscosity, a diffusion coefficient or a velocity — which means Modules 1 and 2 already gave you the whole toolkit. This tab sets the scene and lists the seven questions. Tabs 2 and 3 answer them, one collapsed reveal at a time. Open a reveal only after you have committed to an equation.

Why this case. Almost every tool from Weeks 1 and 2 is load-bearing here, and — the part that makes it worth a whole review — three of the seven answers are "that is not the bottleneck." Knowing which of your equations does not explain a pathology is the same skill as knowing which one does.

  2. Switch to CF, coated and then CF, plugged. Watch which numbers change and by how much — thickness, solids, mesh size, transport speed, bacterial load.

2.1 · Step 1 — How long does the drug take to cross the film?

The nebulizer deposits drug on the top of the mucus film. The bacteria live at the bottom of it, on and in the epithelial surface. So the first question is the simplest one in transport: how long does a molecule take to get from one side of a 50-µm film to the other?

Before you answer it, notice that you have been handed a three-dimensional problem. The airway is a tube; the film wraps around the inside of it; the drug lands in patches. Modules 1 and 2 wrote everything in one dimension. Question 1 is really two questions: what is the crossing time, and am I allowed to compute it in 1D?

Step 1 — the 1D licence, and the crossing time

First, earn the 1D model

A one-dimensional model is not a simplification you are entitled to; it is a claim about the geometry, and it has to be checked. Three conditions license it here, and they are worth stating as a criterion because you will re-use it every time you flatten a real tissue into a slab:

eq. R.2.1 $$\text{(i)}\ \frac{L}{W}\ll 1 \qquad \text{(ii) boundary conditions uniform over } W \qquad \text{(iii)}\ \frac{t_{\text{across}}}{t_{\text{along}}} = \Big(\frac{L}{W}\Big)^{2} \ll 1$$

Check them against the case. (i) Geometry: the film is \(L \approx 50\) µm thick; the airway it lines is millimetres across, so \(W \approx 5\) mm and \(L/W \approx 10^{-2}\). (ii) Boundaries: the air above is the same everywhere and the epithelium below is the same everywhere, so nothing in the problem varies along the surface — within a deposition patch. (Hold that caveat; Step 2 is about to break it.) (iii) Timescales: by eq. 1.2.8, crossing scales as \(L^2\) and spreading sideways scales as \(W^2\), so lateral transport is \((W/L)^2 = 10^{4}\) times slower than transport across. During the entire crossing, the sideways profile does not have time to change.

Condition (iii) is the one that does the real work, and it is worth saying in words: each column of mucus is its own independent one-dimensional problem, because its neighbours cannot get news to it in the time the crossing takes. \(\nabla^2C\) collapses to \(\partial^2C/\partial x^2\) with \(x\) = depth.

Now the time

The question asks for a time given a distance, so it is eq. 1.2.8 — and here the film's geometry makes that estimate exact rather than an order of magnitude: for a molecule released at a reflecting surface (the air interface, which it cannot cross) and absorbed at a plane a distance \(L\) away (the epithelium, where it is taken up), the mean first-passage time is exactly \(L^2/2D\) — the \(d=1\) case:

eq. 1.2.8 $$t \;\approx\; \frac{L^2}{2dD} \qquad\xrightarrow{\ d=1\ }\qquad t_{\text{cross}} = \frac{L^2}{2D}$$

Why this equation and not eq. 1.2.6. \(\langle r^2\rangle = 2dDt\) and \(t = L^2/2dD\) are the same statement rearranged, but they answer different questions: the first asks "how far by now", the second "how long to there". You were asked for a time. Take the water value for now — \(D_0 \approx 550\) µm²/s for tobramycin, computed properly in Step 3 — and read off the ladder:

Step 2 — the sedimentation length, and why the dose lands in patches

Gravity gives every object a potential energy that grows with height. Module 1 §2.2 wrote it with the buoyant weight, because a particle in a fluid only feels the density it does not share with the fluid:

eq. 1.2.1 $$U(h) = \Delta\rho\,V g\,h$$

and then asked at what height that energy equals one unit of thermal currency, \(k_BT\) — the amount of energy the surroundings hand out for free:

eq. 1.2.2 $$U(\ell_g) = k_BT \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ell_g \;=\; \frac{k_BT}{\Delta\rho\,V g}$$

Read it as a competition and the interpretation is automatic. If \(\ell_g\) is much bigger than the container, thermal energy wins and the object is spread uniformly: a diffuser. If \(\ell_g\) is smaller than the object itself, gravity wins completely: a projectile that lands and stays. Equilibrium in between is Boltzmann's exponential column, eq. 1.2.7 with \(U\) from eq. 1.2.1 — \(\ell_g\) is precisely its \(1/e\) height:

eq. 1.2.7 $$C(h) = C_0\,e^{-U(h)/k_BT} \;=\; C_0\,e^{-h/\ell_g}$$

Divide eq. 1.3.9 by that and something pretty falls out: \(D/v_{\text{sed}} = k_BT/\Delta\rho Vg = \ell_g\). The sedimentation length is the distance at which settling and diffusion are exactly matched — which is the same statement as \(\mathrm{Pe} = 1\), Step 7's referee, with \(v_{\text{sed}}\) as the flow. One length, three equations, all consistent.

Answer 2, and it changes Step 1. The molecule never settles: a kilometre of sedimentation length over a 50-µm film means the drug's concentration has no vertical structure from gravity at all, which is exactly the licence to leave gravity out of Fick's laws in Steps 4 and 5. The droplet, on the other hand, settles at 265 µm/s and lands ballistically — so the dose is delivered by gravity and inertia, and only then distributed by diffusion. That is why deposition is patchy, why breath-holding after a puff increases delivery, and why the uniform-surface boundary condition of eq. R.2.1 is only honest inside a patch. Between patches, the airway got no drug at all, and no amount of diffusion in 15 minutes will fix that: eq. 1.2.8 says spreading 1 mm sideways takes 15 minutes at best and hours in sputum.

  2. Nebulizer droplet: everything is on the floor within a second, and the profile is a spike, not an exponential. \(\ell_g\) = 31 nm is smaller than the droplet itself.
  3. Mucin raft: \(\ell_g\) lands inside the film — the only cargo where both terms matter, and the one case where the gel's own elasticity (not in this model) is what actually holds it up.

Step 3 — the viscosity trap, and what actually slows the drug down

The equation is right and the number you put in it is wrong. Start with the equation, the whole of Week 1 in one line:

eq. 1.3.9 $$D = \mu\,k_BT \;=\; \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta a}$$

Its \(\eta\) is the viscosity that the moving particle feels as it drags fluid past itself — the medium's resistance on the length scale of the particle. Bulk rheometry measures something else: the force needed to shear the entire mucin network, a mesh of entangled polymers hundreds of nanometres across. Those two numbers are the same only when the particle is big enough to have to push the network around.

A tobramycin molecule is 0.6 nm across. The mesh in CF sputum has pores of roughly \(\xi \approx 60\)–140 nm. The drug swims through water-filled holes in a stiff net — it never touches the net. Its \(\eta\) is water's, and its \(D_0\) is 550 µm²/s. Plugging 1 Pa·s into eq. 1.3.9 gives 0.38 µm²/s and predicts a 55-minute crossing time for a film that measurement says fills in seconds. The mistake is not arithmetic; it is using a macroscopic material property for a nanoscopic event.

The rule to carry out of here. In eq. 1.3.9, use the viscosity on the scale of the diffuser. If \(a \ll \xi\), that is the solvent's viscosity, whatever the rheometer says. If \(a \gtrsim \xi\), the network is the medium and bulk-like slowdowns are real. Mucus is not "thick" — it is selectively thick, and the selection variable is size.

So what does slow the drug down?

Two things, and neither is bulk viscosity. Steric hindrance from the mesh, which for a small molecule is almost nothing, and binding, which for this particular drug is everything: tobramycin is a polycation, and CF sputum is an anionic soup of mucins, extracellular DNA and F-actin. Most of the drug that arrives is stuck to something and neither moving nor killing. Bookkeeping both effects in one line:

eq. R.2.2 $$D_{\text{eff}} = \underbrace{H(a/\xi)}_{\text{steric}}\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+K}}_{\text{binding}}\;D_0, \qquad c_{\text{free}} = \frac{c_{\text{tot}}}{1+K}, \qquad H = e^{-a/(\xi-a)}\ \ (a<\xi)$$

\(K\) is the bound-to-free ratio at equilibrium; the \(1/(1+K)\) appears twice for the same reason — a bound molecule neither travels nor binds a ribosome. The hindrance factor \(H\) here is a deliberate cartoon, chosen because it reproduces the measured ladder (free passage for solutes, catastrophic trapping as \(a \to \xi\)); the literature models of record are obstruction-scaling for the steric part and adhesive trapping for the rest. With \(K \approx 9\) as an order-of-magnitude sputum-binding number, \(D_{\text{eff}} \approx 55\) µm²/s and only a tenth of the measured sputum drug is free — the two corrections that Steps 5 and 6 will use.

2.4 · Step 4 — How deep does oxygen get?

Change molecules. A CF mucus layer is not just thick; it is inhabited. There are 10⁸–10⁹ bacteria in every millilitre of sputum, and every one of them is burning oxygen at roughly a million molecules per second. This is a supply-and-demand problem with a fixed supply route, and it has a definite answer: a depth beyond which there is no oxygen at all. That depth turns out to be one of the most consequential numbers in the case — including for the antibiotic, for a reason that will not be obvious yet.

Step 4 — Fick's first law, supply versus demand, and an anoxic biofilm

Delivery rate from a gradient is Fick's first law, the equation Module 1 met inside Einstein's derivation and Module 2 canonized:

eq. 1.3.5 $$J_{diff} = -\,D\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}$$

Do the crude version first, because it takes ten seconds and decides whether the careful version is worth doing. If the profile were the straight line of eq. 2.1.2 — surface at \(C_s\), zero at the bottom —

eq. 2.1.2 $$C(x) = C_L + (C_R - C_L)\frac{x}{L}, \qquad J = D\,\frac{C_L - C_R}{L} \quad\text{(steady state)}$$

then the supply through the surface is \(J = D_{\mathrm{O_2}}C_s/L\), while the demand of a colonized layer of thickness \(L\) is \(Q\,L\), where \(Q\) is the volumetric consumption. With \(D_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 2000\) µm²/s, \(C_s = 100\) molecules/µm³ (dissolved O₂ in equilibrium with airway gas) and \(Q = 500\) molecules/(µm³·s) (5×10⁸ cells/mL × 10⁶ per cell per second): supply through a 50-µm layer is 4000, demand is 25 000, per µm² per second. Demand beats supply sixfold. The bottom of the layer is anoxic, and that took one division.

Now the careful version, which also gives the profile's shape. Steady state with consumption everywhere means what arrives at a slice minus what leaves it equals what is eaten there — Fick's second law with a sink, set to \(\partial C/\partial t = 0\): \(D\,d^2C/dx^2 = Q\). Integrate twice with \(C(0) = C_s\) and, at the depth \(\delta\) where oxygen runs out, both \(C = 0\) and \(dC/dx = 0\) (nothing can be delivered past the point where nothing is left):

eq. R.2.3 $$C(x) = C_s\Big(1 - \frac{x}{\delta}\Big)^{2}, \qquad \boxed{\ \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2 D\,C_s}{Q}}\ }$$

The profile is a downward-bending parabola, not a straight line — the gradient gets shallower with depth because there is less flux left to carry. And the penetration depth is a square root, so it is stubborn: quadrupling the bacterial load only halves the oxygenated depth.

Step 5 — Fick's second law, and the two per cent

A profile that is still changing is Fick's second law — the diffusion equation, and the only equation in this module with a time derivative in it:

eq. 2.2.1 $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} \;=\; -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x} \;=\; D\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}$$

Read it as bookkeeping, the way Module 2 built it: the concentration at a point changes only because more arrives than leaves. For this case the equation needs two additions, and both are physiology rather than new physics — a source at the surface that switches on for the nebulization and off afterwards, and a first-order clearance term \(-C/\tau_{\text{clr}}\) representing absorption, swallowing and mucus turnover, with \(\tau_{\text{clr}} \approx 2\) h. The simulation below integrates exactly that, then divides by \(1+K\) to report free drug, since only free drug kills (eq. R.2.2).

The lag before the far side notices is Step 1's equation again — this is the same \(L^2/2D\), now used as a filling time rather than a crossing time, because they are the same number:

eq. 1.2.8 again $$t_{\text{fill}} \approx \frac{L^2}{2D_{\text{eff}}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad L^\star = \sqrt{2 D_{\text{eff}}\,t_{\text{dose}}} \approx 315\ \mu\mathrm{m}$$

So a 50-µm film fills in 23 seconds and a 500-µm plug does not fill at all inside a 15-minute dose. Step 6 — Smoluchowski, and the ten-million-to-one clue

The rate at which a diffusing species finds a target is Smoluchowski's, previewed in Module 1 §5.1 and derived line by line in Module 2 §3.3:

eq. 1.5.1 $$k_s = 4\pi (D_1 + D_2)(a_1 + a_2)$$

Both of Module 2's riddles are answered inside this case. The \(D\)'s add because what matters is the relative walk, and the variances of two independent walks add — but here the bacterium is immobilized in a biofilm, so \(D_2 \approx 0\) and the sum is just the drug's \(D_{\text{eff}}\). The radii add because contact is a statement about centre-to-centre distance — but here \(a_1 + a_2 = 0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m} + 0.6\ \mathrm{nm}\), so the target radius is the bacterium's and the drug's own size is irrelevant. Both corrections collapse, which is exactly why the general form is worth knowing: it tells you when you may drop them.

eq. 1.5.1 again — immobile target, point-like ligand $$k_s \to 4\pi D_{\text{eff}}\,a_{\text{cell}} = 4\pi(55\ \mu\mathrm{m^2/s})(0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}) = 345\ \mu\mathrm{m^3/s}$$

Then the encounter rate is \(k_s c_{\text{free}}\), and Step 5's free concentration of 100 µg/mL is \(1.3\times10^{5}\) molecules/µm³ (that is the unit conversion Module 2 §3.3 warned you about: 1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³). The product is 4.5×10⁷ encounters per second, on one bacterium. Even at the planktonic MIC of 4 µg/mL it is 1.8×10⁶ per second. The cell has of order 10⁴ ribosomes; every one of them could be handed a drug molecule thousands of times a second.

Answer 6: delivery is not remotely the bottleneck — and the size of the mismatch is the clue. Tobramycin takes tens of minutes to hours to kill. Encounters happen ten million times a second. Between those two numbers sit six or seven orders of magnitude, and something has to account for them. It is not diffusion; the physics says arrival is free. It is the membrane: aminoglycoside uptake is an energy-dependent, proton-motive-force-driven process — and Step 4 just showed that the bacteria in question live below the oxygen line, with no respiration to drive it. Steps 4 and 6 are the same finding approached from two directions.

Note also where this \(k_s\) sits on Module 2's ceiling. Converted to the units enzymologists use, 345 µm³/s is \(2\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}\) — an order of magnitude above the famous 10⁹–10¹⁰ diffusion limit, and legitimately so: that ceiling is quoted for protein-sized targets, and this target is a whole cell a thousand times larger. Rate goes like \(a\), so a big target buys a big rate.

  2. Double the cell radius. The rate doubles — it does not quadruple. Supply, not surface area, is the bottleneck, which is Module 2 §3.1's headline in a clinical setting.

3.2 · Step 7 — The escalator, and what happens when it stops

Why a stalled escalator is a one-way door

Stokes–Einstein for a 0.5-µm cell in water gives \(D \approx 0.7\) µm²/s. Put it in CF sputum, where the mesh is 140 nm, and eq. R.2.2's steric factor goes to zero: a bacterium cannot diffuse through mucus at all. Its \(D\) is not small; it is effectively absent. Now read eq. 2.3.8 as a statement about exits: with \(D \to 0\), the diffusive escape time \(L^2/2D\) diverges, and the only transport left is the convective one, \(t = L/v\). Healthy: \(10^{4}/50 = 200\) s — a bacterium that lands is gone in three minutes. CF at \(v = 2\) µm/s: 5000 s, and at \(v \to 0\) there is no exit at all except a cough.

Answer 7, and the origin of the whole case. Mucociliary clearance is a convective transport system that exists precisely because diffusion cannot do this job — Pe for a bacterium in an airway is astronomical, which is another way of saying it has no diffusive alternative. CFTR loss dehydrates the blanket and takes \(v\) to zero. When \(v \to 0\) with \(D \approx 0\), the removal rate of anything bacterium-sized goes to zero, and residence time goes from minutes to indefinite. That is the disease: not a killing defect, a transport defect. Chest physiotherapy, exercise, huff-coughing and airway clearance devices are, in this language, prosthetic convection.

There is one uncomfortable corollary worth stating, because it is the kind of thing that only shows up if you write the numbers down: the stalled escalator also fails to remove the drug. In a healthy airway an inhaled dose would be swept to the pharynx in a few minutes. In the CF airway it stays, which is part of why inhaled dosing achieves the sputum levels it does. The same broken mechanism causes the infection and prolongs the therapy's contact with it.

  2. Switch to CF, stalled: the bolus stops moving. Nothing leaves. Toggle the cargo to bacterium and note that even the small residual spreading disappears.

    With \(D \to 0\), \(t_{\text{diff}} \to \infty\): the diffusive exit does not exist at any speed, so removal rate is entirely \(v/L\). Healthy 200 s; CF at 2 µm/s, 5000 s; CF at \(v = 0\), never.
  5. Interpret. Pe did not just tell us which term to keep. It told us that one transport mode was carrying the entire clearance function, and that a disease which zeroes \(v\) therefore has no fallback. Every physical therapy in CF is an attempt to restore that one number.
Pe = 0.05 across the film, 9×10³ along the airway, effectively infinite for a bacterium. Question 7's answer: diffusion runs the film, convection runs the airway, and CF is the loss of the convection.
  • Eq. 1.5.1 in this case simplifies twice over — immobile target kills the \(D\) sum, micron target kills the radius sum. Knowing why lets you drop terms safely.
  • Encounters per second exceed the killing rate by ~10⁷. When the physics says a step is free, the bottleneck is elsewhere: here, oxygen-dependent uptake.
  • Pe is not a property of a tissue, it is a property of a question. Choose \(L\) and the answer changes.
  • A cargo with no diffusive mobility has exactly one exit. Cystic fibrosis closes it.

Two equations on the shortlist never got used: eq. 2.2.3 (the FRAP half-time) and eq. 2.3.7 (\(\lambda = \sqrt{D\tau}\)). The first is a measurement technique — it is how you would obtain \(D_{\text{eff}}\) in a sputum sample on a microscope, not a step in the argument. The case, in one paragraph. Losing CFTR dehydrates the mucus blanket and stops the escalator (Step 7); with no diffusive exit, bacteria that would have been swallowed in three minutes stay for decades. They grow into a layer thick enough to outstrip the oxygen supply (Step 4), which both selects the biofilm phenotype and disables the proton-motive force that aminoglycoside uptake depends on. The inhaled drug arrives easily (Steps 1, 3, 6) — arrival was never the problem — but only a tenth of it is free (Step 3), and the free fraction sits above the biofilm's killing threshold for about fifteen minutes of every twelve hours (Step 5). In plugs it never gets there at all. The result is suppression without cure, which is exactly what the cultures show.

4.2 · The delivery chain as one product

Every step above multiplied the dose by a factor smaller than one. That is worth writing as a single line, because it shows why the therapy is marginal rather than either useless or curative — and where each drug on her medication list is trying to intervene:

eq. R.4.1 $$\Theta \;=\; \frac{c_{\text{free}}(\text{biofilm})}{\mathrm{MIC}_{\text{biofilm}}} \;=\; \underbrace{f_{\text{dep}}}_{\text{where it lands}}\;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{1+K}}_{\text{how much is free}}\;\times\; \underbrace{\min\!\Big(1,\ \frac{2D_{\text{eff}}\,t_{\text{dose}}}{L^2}\Big)}_{\text{how deep it got}}\;\times\; \frac{c_{\text{neb}}}{\mathrm{MIC}_{\text{biofilm}}}$$

with two gates that no value of \(\Theta\) can open on its own: the drug must be taken up (which needs oxygen, Step 4) and the organism must be removed or it simply regrows (which needs \(v\), Step 7). Three multiplicative losses and two gates — that is the entire case as an engineering diagram.

Run the interventions against it. The point of the simulation is not the exact numbers, which are order-of-magnitude; it is which variable each therapy actually touches, and how much of the chain that variable controls.

  1. Start with everything off — the untreated CF airway. Note which of the three gates is failing.
  2. Turn on higher/longer dose alone. It multiplies the smallest factor in the chain and still cannot open the oxygen gate. Brute force has a ceiling, and eq. R.4.1 shows you exactly where it is.
  3. Turn on dornase alfa and watch two factors move at once — the layer thins and \(K\) falls, because the DNA it digests was both the viscoelastic problem and the drug's main binder.
  4. Turn on the CFTR modulator last. Watch it move \(v\), \(L\), the mesh and the bacterial load together. One intervention upstream of everything is worth several downstream ones — which is the actual history of this disease since 2019.

Every gain came from changing the transport environment — thinner layer, less binding, restored convection, smaller bacterial load — and the three effects multiply. 4.3 · Honesty: what this model cannot tell you

Every one of these seven calculations is a cartoon with a domain of validity, and a review module that did not say so would be teaching the wrong lesson. The specific limits:

  • The slab is a fiction at the airway scale. Eq. R.2.1 is satisfied within a patch of a coated airway. It is not satisfied for a plug, which is a three-dimensional object with a curved boundary and no clean "surface". Plug pharmacology needs geometry this module does not have.
  • The hindrance factor \(H\) in eq. R.2.2 is invented for teaching. It has the right limits and the right qualitative ladder; the quantitative literature uses obstruction-scaling models plus explicit adhesive trapping, and measured hindrance for 100-nm particles in CF sputum spans two orders of magnitude depending on surface chemistry.
  • Binding is treated as instantaneous equilibrium with one \(K\). Real sputum binding is a spectrum of affinities to mucin, DNA and F-actin, and it is saturable — at high doses the free fraction rises, which is one honest argument for very large inhaled doses that this model handles only crudely.
  • The biofilm threshold is not a real MIC. "10–100× MIC" collapses several distinct phenomena — extracellular-polymer barriers, slow growth, persister cells, altered gene expression — into one number. Persisters in particular are not a concentration problem at all.
  • Zero-order oxygen consumption is an approximation. Real respiration saturates and then falls off at low \(P_{\mathrm{O_2}}\), which makes the true profile softer than eq. R.2.3's parabola near the anoxic boundary. The penetration depth is right to a factor of ~1.5.
  • There is no immune system in this model, and that is the biggest omission. Lung function in CF is destroyed largely by neutrophil-driven inflammation and proteases, not directly by bacterial numbers. A transport model can explain why the infection persists. It cannot explain why the lung fails.
  • Deposition is not modeled at all. \(f_{\text{dep}} = 0.5\) hides an entire field: droplet size distributions, airway branching, ventilation heterogeneity, and the awkward fact that aerosol preferentially reaches the airways that are least obstructed — the ones that need it least.
What the model does earn. Order-of-magnitude transport reasoning got us four true statements that no amount of intuition would have supplied: arrival is fast, oxygen fails at 28 µm, coverage above the biofilm threshold is ~2 % of the dosing interval, and a bacterium in mucus has exactly one exit. Those four survive every caveat above, because each depends on a ratio of scales rather than on a parameter value.

4.4 · Where this goes next

The case leaves one debt, and it is a big one: Step 6 found seven orders of magnitude between how often the drug arrives and how fast it acts, and blamed the membrane. That is a statement about binding and transport kinetics competing with diffusion, and it is the next module's entire subject — receptor occupancy, on- and off-rates, and the dimensionless number that decides whether a surface is transport-limited (diffusion cannot keep up) or reaction-limited (the surface cannot keep up with delivery), the Damköhler number. Every "not the bottleneck" verdict in the table above is a Damköhler statement waiting to be written properly.

Two other threads from this case return later in the course. The oxygen calculation of Step 4 is the same calculation that sets capillary spacing in every tissue you have (Part III), and the Péclet argument of Step 7 is the reason you have a circulatory system at all — the same equation, four orders of magnitude up in \(L\).
