1.1 · One cell wide

Where we are in the story. The bite is a few minutes old, and a pale bump is rising on the skin of the calf. Everything Module 7 built, the pressure, the fourth power, the wide tree, exists to bring blood close to every cell in the body for about a second. What happens in that second? Glucose and oxygen leave the blood; water leaves, crosses the tissue and finds its way back; and the bump on the skin rises through the same wall. This module is that wall: how it is built, what it lets through, and how the body decides how much blood each piece of it gets.

Lower the camera from Module 7's venule into the vessels that feed it. Module 7 §3.4 ended at the left end of its viscosity curve: as the tube narrows below the size of a red cell, the lubricating sleeve of plasma is squeezed to a film and the apparent viscosity climbs again, the inverse Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect. The capillary is that end of the curve. Its lumen is about 5–6 µm across (5.3 µm in human thigh muscle, Gidlöf 1988; Module 7's table used 6), and a red cell is 7.8 µm across, so the cell does not fit flat. It folds into a shape like a parachute, its rim dragged back by the wall, and the cells go through one at a time, in single file, with only a thin film of plasma between each cell and the wall.

The wall itself is a single layer of endothelial cells, and where the cell is thinnest, away from its nucleus, it is about 0.2–0.3 µm thick. So the oxygen carried by a red cell in a capillary is separated from the tissue by a fraction of a micron of plasma, a fraction of a micron of endothelial cell, and then a few microns of tissue fluid. Nowhere else in the circulation is the blood this close to anything.

How long does it stay? Module 7's tree table gave the capillary a mean speed of 0.30 mm/s and a transit of 1.7 s, by continuity: the body's whole flow divided by the cross-section of all its capillaries, an average over every organ. Measured in one bed, the skin, red cells in human capillaries move at about 0.65 mm/s, and the number varies: by about ±0.3 mm/s from one capillary to the next, and in the same capillary from moment to moment (Fagrell 1977). Continuity, read for one tube, says the time inside is its length over the speed:

eq. 7.2.1 again — continuity, \(\bar u = Q/A\), read for the time in one capillary $$\bar u = \frac{Q}{A} \quad\longrightarrow\quad t_{transit} = \frac{L}{\bar u} = \frac{0.5\ \text{mm}}{0.65\ \text{mm/s}} \approx 0.8\ \text{s}$$

with \(Q\) the flow through the tube, \(A\) its cross-section, \(\bar u\) the mean speed and \(L\) the length. Across the measured spread that is anything from about half a second (0.95 mm/s) to a second and a half (0.35 mm/s). Keep the range, not just the middle: §1.3 shows that what the blood gives up depends on exactly this time, so a capillary running fast gives up less of each solute than one running slow.

The closeness that cost Module 7 is what Module 8 spends. The scraping of folded cells against a one-cell wall is why the apparent viscosity rose again at the narrowest tubes. The same scraping is why the exchange works: the blood is pressed against the thinnest wall in the body, for most of a second.

1.2 · Three knobs for every millilitre of blood

Start with something you can make in a kitchen

Drop a tea bag into a cup of hot water. Colour leaves the bag through its paper wall, slowly at first. Three things decide how strong the cup gets, and you already know all three. A bigger bag, or two bags, gives the water in the cup more paper to touch (wall for the volume). Leaving it in longer lets more cross (time). A thinner or more open paper lets it out faster (how easily the wall passes it). Put the same bag in a bucket instead of a cup and the tea is weak: what counts is the wall for each cupful, not the wall alone. A dialysis machine is the same idea built by engineers to clean blood: thousands of hollow fibres, each a thin-walled tube, with blood running slowly inside and clean fluid outside.

The same three knobs, in Week 2's law

Week 2 already wrote what crosses a layer per second. Across a slab of thickness \(L\) with the concentration held at \(C_L\) on one side and \(C_R\) on the other, the steady flux (molecules per area per second) is

eq. 2.1.2 again — steady flux through a slab $$J = D\,\frac{C_L - C_R}{L}$$

with \(D\) the molecule's diffusion coefficient. Week 3 packaged the \(D\) over the thickness into one speed, the mass-transfer coefficient, which for a wall that is a film of water of thickness \(\delta\) is

eq. 3.3.8 again — how easily a wall passes a molecule, as a speed $$k = \frac{D}{\delta}$$

so the flux is \(J = k\,\Delta C\). Now follow one volume \(V\) of blood through a tube of radius \(r\). A tube's wall area per volume inside it is \(2\pi r L/(\pi r^2 L) = 2/r\), so in a time \(t\) that blood touches an area \((2/r)V\), and what crosses, per unit of volume and of concentration difference, is

$$\frac{\text{what crosses}}{V\,\Delta C} = k\times\frac{2}{r}\times t \;=\; z$$

three knobs multiplied: how easily the wall passes the molecule, how much wall each millilitre of blood touches, and for how long. Call the product \(z\), the exchange number. When \(z\) is well under one, about a fraction \(z\) of the difference gets across; when it is well over one, all of it does (§1.3's bench measures exactly how). A capillary's \(2/r\) is enormous: 0.67 m² of wall for every millilitre of blood, against the aorta's 1.6 cm².

The wall is not a film of water

Eq. 3.3.8 would make a 0.3 µm wall pass glucose at \(D/\delta = 9.2\times10^{-10}/0.3\times10^{-6} = 3\times10^{-3}\) m/s. The measured value is \(1.0\times10^{-7}\) m/s (Alvarez & Yudilevich 1969), thirty thousand times less. Glucose cannot dissolve in the cells' fatty membranes; it can only go between the cells, through narrow breaks in the junctions that seal them, and those breaks are a tiny fraction of the wall. Write that fraction into Week 3's speed:

eq. 3.3.8 again — with only a fraction of the wall open to the molecule $$k_w = f_{open}\,\frac{D}{\delta}$$

with \(k_w\) the wall's measured permeability for this molecule, \(f_{open}\) the fraction of its area that behaves as open water to it, \(D\) its diffusion coefficient and \(\delta\) the path through the wall. For glucose \(f_{open} \approx 3\times10^{-5}\): about one part in thirty thousand. To glucose, the capillary wall behaves like 9 mm of water, thicker than the aorta's whole wall. Thinness is not what makes the capillary good at passing glucose; wall for the volume is. Oxygen is different: it dissolves in the membranes and crosses the whole wall (\(f_{open} = 1\)), and for oxygen the thinness counts in full. §1.4 returns to \(f_{open}\), because it is the number a wall with windows changes.

Blood passing through the aorta gives up four hundredths of a per cent of its oxygen difference with the wall; for blood in a capillary the wall alone would let all of it go within the first few microns: the wall is not what limits oxygen there. The capillary wins on wall for the volume and, for oxygen, on thinness; the aorta wins on time, by about three. The aorta is built to carry, the capillary to touch.

  3. Per pass. A capillary: \(z = 3.1\times10^{-7}\times6.7\times10^{5}\times0.77 = 0.16\), so 15 % of the urea difference crosses. A fibre: \(z = 1.2\times10^{-5}\times2.16\times10^{4}\times13 = 3.4\), so 97 %. The machine wins on the wall and on time, and loses on wall for the volume by thirty-one times.
  4. The whole bed against the whole machine. Module 7's tree puts the capillaries' cross-section at 2 800 cm², so their wall, \(A\times2L/r\), is about 93 m² (the whole endothelium, every vessel included, is about 350 m², Pries & Kuebler 2006). The dialyser is a better wall used slowly: each pass nearly empties its blood of urea. The capillary bed is a leakier-looking but worse wall for urea, spread over fifty times the area and run for every second of a life. Clearing a patient's blood for an afternoon, three times a week, is the machine making up with time what it lacks in area.
Check yourself

1. Exercise shortens the capillary transit from about a second to half a second. What happens to \(z\) for each millilitre, and does the tissue get less glucose?

Answer

\(z\) halves, so each millilitre gives up about half as large a fraction of a wall-limited solute; but twice as many millilitres pass each second, so the delivery per second is about the same. To deliver more, the wall must pass more (a larger \(k_w\), §1.3's insulin) or more wall must carry blood. Whether working muscle starts blood through capillaries that carried none at rest is still argued: red cells were seen entering more capillaries in contracting hamster muscle (Gorczynski 1978), while a 2023 review of the evidence calls this "capillary recruitment" a myth, with most capillaries already flowing at rest (Poole & Musch 2023).

2. Fluid collects in the tissue around a capillary and pushes the nearest cells 20 µm further from it. Which part of the journey has changed?

Answer

Not the wall: the path beyond it. What a molecule has to cross after the wall is longer; §3.2 measures what that does to oxygen, and Tab 2 is where the fluid comes from.

Blood flows through it at \(Q\) (volume per second); the solute's concentration is \(C(x)\) coming in and \(C(x) + dC\) going out, and the tissue just outside holds it at \(C_t\).

2 · What comes in, minus what goes out, is what leaked through the wall

In steady state nothing piles up in the slice. The solute carried in per second is \(Q\,C(x)\); carried out, \(Q\,(C + dC)\); and through the slice's wall, whose area is \(2\pi r\,dx\), the wall's speed \(k_w\) from §1.2 gives \(k_w\,2\pi r\,dx\,(C - C_t)\). So

$$Q\,C - Q\,(C + dC) = k_w\,2\pi r\,dx\,(C - C_t)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dC}{dx} = -\frac{2\pi r\,k_w}{Q}\,(C - C_t)$$

3 · The rate of loss is proportional to what is left, so the fall is exponential

The concentration difference \(C - C_t\) falls at a rate proportional to itself, the same shape as Week 3's survival curve. Integrate from the entrance, where \(C = C_{in}\), and write \(Q = \pi r^2\bar u\):

eq. 8.1.1 — extraction along a capillary (Renkin–Crone) $$\frac{C(x) - C_t}{C_{in} - C_t} = e^{-2k_w x/(r\bar u)},\qquad f_{ex} = 1 - e^{-k_w A_w/Q}$$

The left-hand form is the curve the bench's counts draw: the fraction of the entering difference \(C_{in} - C_t\) still in the blood at distance \(x\). The right-hand form is the same curve read at the exit, \(x = L\), where the wall's whole area is \(A_w = 2\pi r L\): the extraction \(f_{ex}\), the fraction of that difference that crossed before the blood left. For the bench's tracer, which the tissue holds none of (\(C_t = 0\)), it is simply the fraction of the tracer that left. The exponent \(k_wA_w/Q = 2k_wL/(r\bar u)\) is §1.2's exchange number \(z\), with \(L/\bar u\) the time. The letters: \(k_w\) the wall's permeability for this solute (m/s), \(r\) the capillary's radius, \(\bar u\) the blood's speed, \(Q\) the flow, \(A_w\) the wall's area. Renkin (1959) and Crone (1963) wrote it for a whole organ, \(E = 1 - e^{-PS/F}\), with the organ's permeability–surface product \(PS\) in place of \(k_wA_w\) and its blood flow \(F\) in place of \(Q\) (some authors use the plasma flow; this page uses the blood flow).

4 · Read the two limits

If \(z\) is large, the exponential has died long before the exit and \(f_{ex}\to1\): the blood has come all the way to the tissue's level early in the capillary, and the only way to deliver more is to bring more blood. That is called flow-limited. Oxygen is the extreme case: the wall does not hold it back at all (\(z\approx4\,000\)), so on the wall alone its difference with the tissue would be gone in the first microns; what does limit it lies past the wall, in the tissue (Tab 3). If \(z\) is small, \(e^{-z}\approx1-z\) and \(f_{ex}\approx k_wA_w/Q\): only a small fraction gets out, in proportion to the time the blood spends there. That is permeability-limited (wall-limited).

The wall sorts the traffic, but not by sieving. Per capillary at the measured speed, urea gets 15 % of the way out, glucose 5 %, inulin 1.4 %, albumin 0.03 %. From urea (60 Da) to inulin (5 200 Da) the ratio of permeability to diffusion coefficient is "the same for all these substances" (Alvarez & Yudilevich 1969): within that range the openings are wide enough for all of them, and \(k_w\) falls only because bigger molecules diffuse more slowly. Sieving begins at the size of the proteins, at the sugar coat over the openings and in the wall's few larger gaps (Renkin 1992: "many openings 8 nm and a few 40 to 60 nm wide"), which is why albumin, thirteen times inulin's weight, crosses about forty times less readily.
Derive the e-fold length, and why it is the capillary's own ruler

Write the exponent of eq. 8.1.1 as \(x/\ell\) with \(\ell = r\bar u/(2k_w)\). After a length \(\ell\) the difference has fallen to \(1/e\), 37 %; after \(3\ell\), to 5 %. A capillary whose length \(L\) is several \(\ell\) long empties the solute; one much shorter than \(\ell\) barely touches it.

For glucose at 0.65 mm/s, \(\ell = (3\ \mu\text{m}\times0.65\ \text{mm/s})/(2\times1.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m/s}) = 9.8\) mm, twenty times the capillary's length: a fast skin capillary barely dents the blood's glucose. For urea \(\ell\) is 3.1 mm; for albumin, a metre and a half.

Check yourself

1. A solute's extraction is 3 % at rest and the tissue holds none of it. The flow through the capillary doubles. Roughly what is the extraction now, and does the tissue receive more of it per second?

Answer

In the wall-limited limit \(f_{ex}\approx k_wA_w/Q\), so doubling \(Q\) halves it, to about 1.5 %. The delivery per second is \(Q\,f_{ex}\,(C_{in} - C_t)\), and \(Q\,f_{ex} \approx k_wA_w\) is unchanged: for a wall-limited solute, more flow alone does not help. 2. Why does a tissue that is working hard get more glucose from each millilitre of blood than a resting one, with the same wall?

Answer

Its cells take glucose faster, which lowers the tissue's own level \(C_t\). Eq. 8.1.1 acts on \(C_{in} - C_t\), so a lower \(C_t\) means a bigger difference and a bigger net delivery for the same \(f_{ex}\).

1.4 · Three walls, and why some have windows

Start with a screen door

A screen door lets the summer breeze straight through and keeps the insects out. It manages both because it is mostly holes, and every hole is covered by a mesh finer than an insect. The body builds some of its capillaries the same way. Everything so far has been the capillary of muscle and skin, whose cells are joined edge to edge with only narrow breaks between them. But the kidney, the gut and the glands that make hormones have capillaries whose cells are punched through with windows, each covered by a thin film: a screen door for plasma.

1 · The three walls

Continuous (muscle, skin, heart, lung; the brain's are sealed tighter still): the endothelial cells are joined edge to edge, and water and small molecules cross only through narrow breaks in those joins, behind the sugar coat on the wall's inner face. This is §1.2's wall, open to glucose over about one part in thirty thousand of its area. Fenestrated (the kidney's glomeruli and the capillaries around its tubules, the gut's villi, the hormone glands such as the pancreatic islets, thyroid, adrenal cortex and pituitary, and the choroid plexus that makes the brain's fluid; Kamba 2006; Satchell & Braet 2009): the cell is thinned and punched through with windows, the fenestrae, 60–70 nm across, most of them covered by a diaphragm 3–5 nm thick (Satchell & Braet 2009). Sinusoidal (liver, spleen, bone marrow): gaps of 100–175 nm with no film over them, covering 6–8 % of the liver's endothelial surface (Braet & Wisse 2002), wide enough for plasma proteins and even the particles that carry fat in the blood to reach the liver's cells.

2 · What the windows change, and what they do not

Measured across many tissues, a wall's hydraulic conductivity and its permeability to small molecules both rise with the density of its windows (Levick & Smaje 1987). A window conducts water so well that "a single fenestra (diam 0.06 micron, area 0.003 micron²) has a greater conductance than 1 micron² of continuous (skeletal muscle) endothelium": per unit of its own area it passes water about 380 times as easily as the continuous wall. A wall that is a fraction \(f_{fen}\) windows passes water at

$$L_p = (1 - f_{fen})\,L_{p,\,cont} + f_{fen}\,K_{fen} = 0.95\times10^{-7} + 0.05\times3.8\times10^{-5} = 2.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{cm/(s·mmHg)}$$

for a wall one-twentieth windows (a fraction the page declares; the measured fractions vary from organ to organ): twenty times the continuous wall. For small molecules the measurements say the same direction, "more permeable to small ions and molecules than are continuous endothelia", without a single factor; the bench declares ten for glucose, which in §1.3's capillary moves its extraction from 5 % to 40 % per pass. And then the part that makes the windows worth having: "their permeabilities to plasma proteins are about the same" (Renkin 1992). The diaphragm and the sugar coat still hold albumin back. The windows open the wall to water and small molecules and keep it shut to protein, and Tab 2 shows why the protein has to stay in.

3 · Why a body would want windows

Fenestrae sit where an organ's job is moving water and small molecules in bulk. The kidney's glomeruli filter the plasma to make urine: 107.6 mL a minute in healthy adults (per 1.73 m² of body surface, measured in living kidney donors, Poggio 2009), about 155 litres a day, twenty times what all the body's other capillaries filter (§2.3). The gut's villi take a meal's water and sugars into the blood; the hormone glands send what they make out into it. These are also the places where the blood takes water back steadily, which the rest of the body's capillaries do not (§2.3): "steady state fluid uptake into plasma only occurs in tissues such as intestinal mucosa and renal peri-tubular capillaries" (Michel, Woodcock & Curry 2020).

What the windows cost. They have to be maintained and they have to stay sealed to protein. Mice whose fenestrae lack the diaphragm protein PV-1 keep their windows but leak: "a major leak of plasma proteins", and they die of protein loss through the gut (Stan 2012). And fenestrated capillaries need the growth factor VEGF to survive: one to three weeks of VEGF blockade made them regress in the islets, thyroid, adrenal cortex, pituitary, choroid plexus and intestinal villi, and those that survived had fewer windows (Kamba 2006).

One number, three walls. Every wall in the body is eq. 3.3.8 with a different open fraction. The continuous wall opens one part in thirty thousand to glucose; the fenestrated wall cuts windows that multiply its water traffic by tens and keeps them screened against protein; the sinusoid opens everything. The body chooses the wall by the organ's job.

2.1 · Two pressures across one wall

Start with a gummy bear

Leave a gummy bear in a glass of water overnight and in the morning it is several times its size. Sprinkle salt on slices of cucumber and ten minutes later they are sitting in a puddle. In both, water moved across a barrier with nobody pushing it: into the bear, where the sugar and gelatin are, and out of the cucumber, towards the salt. Water moves towards the side with more dissolved stuff that cannot follow it.

Tab 1 ended with the wall holding albumin back. That makes the blood the gummy bear: it carries proteins the wall keeps in, so water is drawn towards it. And Module 7 gave the blood a pressure of its own, 35 mmHg entering the capillary and 15 leaving, which pushes water out. If the blood pressure were the whole story, water would leave every capillary, everywhere, all the time, and nothing would ever bring it back. The protein's pull is what makes a way back possible. 1 · The pull, measured as a height

Put plasma in a bag whose wall water crosses but protein cannot, stand it in water, and fit a tall tube to its top. Water flows into the bag and up the tube until the weight of the column above the bag pushes back as hard as the protein pulls; then it stops. That stopping pressure, \(\rho g h\) of the column, is the osmotic pressure \(\Pi\): the pressure you would have to apply to the protein side to keep water from entering it. Week 1 counted molecules by the pressure they put on a wall, \(PV = nRT\) (eq. 1.1.3). Measured this way, a protein the wall holds back follows the same law with its concentration \(n/V\) in place of the gas's (van 't Hoff, 1887):

eq. 8.2.1 — osmotic pressure (van 't Hoff) $$\Pi = C\,R\,T$$

with \(\Pi\) the osmotic pressure (Pa), \(C\) the concentration of the molecules the wall holds back (mol/m³), \(R\) = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and \(T\) the temperature. Water flows towards the side where \(\Pi\) is higher, and it takes a pressure of \(\Pi\) on that side to stop it. Plasma carries about 40 g of albumin per litre, molecules of 66,348 Da (Merlot 2014): \(C = 0.60\) mol/m³, so eq. 8.2.1 gives \(\Pi = 0.60\times8.314\times310 = 1\,550\) Pa \(= 11.7\) mmHg, a column of water 16 cm tall. The proteins' share of the osmotic pressure is called the oncotic (or colloid osmotic) pressure, and it is the one that matters at a capillary wall: small molecules like salt and glucose cross that wall freely (§1.3), end up on both sides at about the same concentration, and pull neither way.

Albumin alone under-counts. The measured colloid osmotic pressure of plasma is about 27 mmHg (26.9 in healthy people, Noddeland 1982), a 37 cm column, of which albumin is responsible for about 80 %, some 21 mmHg: van 't Hoff on albumin alone falls nearly half short. Two things it leaves out. Albumin is charged, so it holds extra small ions on its side of the wall, and those count too (the Gibbs–Donnan effect, worth several mmHg); and at this crowding the molecules are not the ideal, point-like gas eq. 8.2.1 assumes. The law gets the order of magnitude and the direction; the page uses the measured 26.9.

2 · The balance: add up the four pressures

Four pressures act across the wall. Two push water out of the blood: the blood's own pressure \(P_c\), and the tissue fluid's oncotic pressure \(\Pi_t\) (the few proteins that have leaked out pull water towards themselves). Two push water in: the tissue fluid's pressure \(P_t\), and the plasma's oncotic pressure \(\Pi_c\). The wall is not a perfect barrier to protein, so only a fraction \(f_r\) of the oncotic difference acts on it: the fraction of the protein the wall reflects, between 0 (protein crosses freely, so pulls on nothing) and 1 (protein held back completely). Water crosses in proportion to the net, with the wall's hydraulic conductivity \(L_p\) as the constant; Landis measured exactly this relation, filtration against pressure, in single capillaries (Landis 1927), and Starling had argued for the balance in 1896:

eq. 8.2.2 — Starling filtration $$J_v = L_p\Big[\big(P_c - P_t\big) - f_r\,\big(\Pi_c - \Pi_t\big)\Big]$$

with \(J_v\) the water flux, volume per wall area per second (m/s; positive means out of the blood), \(L_p\) how easily water goes through the wall, and the four pressures as above. Textbooks write \(f_r\) as \(\sigma\); this course keeps \(\sigma\) for stress. Blood pressure alone is the first bracket; it would push water out everywhere. The second bracket is the osmotic pull of §2.1's column, and it is the only thing that can turn the flow round.

2.2 · The tissue pushes back: a spring, a leak, and a swelling

Water that leaves the blood does not vanish: it fills the space between the tissue's cells, and the tissue pushes back. Normally the lymph carries the filtrate away as fast as it arrives (§2.3), so nothing moves. Then the bite. Histamine opens the venules, the 7–8 µm vessels just after the capillaries: within minutes their endothelial cells pull apart, leaving gaps 0.1–0.8 µm wide (Majno & Palade 1961). Measured in single venules, histamine raises \(L_p\) about three-fold, peaking 6–9 minutes after it arrives, and the leaking protein lowers the effective protein pull to about 0.7 of its resting value (Michel & Kendall 1997); the gaps are gone by about three hours (Majno & Palade 1961). 1 · The tissue is a spring, in pressure and volume

Squeeze more fluid into a fixed amount of tissue and its pressure rises, the way a balloon's does, or the way Week 6's gel pushed back when it was stretched. It is Week 5's spring, with pressure playing force and fractional swelling playing stretch:

eq. 5.5.1 again — Hooke, the tissue as a spring in pressure and volume $$F = k\,x \quad\longrightarrow\quad P_t = P_{t0} + K_t\left(\frac{V}{V_0} - 1\right)$$

with \(P_{t0}\) the tissue's pressure at its normal volume \(V_0\), \(V\) its swollen volume, and \(K_t\) its stiffness in pressure per fractional swelling (mmHg). For skin near its normal hydration, measurements in rats give a compliance of about 5 mL per 100 g of tissue per mmHg (Wiig & Lund 2001), a 5 % swelling for each mmHg, so \(K_t \approx 20\) mmHg; F25's homework used 10. Real tissue is straight like this only near normal. Once it is overfilled, "the maximal increase in IFP during overhydration was approximately 2 mmHg" (Wiig & Reed 1987): the pressure stops rising while the volume goes on growing, the spring gives way, and that is how oedema can become large.

2 · Put the spring into Starling, and the swelling has somewhere to go

Substitute eq. 5.5.1 into eq. 8.2.2. As the tissue fills, \(P_t\) climbs, and \(P_t\) is one of the terms pushing water in. So filtration slows as the tissue swells, and it would stop when the bracket reaches zero:

$$\big(P_c - P_t\big) - f_r\big(\Pi_c - \Pi_t\big) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{V_{end}}{V_0} = 1 + \frac{(P_c - P_{t0}) - f_r(\Pi_c - \Pi_t)}{K_t}$$

The tissue heads for the volume at which its own pressure has risen by the whole of the net push. A stiff tissue (large \(K_t\)) heads for a small swelling; a soft one for a large one.

3 · And it takes time, with Week 6's clock

Water arrives through all the wall in the tissue, \(S_VV_0\) of it, where \(S_V\) is the capillary wall area per volume of tissue: \(dV/dt = S_VV_0\,J_v\). Write \(J_v\) with eq. 5.5.1 inside it and the swelling still to come shrinks at a rate proportional to itself, \(dV/dt = S_VL_pK_t\,(V_{end} - V)\). That is Week 6's Kelvin–Voigt element, a spring and a damper side by side, under a step of load:

eq. 6.2.4 again — Kelvin–Voigt, a solid with a delay $$F = k\,x + \eta\,\dot x$$

Here the tissue is the spring, \(k \leftrightarrow K_t\), and the wall is the damper, \(\eta \leftrightarrow 1/(S_VL_p)\), a resistance to water. Under a step the volume creeps to its end with one clock, \(\tau = \eta/k\):

$$V(t) = V_{end} + (V_0 - V_{end})\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = \frac{\eta}{k} = \frac{1}{S_V\,L_p\,K_t}$$

The clock is short when the wall is leaky (large \(L_p\)), when the tissue is crowded with capillaries (large \(S_V\)), or when the tissue is stiff (large \(K_t\)).

2.3 · The way back: lymph, and when the capillary takes water in

Look back at the ledger in §2.1. Along the whole length of a resting capillary the net push is outward: nowhere inward. If nothing removed the water, every tissue would slowly fill. Over the whole body, about 8 litres of fluid a day leave the capillaries this way (Renkin 1986, as cited by Moore & Bertram 2018), the whole plasma volume about every nine hours (Breslin 2018). That water comes back to the blood by three roads.

The lymph, for most of the body. Blind-ended, thin-walled lymphatic vessels start in the tissue, collect the fluid and the protein that have leaked out, and carry them back to the veins near the heart. In most tissues, most of the time, this is the only road: the capillary filters a little along its length, and the lymph closes the circuit. Lymphatic vessels have about a tenfold reserve over their normal flow, and their flow rises as the tissue's pressure rises (Breslin 2018), one of the body's defences against swelling.

Back into the capillary, for a while. Wherever the blood pressure falls below the protein's pull, eq. 8.2.2's bracket turns negative and the capillary takes water in. It does not last: as the capillary draws water out of the tissue, the protein left behind in the fluid around it concentrates and pulls back ("ISF colloid osmotic pressure (COP) rises as J(v) falls", Levick & Michel 2010), so "fluid uptake is transient, being rapid at first then attenuating". The clearest case is a bleed: "the rapid overall decline in capillary pressure after acute blood loss initiates rapid fluid uptake from tissue to plasma, that is, autotransfusion" (Michel, Woodcock & Curry 2020). The tissues give water back to the blood when the blood needs it, and blood pressure alone could never explain that.

Back into the capillary, steadily. Only where a neighbouring epithelium keeps pouring protein-free fluid into the tissue, which keeps the tissue's protein pull low: the gut's lining and the capillaries around the kidney's tubules (Michel, Woodcock & Curry 2020), the fenestrated beds of §1.4.

The bite drains by the first two roads. When the histamine wears off, the junctions close: \(L_p\) and \(f_r\) return to their resting values. The swollen tissue's own pressure, now +1.3 mmHg, pushes back on every capillary and venule under it. At the venule, where the push was only 0.9 mmHg outward, it turns inward, to −0.8 mmHg: water goes back into the blood, for a while. Along the rest of the bed the filtration is turned down, while the lymph runs on at its resting rate and carries away the difference. The bump relaxes back to \(V_0\) on eq. 6.2.4's clock with the closed wall's \(L_p\) as the damper: \(\tau = 1/(58\times1.0\times10^{-7}\times20) = 8\,600\) s, about 2.4 hours. The extra lymph flow the swollen tissue calls up only shortens it. Histamine in the bitten skin is back to baseline within two hours (Horsmanheimo 1996), and the bump fades over hours.

That is the right answer to its own assumptions, and the assumption that makes it slow is the wall area: it gave the region 2.2 cm² of capillary wall per cm³, against the 58 measured in muscle, and at the measured area the same push clears it in about 16 hours. The revised Starling picture adds that a push back into the blood does not last, and that the lymph, which the homework left out, does the lasting work.

Draining: the same spring, the closed wall's \(L_p\), hours, with the lymph carrying the water away. Week 6's solid with a delay, in a bump on the skin.

3.1 · Oxygen arrives on Week 3's curve

Oxygen barely dissolves in water. Blood carries it anyway, about sixty-five times more than plasma alone could hold, because each red cell is packed with haemoglobin, a protein with four sites that each bind one oxygen molecule. Week 3 already taught what a protein with several sites that help one another does: the Hill isotherm, eq. 3.2.11, the switch. Haemoglobin is the textbook example of it. Write the concentration of oxygen as its partial pressure \(P\), which is how physiologists measure it, and the half-point as \(P_{50}\), the pressure at which half the sites are full:

eq. 3.2.11 again — Hill, for haemoglobin, in oxygen pressure $$\theta = \frac{(K_{eq}C)^n}{1 + (K_{eq}C)^n} \quad\longrightarrow\quad S = \frac{(P/P_{50})^n}{1 + (P/P_{50})^n}$$

with \(S\) the saturation (the fraction of haemoglobin's sites holding oxygen, Week 3's \(\theta\)), \(P\) the oxygen pressure in mmHg, \(P_{50} \approx 26.8\) mmHg, and \(n \approx 2.7\), the steepness. \(K_{eq}C\) of Week 3 has become \(P/P_{50}\).

Pressure to concentration: Henry's law, in one line. The amount of oxygen dissolved in plasma is proportional to its pressure: 0.003 mL of oxygen per decilitre of blood for every mmHg. At the 100 mmHg of arterial blood that is 0.3 mL/dL. Haemoglobin carries the rest: each gram holds at most 1.34 mL, and blood has about 15 g per decilitre. So the oxygen content of blood (F25's line) is

$$C_{O_2} = 1.34\,[\text{Hb}]\,S + 0.003\,P\qquad(\text{mL O}_2\ \text{per dL of blood})$$

Arterial blood, at 100 mmHg, is 97 % saturated and carries about 19.8 mL of oxygen per decilitre; only 0.3 of that is dissolved. Henry's line is what turns the pressure on the curve's axis into the concentration that Week 2's flux and §3.2's diffusion need. The capillary wall does not hold oxygen back (§1.3), so as the blood passes it gives oxygen up as fast as the tissue beyond can take it, and the curve says how much of the haemoglobin's load comes off for each fall in pressure.

Why the curve is a switch, and why that matters. Compare haemoglobin's \(n = 2.7\) with a protein whose sites do not help one another (\(n = 1\), Week 3's Langmuir) at the same half-point. In the lung, at 100 mmHg, haemoglobin fills to 97 %; the Langmuir protein only to 79 %. In a hard-working muscle, at about 20 mmHg, haemoglobin has let go down to 31 %, the Langmuir protein only to 43 %. Between the lung and the working muscle, haemoglobin delivers 66 points of saturation and the Langmuir protein 36: cooperation nearly doubles what each red cell carries to work. Week 3 called this the switch; here it is the reason the switch exists.

3.2 · How far oxygen reaches

Oxygen that has left the capillary still has to reach the cells, and every cell it passes eats some on the way. R1 met this problem in the mucus of a cystic-fibrosis airway: oxygen diffusing into a layer whose bacteria eat it reaches only a limited depth, \(\delta = \sqrt{2DC_s/Q}\) (eq. R.2.3), set by diffusion over consumption. R1's \(Q\) was the oxygen eaten per second by each millilitre of the layer. It is the same quantity here, the oxygen each millilitre of tissue uses per second, and because \(Q\) is now the blood flow this module writes it \(\dot V_{O_2}/V\): the dot for a rate, as in §3.3's \(\dot V_{O_2}\) for the whole muscle, divided by the tissue's volume. Wrap R1's layer around a capillary and you have the problem Krogh solved in 1919: each capillary feeds a cylinder of tissue around it, and the question is how wide a cylinder one capillary can keep supplied.

1 · Take a shell of tissue at radius r

This is Module 7 §1.2's cylinder again, with oxygen in place of momentum. The capillary has radius \(r_c\) and feeds the tissue out to radius \(R_K\), beyond which the next capillary takes over. Every bit of tissue eats oxygen at the same rate \(\dot V_{O_2}/V\) (millilitres of oxygen per millilitre of tissue per second). Draw a cylinder of radius \(r\) around the capillary, one unit long.

2 · What diffuses out through the cylinder feeds everything outside it

In steady state, the oxygen diffusing outward through the cylinder's surface, \(2\pi r\) per unit length, is exactly what the tissue between \(r\) and \(R_K\) eats, \((\dot V_{O_2}/V)\,\pi(R_K^2 - r^2)\). Fick's law, written in pressure with Henry's solubility \(\alpha\) turning pressure into concentration, gives the flux as \(-D\alpha\,dP/dr\):

$$-D\alpha\,\frac{dP}{dr}\,2\pi r = \frac{\dot V_{O_2}}{V}\,\pi\big(R_K^2 - r^2\big)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{\dot V_{O_2}/V}{2D\alpha}\left(\frac{R_K^2}{r} - r\right)$$

3 · Integrate from the capillary wall outward

Start at the wall, \(P(r_c) = P_c\), and integrate:

$$P(r) = P_c - \frac{\dot V_{O_2}/V}{2D\alpha}\left[R_K^2\ln\frac{r}{r_c} - \frac{r^2 - r_c^2}{2}\right]$$

That is the profile the bench's solve settles to and plots.

4 · The pressure the cylinder costs

Set \(r = R_K\): the drop in oxygen pressure from the capillary to the edge of its cylinder is

eq. 8.3.1 — Krogh's cylinder: the oxygen pressure a capillary spends feeding radius \(R_K\) $$P_c - P(R_K) = \frac{\dot V_{O_2}/V}{4D\alpha}\left[2R_K^2\ln\frac{R_K}{r_c} - R_K^2 + r_c^2\right]$$

with \(P_c\) the oxygen pressure in the capillary (mmHg), \(P(R_K)\) at the cylinder's edge, \(\dot V_{O_2}/V\) the tissue's consumption, \(D\) oxygen's diffusion coefficient in tissue and \(\alpha\) its solubility there, \(r_c\) the capillary's radius and \(R_K\) the cylinder's. Setting \(P(R_K) = 0\) and solving for \(R_K\) gives the reach: the widest cylinder the capillary can keep supplied. The bracket grows roughly as \(R_K^2\), so, like R1's depth, the reach shrinks roughly as the square root of the consumption: four times the work and the reach falls by 1.8.

R1's depth is inside it. When the cylinder is thin beside the capillary, \(R_K - r_c \ll r_c\), the bracket shrinks to \(2(R_K - r_c)^2\), and setting the drop equal to \(P_c\) gives \(R_K - r_c = \sqrt{2D\alpha P_c/(\dot V_{O_2}/V)}\): R1's \(\delta = \sqrt{2DC_s/Q}\) with \(C_s = \alpha P_c\). Krogh's cylinder is R1's layer wrapped around a tube.

  2. At rest. Resting thigh muscle uses 0.11 mL of oxygen per minute per 100 g (Heinonen 2013), \(\dot V_{O_2}/V = 1.9\times10^{-5}\) mL per mL per second. Eq. 8.3.1 at \(R_K = 36\) µm costs only 0.3 mmHg; with 40 mmHg in the capillary the reach is about 300 µm, eight to ten times the cylinder's radius, seventy to ninety times its area.
  3. At full effort. Maximally working knee-extensor muscle uses about 0.35 L of oxygen per minute per kg (Andersen & Saltin 1985), \(\dot V_{O_2}/V = 6.2\times10^{-3}\), over three hundred times rest. Eq. 8.3.1 at \(R_K = 31\) µm now costs 60 mmHg, and the capillaries hold only about 38 mmHg at this effort (Richardson 1995): the reach is 26 µm, and the model starves 32 % of a 31 µm cylinder and 48 % of a 36 µm one.
  4. What measured muscle does. At that effort the oxygen inside the fibres sits at about 3 mmHg (Richardson 1995), and no anoxic tissue has been found: "neither radial nor longitudinal intracellular PimO2 gradients or anoxic loci (i.e., 0 mmHg) have been detected" (Poole & Musch 2023). The pressure falls steeply, from 38 in the capillary to 3, across the short step from the red cell to the fibre's membrane, "consistent with an O₂ diffusion limitation across the 1-5-microns path-length from red cell to the sarcolemma" (Richardson 1995); inside the fibre, helped by myoglobin, the oxygen spreads with almost no gradient at all.
At rest Krogh's cylinder says each capillary could feed seventy times the tissue it is given; at full effort it says a third to a half of every cylinder should run out of oxygen. Real muscle does not run out. The model is right that at full effort diffusion is close to the limit, and wrong about where: the steep step is between the red cell and the fibre, and inside the fibre the oxygen is carried almost evenly. That is the part of Krogh's 1919 picture the measurements have revised.

3.3 · Supply must match demand

Demand, counted from Week 4's motors. A calf or thigh muscle is Week 4's myosin motors by the trillion, and every cycle of a myosin head burns one ATP (eqs. 4.2.1–4.2.3). The muscle makes that ATP almost entirely by burning oxygen: about 2.5 ATP per oxygen atom (Hinkle 2005), so about five per molecule of O₂. Working flat out, a kilogram of muscle uses about 350 mL of oxygen a minute (Andersen & Saltin 1985), which is 1.3 mmol of ATP per second per kilogram; spread over the muscle's myosin heads (about 0.2 mM of them, a frog-muscle figure, Pertici 2023) that is six or seven cycles per head per second, against the twenty a second Week 4's myosin can manage. At rest the same muscle uses 0.11 mL per 100 g per minute (Heinonen 2013), three hundred times less. The bench feeds the muscle at each demand and reads what comes out.

3.4 · Smooth muscle turns the tap

Start with your own skin

Plunge a hand into icy water and within a minute the fingers go pale; take it out into a warm room and they flush red. Climb a few flights of stairs and your thighs grow warm. No pipe was replaced. Every arteriole is wrapped in a ring of muscle, and the ring tightens or lets go.

An arteriole's wall is a lining of endothelium wrapped in one or two layers of smooth-muscle cells running round it. Each cell is Week 4's actin and myosin, arranged to pull slowly and to hold its pull cheaply: once contracted, many of its myosin heads stay attached without cycling, "an attached, noncycling cross-bridge (latch-bridge) which is responsible for the high economy of force maintenance in this tissue" (Dillon 1981). The ring shortens and the lumen narrows; it relaxes and the blood's pressure opens it. "The smooth muscle cell directly drives the contraction of the vascular wall and hence regulates the size of the blood vessel lumen" (Brozovich 2016).

Three things tell the ring what to do. Stretch: raise the pressure inside an arteriole and its muscle answers by contracting, the myogenic response, "critically important for the development of resting vascular tone, upon which other control mechanisms exert vasodilator and vasoconstrictor influences" (Davis & Hill 1999). The tissue's own work: working muscle fibres release products that relax the rings near them; many have been found and "no one dominant dilator mechanism accounts for exercise hyperemia" (Joyner & Casey 2015). Nerves: sympathetic nerves tighten the rings; in the skin they are how the body shuts down the flow in the cold (Charkoudian 2003), and in working muscle the tissue's own signals partly overrule them: "sympathetic constriction of proximal arterioles and feed arteries can restrict functional hyperemia while dilation prevails in distal arterioles" (Segal 2005). Module 9 takes these cells further. This section asks the engineering question: how much can turning one ring do?

1 · A bed is pipes in series

Module 7 §2.2 joined its pipes with Week 6's two rules: in series the flow is shared and the resistances add. A muscle's bed, from the artery that feeds it to the vein that drains it, is its levels in series: \(R_{h,bed} = R_{h,a} + R_{h,rest}\), where \(R_{h,a}\) is the resistance of the vessels that can open (the arterioles, and the small feed arteries above them) and \(R_{h,rest}\) everything else. By eq. 7.1.4 a vessel's resistance goes as the inverse fourth power of its radius, so widening the vessels that open from \(R_{rest}\) to \(R_{work}\) divides their resistance by \((R_{work}/R_{rest})^4\). Write \(f_a = R_{h,a}/R_{h,bed}\) at rest, the share of the bed's resistance those vessels hold, and the flow through the bed changes by

eq. 8.3.3 — the bed's flow gain $$\frac{Q_{work}}{Q_{rest}} = \frac{\Delta P_{work}/\Delta P_{rest}}{(1 - f_a) + f_a\,(R_{rest}/R_{work})^4}$$

with \(Q\) the bed's flow, \(\Delta P\) the pressure across it, \(f_a\) the share of its resting resistance in vessels that open, and \(R_{rest}/R_{work}\) their radius ratio. Two limits. If the vessels that open are the whole bed (\(f_a = 1\)), it is Module 7's single pipe: the gain is the fourth power of the widening. If they open without limit, the gain stops at \((\Delta P_{work}/\Delta P_{rest})/(1 - f_a)\): whatever cannot open caps it.

2 · What the working thigh demands

Module 7's tree, averaged over the whole body, has the arterioles spending 45 of the 70 mmHg lost between the arterioles' entrance and the venules' exit: \(f_a = 0.64\). On that bed, opening the arterioles however far gains at most 2.8 times. A muscle that gains a hundred and fourteen times cannot be an average bed. Put in the measurements: the flow rose 114 times (§3.3) while the mean arterial pressure rose from 100 to 130 mmHg (Andersen & Saltin 1985), so the bed's resistance must have fallen 87 times, and eq. 8.3.3 says that is possible only if

$$f_a \ge 1 - \frac{\Delta P_{work}/\Delta P_{rest}}{Q_{work}/Q_{rest}} = 1 - \frac{1.3}{114} = 0.989$$

At rest, at least 98.9 % of the muscle's resistance must sit in vessels that can open: a resting muscle's arterioles and feed arteries are held nearly shut. That is what tone is, and tone at rest is what gives the tap its range. Even then the vessels must widen at least \((114/1.3)^{1/4} = 3.1\) times; if 99.5 % of the resistance can open, 3.5 times.

Measured. One contraction lasting a fifth of a second, in hamster muscle, widened the smallest arterioles by 36 % and the feed arteries above them by 11 % within 15–20 seconds (VanTeeffelen & Segal 2006). And the widening has to travel: release a dilator onto one distal arteriole segment and the muscle's flow did not change, but a dilator whose signal spread upstream along the vessel wall, cell to cell, raised the flow into the branches by 25–80 % (Kurjiaka & Segal 1995). The rings work as a group.

3 · Beds in parallel share one pressure

Every organ hangs off the same arterial pressure, in parallel, by Module 7's other rule: opening one bed's arterioles draws flow into it, and the heart must pump more. A single small muscle can ask for a lot: one leg's knee extensors, 2.3 kg of muscle, took 5.7 L/min at full effort (Andersen & Saltin 1985), more than the whole heart pumps at rest. All of the body's muscle could not be opened like that at once: "the maximal vascular conductance of the musculature exceeds the maximal pumping capacity of the heart", so in hard whole-body exercise "vasodilatation is restrained by the sympathetic system", to keep the pressure up (Calbet & Lundby 2012). The skin is the bed the body uses most to ration: from "minimal levels" when it is cold to "6 to 8 L/min during hyperthermia" (Charkoudian 2003). Blood goes where the rings let it, and the body chooses: the working muscle wins, and when it must, the skin of the hand loses. That is why your hands go cold.

Resistance is a property of the muscle in the wall, not of the pipe. Module 7 treated each vessel as a fixed radius. The radius is set, moment to moment, by a ring of smooth muscle answering stretch, the tissue's work and the nerves; and because a bed's other resistances cap what widening can do, the tap has range only because the rings are held nearly shut at rest.

    $$\frac{Q_{work}}{Q_{rest}} = \frac{1}{0.36 + 0.64/119} = 2.8$$

Chapter 1 · Tether, and why a catch bond

The first contact is a bond between a selectin on the inflamed wall and its sugar-coated partner on the T cell. The bite's signals brought selectins to the surface of the endothelium in this patch, and nowhere else. A selectin bond forms fast and on the fly; the problem is what it must then hold. Module 7 put the push on the cell at about 74 pN, and the first bond to catch takes all of it. Week 4 wrote what force does to an ordinary bond, in its lifetime \(\tau = 1/k_{off}\):

eq. 4.2.5 again — Bell's law, a slip bond under force, read as a lifetime $$k_{off}(f) = k_0\,e^{f/f_\beta}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \tau(f) = \tau_0\,e^{-f/f_\beta}$$

with \(k_0 = 1/\tau_0\) the unloaded off-rate and \(f_\beta\) the force that shortens the lifetime by \(e\). At 74 pN a bond that lasts a fraction of a second unloaded would be torn in a blink. Week 5 met the other kind, the catch bond: a second way to let go that force closes, so its lifetime first rises as the force grows and only then falls:

eq. 5.2.3 again — the catch bond, in its lifetime $$\tau(f) = \frac{1}{k_s\,e^{f/F_s} + k_c\,e^{-f/F_c}}$$

with \(k_s, F_s\) the slip path (Bell's \(k_0, f_\beta\); \(k_c = 0\) gives eq. 4.2.5 back) and \(k_c, F_c\) the catch path, which force shuts. The lifetime peaks at the force \(f^*\) where the two paths balance. The selectin bond is the one Marshall and colleagues caught doing it in 2003: pulled on, the P-selectin bond's lifetime rose to a peak of about 0.7 s near 10 pN before falling again (Marshall 2003; the peak's numbers from the model fitted to their data, Barsegov & Thirumalai 2005). Spread the 74 pN over the handful of bonds at the back of the cell, about eight at 10 pN each (declared), and each sits near the top of its curve. It also fits a strange fact: below a wall stress of about 0.5 dyn/cm² the cells do not roll at all (Lawrence 1997, after Finger 1996). Lawrence put the threshold down to the torque the shear applies; later measurements tied it to catch bonds, which need force to hold: "catch bonds govern adhesion through L-selectin at threshold shear" (Yago 2004). The venule beside the bite shears its wall at about 5.7 dyn/cm² (Module 7 §3.3, the plasma sleeve counted), well above the threshold.

Chapter 2 · Roll: seconds beside the patch

Bonds form at the front of the contact as the cell turns, and break at the back when their time is up, so the cell does not stop: it rolls. Measured in the venules of a mouse lymph node (the stand-in), lymphocytes roll at 39 ± 22 µm/s (median 15) while the blood beside them moves at 1.1 mm/s (Warnock 1998): 1.3 to 3.5 per cent of the stream's speed, thirty to seventy-five times slower. At those speeds a 100 µm patch takes 2.6 to 6.8 seconds to pass, instead of Module 7's tenth of a second.

Chapter 3 · Read: the signal is pinned to the wall

The T cell is looking for a chemokine, a small signalling protein made by the inflamed tissue. Could it simply be dissolved in the plasma beside the patch? Week 2's Péclet number answers. Over the 100 µm patch, at the venule's millimetre a second, a molecule with \(D \approx 100\) µm²/s (declared: the signal of F25's homework, the one Module 7's floor used) has

eq. 2.3.8 again — the Péclet number, flow against diffusion $$\mathrm{Pe} = \frac{L\,v}{D}$$

with \(L\) the patch's length, \(v\) the flow's speed and \(D\) the molecule's diffusion coefficient: \(\mathrm{Pe} = (100\ \mu\text{m})(1\ \text{mm/s})/(100\ \mu\text{m}^2/\text{s}) = 1000\). Flow wins a thousandfold. A free chemokine released beside the patch is a tenth of a millimetre downstream in a tenth of a second, long before it could diffuse across even half the venule (about a second). So the body does not leave it free: the endothelium carries the chemokine through itself to its inner face and holds it there on the sugar chains of its coat (Middleton 1997; Proudfoot 2003), and chemokine presented on the wall triggers the T cell where the same chemokine in solution does not (Shamri 2005). The signal is pinned to the wall, as Module 7 said it would be.

How long does the reading take? Module 7 asked it with Week 3's floor: the time a cell needs to know a concentration to fifteen per cent by counting molecules that diffuse to it,

eq. 3.3.12 again — Week 3's counting floor, solved for the time $$\frac{\delta C}{C} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D a C\,t_{avg}}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t_{avg} = \frac{1}{4\pi D a C\,(\delta C/C)^2}$$

with \(a\) the cell's sensing radius, \(C\) the signal's concentration and \(t_{avg}\) the time it averages over. At F25's numbers (\(a = 7\) µm, \(C = 0.04\) molecules per µm³, about 66 picomolar, \(D = 100\) µm²/s) that is 0.13 s. A pinned signal is read by touch rather than by molecules arriving, so for the pinned chemokine this floor is a yardstick of scale, not its law. By that yardstick the cell beside the patch at Module 7's millimetre a second had 0.10 s, less than the floor; rolling, it has 2.6 to 6.8 s, twenty to fifty times the floor. Rolling is what buys the time to read.

Chapter 4 · Stop: enough bonds to hold the push

When the reading says yes, the cell switches on its integrins (LFA-1) from the inside, and they grip the wall's own ligands. The switch is fast: arrest is "abrupt" (Shamri 2005), and the integrins must be triggered "in less than 1 s" (Warnock 1998), because a rolling cell is past the patch in a few. How many bonds must hold?

eq. 5.2.4 again — load sharing, read for the number of bonds $$\bar f = \frac{F}{N_{bound}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{bound} \ge \frac{F}{\bar f_{max}}$$

with \(F\) the push, \(N_{bound}\) the bonds holding it and \(\bar f_{max}\) the most each can carry. This is F25 HW4's question, done in a venule. The push is 74 pN; if each bond carries of the order of 10 pN (declared: the catch bond's best force, and Week 5's bonds' scale), about eight bonds must hold at once. Eight is a small patch by Week 5's standard: the number is easy, and the hard part was the switch, which is why it waits for the reading.

Chapter 5 · Crawl to a junction

Stopped is not inside. The cell spreads, crawls along the wall with the motor–clutch of Weeks 4–5 looking for a way through, and decides to cross. Firm adhesion lasts tens of seconds, and the decision to migrate across the wall comes within several minutes (Carman & Springer 2004, citing Luu 1999). The scene compresses those minutes into a few seconds of clock.

Chapter 6 · Squeeze

The way through is a small opening, at a junction between two endothelial cells or straight through one: Carman & Springer (2004) saw an ICAM-free passage about 0.5–2 µm across, by both routes. The body of a T cell can flow through a small hole; its nucleus is the hard part. R3 measured that problem for a tumour cell with Week 6's eq. 6.5.1:

eq. 6.5.1 again — the nucleus through a gap $$t_{pass} = -\tau_\varepsilon\,\ln\frac{F_0/k_\infty - \Delta}{F_0/k_\infty - F_0/k_0}$$

with \(F_0\) the cell's push on its nucleus, \(k_0\) and \(k_\infty\) the nucleus's stiffness at first and after it has crept, \(\tau_\varepsilon\) its creep clock and \(\Delta\) how far it must deform to fit the gap. How stiff is a T cell's nucleus? Lamin A, the protein that stiffens a nucleus, "was almost negligible in resting naïve T lymphocytes, but was increased upon activation" (González-Granado 2014); the memory cell that enters skin lies somewhere between, so R3's two casts bracket it. On R3's lamin-poor (neutrophil) cast, the cell's own push deforms the nucleus through a 3 µm gap at once; on R3's tumour cast it needs about 24 s at 4 µm and never gets through below about 3.2 µm. And there is a hard floor: Wolf (2013) found T cells stop at pores of 4 µm², about 2.3 µm across, whatever the force. The passage Carman saw, 0.5–2 µm, is narrower than that floor, so it cannot be a fixed hole: it must widen as the cell pushes through (our inference from the two measurements). Either way the nucleus is not what takes the minutes: the cell's own protrusions and the endothelium's response are.

Chapter 7 · Inside: the tissue it lands in

The T cell comes out into the dermis of the papule, into the fluid Tab 2 followed: filtered from the capillaries, crossing the tissue, on its way to the lymph. Is the swollen tissue short of oxygen? Suppose it is swollen by as much as the wheal ever headed for, a quarter more volume (§2.2). Swelling evenly in three directions moves the capillaries apart by the cube root, from 36 µm to 39 µm. Tab 3's resting reach was about 300 µm: the capillaries still feed the dermis several times over, and the T cell arrives in tissue that has oxygen to spare for its work.

The wrong signal

Run the same venule with a T cell whose receptors do not match the bite's chemokine. It tethers and rolls exactly as before: the selectins do not care what the cell is looking for. But the occupancy never reaches the threshold, the integrins are never switched on, and at 15–40 µm/s the patch ends in a few seconds. The cell rolls off the end, the catch bonds let go as the shear carries it away, and it is back in Module 7's circuit, round the body in about a minute, to brush other venules. Most passes are this one; only the cell with the right receptor stops.

The capstone's answer. Thirty- to seventy-five-fold slower without stopping is a catch bond under the venule's shear: it buys the T cell seconds beside the patch, enough to read a signal that the flow would otherwise wash away and so has to be pinned to the wall; the integrins then stop it with a handful of bonds against 74 pN, and the nucleus, soft enough or not, is not what takes the time. Every step borrowed a law from an earlier module, and the tissue it lands in is the one Tabs 1–3 built: filtered water on its way to the lymph, and capillaries close enough to spare the oxygen.

5.1 · Next module's headline, measured today — the rings work as a group

§3.4 turned one ring of smooth muscle and found a limit: widening one stretch of a bed does little if the rest of the bed does not widen with it. A working muscle gets its hundred-fold flow only because the rings from the feed arteries down to the smallest arterioles open together. Something has to tell them all.

Watch a small branch of arterioles in a hamster's muscle under a microscope. Deliver a tiny dose of one dilator, acetylcholine, onto a single small arteriole about a millimetre from where it branches off. It widens on the spot, from 16 µm by about 13 µm, and the widening does not stay put: the vessel's origin, its parent arteriole and even its sister branch widen too, by 4–6 µm each, and the blood flowing into the branches rises by 25–80 %. Now deliver a different dilator, nitroprusside, onto the same spot. It widens the arteriole there just as much, and nothing else changes: not the vessels upstream, not the flow. "Dilation of a distal arteriole segment alone had no effect on muscle blood flow" (Kurjiaka & Segal 1995). Eq. 8.3.3 says why: a segment holding a tenth of its path's resistance (declared), widened from 16 to 29 µm, could raise the flow by about ten per cent at most.

Assumption broken. "Each ring of smooth muscle acts alone." It does not: "electrical signals initiated on microvascular smooth muscle and endothelial cells can travel rapidly for millimeters through cell-to-cell conduction via gap junction channels" (Segal 2005). The cells of the wall are wired to one another, and what one hears, its neighbours hear. Module 9 starts there, with smooth-muscle cells acting as a group in the organs they build, and then turns to the other muscle §3.3 counted: the skeletal muscle whose motors made the demand.
