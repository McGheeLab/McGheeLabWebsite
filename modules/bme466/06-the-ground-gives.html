1.1 · The same machine, camera lowered

Where we are in the story. The neutrophil has left the vessel and is crawling through the dermis toward the bite, and what it grips there is not a dish but mostly collagen: a tangle of protein fibres tied to one another where they cross, with fluid filling the gaps. Pull on that tangle and it pulls back like a spring, except that a spring keeps pulling just as hard for as long as you hold it stretched, and collagen does not. Hold collagen stretched and its pull slowly drops (it relaxes); pull on it with a steady force and it slowly stretches further (it creeps). Week 5 ended on the warning that both take about as long as one integrin bond holds on before it lets go, so a bond feels the ground change while it is still holding. For most of last week the ground under the clutches could not move; this week it gives, and how it gives sets how hard the cell can pull and how fast it crawls to the bite.

Weeks 4 and 5 built the machine; this week builds the ground it pulls on.

Week 5's machine ran on one sentence: the ground does not move. Motors haul the actin network rearward, integrin clutches bolt it to the ground, and every piconewton the clutches carry is a piconewton the motors cannot spend on flow. Every number in that module was computed with the ground's displacement set to zero — until §5.3 let it move and two things appeared that a rigid floor cannot show: a best stiffness, and a load-and-fail cycle. Both came from the same fact, which Tab 3 of Week 5 could not have: once the ground can move, every bound clutch shares one displacement.

So this week starts by lowering the camera. The scene below is Week 5's clutch, drawn the way the clutch literature draws it, with one addition: the ground under the adhesion is in the picture, as a layer of gel standing on far ground that never moves. Every clutch is now a chain of two springs, end to end. On top is the clutch itself, from the actin down to the ligand it holds; below it is the gel that ligand sits in (green). Nothing here is new physics. The gel is still Week 5's single ground spring \(\kappa_s\) in pN/nm, shared by the whole adhesion, so all the green springs lean by the same amount, \(x_s\). What that spring does to the gel around the adhesion is §1.5's business; where its number comes from is Tab 3's. Four chapters, played in turn: rigid ground, then the ground gives, then every bound clutch shares it, then load and fail together.

Watch what gets longer when the ground gives. The whole chain, from actin to far ground, stretches about 110 nm on average at 1 pN/nm, against 7 nm on rigid ground. But the extra is almost all gel. Each clutch's own stretch, which sets the force its bond feels, actually falls, from about 7 nm to about 3.6 nm, because the adhesion's load is now shared by about 38 bound clutches instead of 10. The gel's give is stretch the bond does not feel, and §1.3 turns that into the bond's loading rate.

Two drawing scales make both parts visible, and the canvas prints them. A clutch's own stretch is drawn at 6 px per nm, as in Week 5 §3.1. The gel's give is compressed, so that 100 nm draws as 40 px, because at 6 px per nm it would leave the canvas. The stats and the lower chart carry the real numbers.

1.2 · From the motors to the gel — the whole chain on one spring

§1.1 drew the ground as a spring, \(\kappa_s\), under every clutch. Now hang Week 5's whole machine on it and follow one pull from end to end. Myosin motors drag the actin network rearward. Bound clutches grip the actin, and their feet sit in the gel. As the actin slides it stretches the clutches and the gel together, so the force on every integrin rises and the gel under the adhesion moves. That same force pushes back on the motors, and loaded motors slide the actin more slowly. That loop is the whole mechanism. In this section it runs with nothing else in it: the ground is a single spring, and the clutches, once bound, hold on.

The motors. Week 5's motors obey one law. A team of \(N_m\) motors, each of which can hold at most \(F_m\) before it stalls, slides the actin at a speed that falls in proportion to the force \(F_{FA}\) the adhesion holds back:

eq. 5.1.5 again — the retrograde speed, with the adhesion carrying the load $$v_{retro} = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_m F_m}\right)$$

With nothing holding the actin it moves at \(v_0 = 120\) nm/s; at the stall force \(N_m F_m\) it stops. Week 5's cast has 100 motors of 2 pN, so the stall force is 200 pN.

The chain. \(F_{FA}\) runs through two springs. First come the \(n\) bound clutches, side by side: they share the load, so each carries \(F_{FA}/n\) and stretches \(F_{FA}/(n\kappa_c)\). Then comes the gel under their feet, which stretches \(x_s = F_{FA}/\kappa_s\) (eq. 5.5.1 again). The clutches' tops are fixed to the actin and the far ground does not move, so the two stretches add up to one thing you can see: the distance \(\Delta\) the actin has slid since the clutches bound. Call the stiffness of the whole chain its effective stiffness, \(\kappa_{eff}\):

$$\Delta = \underbrace{\frac{F_{FA}}{n\kappa_c}}_{\text{clutches}} + \underbrace{\frac{F_{FA}}{\kappa_s}}_{\text{gel}} = \frac{F_{FA}}{\kappa_{eff}}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F_{FA} = \kappa_{eff}\,\Delta$$

So the whole chain, from actin to far ground, acts as one spring, and the rule for springs end to end gives its effective stiffness:

eq. 6.1.1 — the chain: n clutches side by side, in series with the gel $$\frac{1}{\kappa_{eff}} = \frac{1}{n\,\kappa_c} + \frac{1}{\kappa_s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \kappa_{eff} = \frac{n\,\kappa_c\,\kappa_s}{n\,\kappa_c + \kappa_s}$$

Two stiffnesses, two different things. \(\kappa_c\) is the stiffness of one clutch: the molecules themselves (integrin, talin and the ligand the integrin holds) stretched like a very small spring, 0.8 pN for every nanometre in Week 5's cast. It belongs to the molecules, so it is the same on glass or on brain. \(\kappa_s\) is the stiffness of the gel under the adhesion: how many piconewtons it takes to drag that patch of gel one nanometre (§1.5 measures it with beads). It belongs to the ground, and it runs from about 0.1 pN/nm on a very soft gel to thousands on glass. The motors never feel either one alone. The distance they slide the actin is shared between stretching the clutches and moving the gel, so what they pull against is the chain.

When does the series rule matter? Only when the two parts are comparable. A chain of springs end to end is always softer than its softest part, and when one part is much softer than the other, the chain simply is that part, and that part takes the stretch. Each part's share of \(\Delta\) is \(\kappa_{eff}\) over its own stiffness:

What the series rule never changes is the force at the end of the run: that is set by the motors alone, \(N_mF_m\), shared among the \(n\) integrins. The rule decides how the stretch is split, so how far the beads move against how far the integrin stretches, and how fast the force builds, which is what decides whether a bond survives long enough to matter (§1.3).

The loop, step by step. Now let the motors run. Two laws are in play: the chain says how the force depends on how far the actin has slid (\(F_{FA} = \kappa_{eff}\Delta\)), and the motors say how fast the actin slides given the force (eq. 5.1.5). Follow them together.

  1. The force grows as the actin slides. In a short time \(dt\) the actin slides \(v_{retro}\,dt\), so \(\Delta\) grows by that much and the force grows by \(\kappa_{eff}\) times it:
    $$\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt} = \kappa_{eff}\,v_{retro}$$
  2. At the start the motors run free. The instant the clutches bind, \(F_{FA} = 0\), so \(v_{retro} = v_0\) and the force climbs at its fastest, \(\kappa_{eff}v_0\): about 107 pN/s for ten clutches on a 1 pN/nm gel. If it kept that rate it would reach the stall force \(N_mF_m\) after
    $$\tau_L = \frac{N_mF_m}{\kappa_{eff}\,v_0} = \frac{200\ \text{pN}}{106.7\ \text{pN/s}} = 1.875\ \text{s} \approx 1.9\ \text{s}$$
    That is what \(\tau_L\) means: the time to stall at the starting rate. On the bench it is where the dashed starting line meets the end value.
  3. But the rate falls as the force rises. Step 2's rate holds only at the instant the clutches bind, when nothing holds the actin back. Once the clutches hold a force, the motors slow down.

    What the motors pull against. Week 5 §1.3 found the motors' load from the free body of the actin: the adhesion is the only thing holding the actin forward, so the force the motors pull against is exactly the force the adhesion carries, \(F_{FA}\). Week 5 put that load into the motors' load line and got eq. 5.1.5: every pN the adhesion takes is a pN the motors no longer spend on sliding actin rearward. Here the adhesion's force is the force in the chain, so the more the chain is stretched, the slower the actin slides.

    Put the motors' law into step 1. Where step 1 has \(v_{retro}\), write the right side of eq. 5.1.5:

    $$\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt} = \kappa_{eff}\,v_{retro} = \kappa_{eff}v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\right)$$

    \(F_{FA}\) now appears on the right side too: how fast the force grows depends on how large it already is. The table works this out for step 2's chain, ten clutches on a 1 pN/nm gel (\(\kappa_{eff} = 0.889\) pN/nm). Each row puts a force into eq. 5.1.5 to get the actin's speed, then multiplies that speed by \(\kappa_{eff}\) to get the rate:

    Compare the last two columns: when the force left halves, so does the rate. The algebra shows why, one move per line.

    Write the starting rate with \(\tau_L\). The factor in front of the bracket, \(\kappa_{eff}v_0\), is step 2's starting rate, and step 2 defined \(\tau_L\) from it:

    $$\tau_L = \frac{N_mF_m}{\kappa_{eff}v_0}$$

    Multiply both sides by \(\kappa_{eff}v_0\), then divide both sides by \(\tau_L\):

    $$\tau_L\,\kappa_{eff}v_0 = N_mF_m\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\kappa_{eff}v_0 = \frac{N_mF_m}{\tau_L} = \frac{200\ \text{pN}}{1.875\ \text{s}} = 107\ \text{pN/s}$$

    So the starting rate is the stall force divided by the time it takes to reach stall at that rate. Put \(N_mF_m/\tau_L\) in place of \(\kappa_{eff}v_0\):

    $$\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt} = \frac{N_mF_m}{\tau_L}\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\right)$$

    Multiply the factor in front into each term of the bracket:

    $$\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt} = \frac{N_mF_m}{\tau_L} - \frac{N_mF_m}{\tau_L}\cdot\frac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}$$

    In the second term \(N_mF_m\) is on top and below, so it cancels:

    $$\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt} = \frac{N_mF_m}{\tau_L} - \frac{F_{FA}}{\tau_L}$$

    Both terms are divided by \(\tau_L\), so write them over it once:

    $$\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt} = \frac{N_mF_m - F_{FA}}{\tau_L}$$

    In words: the force climbs at a rate proportional to how far it still is from stall. The rate is the force left divided by \(\tau_L\): in the table, 150 pN left ÷ 1.875 s = 80 pN/s. Halfway to stall the force climbs at half the starting rate; nine-tenths of the way, at a tenth.

  4. What is left closes in proportion to itself. Measure what is left in the chain's own units. At stall the chain will be stretched by \(s_0 = N_mF_m/\kappa_{eff}\): 225 nm for the chain in step 2, 25 nm in the clutches and 200 nm in the gel. At any moment it is stretched by \(F_{FA}/\kappa_{eff}\), so the stretch left before stall is
    $$s = \frac{N_mF_m - F_{FA}}{\kappa_{eff}} = s_0 - \Delta$$
    Divide step 3 by \(\kappa_{eff}\), with a minus sign because \(s\) shrinks as \(F_{FA}\) grows:
    $$\frac{ds}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\kappa_{eff}}\,\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt} = -\frac{s}{\tau_L}$$
    The stretch left shrinks at a rate proportional to itself, so in every interval \(\tau_L\) it falls by the same factor, \(e\), to 37 % of what it was. This is the same law as bonds letting go at a constant rate, where the number still bound falls by the same fraction every second. It also says what \(\tau_L\) is in the chain's own terms: \(\tau_L = s_0/v_0\), the time the actin would take to slide the whole stretch at full speed (225 nm ÷ 120 nm/s = 1.9 s). The stretch left starts at \(s_0\), so
    $$s = s_0\,e^{-t/\tau_L}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{FA} = N_mF_m - \kappa_{eff}\,s = N_mF_m\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_L}\right)$$
    After one \(\tau_L\) the force is 63 % of the way to stall; after three, 95 %. The Derive box under eq. 6.1.2 does this step with calculus, one move at a time.
  5. Everything else follows from the force. The flow is eq. 5.1.5 again, and it is in proportion to the stretch left: \(v_{retro} = v_0(1 - F_{FA}/N_mF_m) = v_0\,s/s_0 = v_0e^{-t/\tau_L}\); each integrin carries \(F_{FA}/n\); the gel has moved \(F_{FA}/\kappa_s\).

Collected in one box:

eq. 6.1.2 — the load builds to the motors' stall $$F_{FA}(t) = N_m F_m\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_L}\right),\qquad v_{retro}(t) = v_0\,e^{-t/\tau_L},\qquad \tau_L = \frac{N_m F_m}{\kappa_{eff}\,v_0}$$
Derive eq. 6.1.2 — from the rate in step 3 to \(F_{FA}(t)\), one move at a time

Steps 3 and 4 say how fast the force changes. Eq. 6.1.2 says what the force is at every moment. Getting from one to the other means undoing a derivative, which is integrating. It is easiest in the stretch left, \(s\), because its rate depends on \(s\) alone.

  1. Start from step 3, and write it in the stretch left. Step 3 is
    $$\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt} = \frac{N_mF_m - F_{FA}}{\tau_L}$$
    The stretch left is \(s = (N_mF_m - F_{FA})/\kappa_{eff}\), so \(F_{FA} = N_mF_m - \kappa_{eff}\,s\). \(N_mF_m\) and \(\kappa_{eff}\) are constants, so when \(F_{FA}\) changes, only \(s\) moves: \(dF_{FA}/dt = -\kappa_{eff}\,ds/dt\). The top of the right-hand side is \(N_mF_m - F_{FA} = \kappa_{eff}\,s\). Put both in:
    $$-\kappa_{eff}\,\frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{\kappa_{eff}\,s}{\tau_L}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{ds}{dt} = -\frac{s}{\tau_L}$$
  2. Put everything with \(s\) on one side and everything with \(t\) on the other. Divide both sides by \(s\) and multiply both by \(dt\):
    $$\frac{ds}{s} = -\frac{dt}{\tau_L}$$
  3. Integrate both sides. The integral of \(1/s\) is \(\ln s\); \(\tau_L\) is a constant, so the right side integrates to \(-t/\tau_L\); and an indefinite integral carries a constant of integration, \(C\), that the equation on its own cannot fix:
    $$\int\frac{ds}{s} = -\frac{1}{\tau_L}\int dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln s = -\frac{t}{\tau_L} + C$$
  4. Undo the logarithm. Raise \(e\) to each side, and call the constant \(e^{C}\) by a shorter name, \(A\):
    $$s = e^{-t/\tau_L + C} = e^{C}\,e^{-t/\tau_L} = A\,e^{-t/\tau_L}$$
    Every value of \(A\) solves the equation; the boundary condition picks ours.
  5. Apply the boundary condition. At binding, \(t = 0\) and nothing is held yet, so the stretch left is all of it, \(s(0) = s_0 = N_mF_m/\kappa_{eff}\). At \(t = 0\), \(e^{0} = 1\), so \(s(0) = A\), and therefore \(A = s_0\):
    $$s(t) = s_0\,e^{-t/\tau_L}$$
  6. Turn the stretch left back into force. Use \(F_{FA} = N_mF_m - \kappa_{eff}\,s\), and notice that \(\kappa_{eff}\,s_0 = N_mF_m\):
    $$F_{FA} = N_mF_m - \kappa_{eff}\,s_0\,e^{-t/\tau_L} = N_mF_m - N_mF_m\,e^{-t/\tau_L} = N_mF_m\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_L}\right)$$
    That is eq. 6.1.2.
  7. Check it three ways. At \(t = 0\): \(e^0 = 1\), so \(F_{FA} = 0\) — nothing is held at binding. As \(t \to \infty\): \(e^{-t/\tau_L} \to 0\), so \(F_{FA} \to N_mF_m\) — the motors' stall. And the slope at \(t = 0\): differentiating gives \(dF_{FA}/dt = (N_mF_m/\tau_L)\,e^{-t/\tau_L}\), which at \(t = 0\) is \(N_mF_m/\tau_L = \kappa_{eff}v_0\), step 2's starting rate (107 pN/s).
    $$F_{FA}(0) = 0,\qquad F_{FA}(\infty) = N_mF_m,\qquad \left.\frac{dF_{FA}}{dt}\right|_{t=0} = \frac{N_mF_m}{\tau_L} = \kappa_{eff}\,v_0$$
  8. The flow, and each integrin, follow. Put \(F_{FA}(t)\) into eq. 5.1.5, and divide by \(n\) for one integrin's share:
    $$v_{retro} = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\right) = v_0\,e^{-t/\tau_L},\qquad f(t) = \frac{N_mF_m}{n}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_L}\right)$$
  9. Turn it round: when does each integrin reach a force \(f\)? Solve the last expression for \(t\):
    $$1 - e^{-t/\tau_L} = \frac{n f}{N_mF_m}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad t(f) = -\tau_L\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{n f}{N_mF_m}\right)$$
The last expression has no answer when \(n f \ge N_mF_m\): the logarithm of zero or a negative number does not exist. If the clutches share the motors' whole force so widely that each one's share is below \(f\), no amount of waiting gets there. For talin's 12 pN with 100 motors of 2 pN, that happens at 17 bound clutches, on every ground. With ten clutches on 1 pN/nm, each reaches 12 pN at \(-1.875\ln(1 - 0.6) = 1.7\) s.

Read the end of the run first. The force always ends at the motors' stall, 200 pN, whatever the ground is. The gel ends displaced by that force over its spring: 200 nm on a 1 pN/nm ground, 2 µm on 0.1 pN/nm. Each integrin ends up carrying \(N_mF_m/n\): 20 pN when ten clutches share the load, and more if fewer do, or if there are more motors. Then read the time. The ground sets how long the build takes, through \(\kappa_{eff}\): with ten clutches, \(\tau_L\) is 0.21 s on rigid ground, 1.9 s on 1 pN/nm and 17 s on 0.1 pN/nm. So a softer ground gives the same final force, a bigger displacement, and a slower build.

The same numbers set how fast each integrin is loaded at the start, \(\kappa_{eff}v_0/n\). On rigid ground that is \(\kappa_c v_0 = 96\) pN/s however many clutches share the load. On a 1 pN/nm ground, ten clutches sharing are each loaded at 11 pN/s, because they share the gel's give as well as the force. That is §1.1's observation (each clutch's own stretch falls when the ground gives) written as a rate. And it says why §1.3 can load a single bond at the free flow: one bond's 12 pN is 6 % of the motors' 200 pN, so it slows the actin by only 6 %.

1.3 · How the ground's give changes the force on the integrin

Now follow a single bond. §1.2 put \(n\) clutches on the ground; take \(n = 1\). The clutch is a spring of stiffness \(\kappa_c\) (Week 5 eq. 5.3.2), the ground under it a spring \(\kappa_s\) (§1.1), and the actin flowing past drags the clutch's top end at \(v_{retro}\). With one bond, the adhesion's force \(F_{FA}\) is the one integrin's force \(f\) (\(F_{FA}/n\) with \(n = 1\)). The chain is eq. 6.1.1 with one clutch:

eq. 6.1.1 again — the chain, with one clutch (n = 1) $$\frac{1}{\kappa_{eff}} = \frac{1}{\kappa_c} + \frac{1}{\kappa_s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \kappa_{eff} = \frac{\kappa_c\,\kappa_s}{\kappa_c + \kappa_s}$$

With \(\kappa_c = 0.8\) pN/nm and a ground of 1 pN/nm, the chain is 0.44 pN/nm, the last row of §1.2's table, where both springs matter. Make the ground rigid and \(\kappa_{eff} \to \kappa_c\); make it very soft and \(\kappa_{eff} \to \kappa_s\), so the bond hardly feels the flow at all. Week 5 §2.2 said the quantity everything turns on is how fast the bond is loaded, and that rate is the stiffness times the flow. It wrote the stiffness as the clutch's alone, because the ground could not move. Now it can, and §1.2 has already worked out the rate. Its step 1 with \(n = 1\) is the rate the integrin is loaded at, and its step 2 says what that rate is while the motors run free. One bond barely slows them: talin's 12 pN is 6 % of the motors' 200 pN, so \(v_{retro}\) stays within 6 % of \(v_0\), and the rate stays at step 2's starting value. The ramp is the straight start of §1.2's curve:

eq. 5.2.2 again — the loading rate, through the chain $$\dot f = \frac{df}{dt} = \kappa_{eff}\,v_{retro} \;\approx\; \kappa_{eff}\,v_0$$

The dot over the \(f\) means its rate of change in time: \(\dot f\) is how fast the integrin's force climbs, in pN/s, and Tab 2 writes every rate the same way. (Week 5 called this rate \(r\); it is the same quantity, and this module keeps the dot so that no new letter has to be learnt.) Why the loading rate matters is Bell's law, which Week 5 wrote as a lifetime: pull harder and the bond lets go sooner,

eq. 4.2.5 again — how long a bond lasts under a load \(f\) $$\tau(f) = \tau_0\,e^{-f/f_\beta}, \qquad \tau_0 = 1/k_0$$

Here \(\tau(f)\) is the bond's lifetime, not §1.2's load time \(\tau_L\), and \(k_0\) is the cast's unloaded off-rate \(k_{off,0}\). That is 10 s with nothing pulling, at \(k_0 = 0.1\) s⁻¹, and 0.025 s at talin's threshold of 12 pN. A ramped bond is therefore in a race. The load is climbing toward the threshold at which talin unfolds and the adhesion reinforces (Week 5 §1.5), and the bond's odds of letting go are climbing with it. The odds of winning the race follow from Bell's rate by solving one differential equation with its boundary condition. The Derive box below the result does it move by move; the result is

$$P(\text{reach }F_T) = \exp\!\left[-\int_0^{F_T/\dot f} k_0\,e^{\dot f s/f_\beta}\,ds\right] = \exp\!\left[-\frac{k_0 f_\beta}{\dot f}\left(e^{F_T/f_\beta}-1\right)\right]$$
Derive the bond's odds under a rising load — Bell's rate as a differential equation, solved with its boundary condition
  1. The law. Let \(P(t)\) be the probability that the bond is still attached at time \(t\). In a short time \(dt\) an attached bond lets go with probability \(k_{off}\,dt\), so the attached fraction falls by that share of itself:
    $$\frac{dP}{dt} = -k_{off}(f)\,P,\qquad k_{off}(f) = k_0\,e^{f/f_\beta}\quad\text{(eq. 4.2.5 written as a rate: } k_{off} = 1/\tau(f)\text{)}$$
    With \(f\) held constant this is the same equation as Tab 2 §2.4's held stretch, and its solution is \(P = e^{-k_{off}t}\), which is where the lifetime \(\tau(f) = 1/k_{off}\) comes from. Here \(f\) is not constant.
  2. The loading. The bond is ramped from zero at the rate eq. 5.2.2 gives, \(f = \dot f\,t\), so the rate of letting go climbs as the load does:
    $$k_{off}(t) = k_0\,e^{\dot f t/f_\beta}$$
  3. Put the loading into the law and solve. Separate the variables, \(P\) on the left and \(t\) on the right:
    $$\frac{dP}{P} = -k_0\,e^{\dot f t/f_\beta}\,dt$$
    Integrate both sides. The left is \(\ln P\). On the right, the integral of \(e^{at}\) is \(e^{at}/a\), here with \(a = \dot f/f_\beta\); and the indefinite integral carries a constant \(C\):
    $$\ln P = -\frac{k_0 f_\beta}{\dot f}\,e^{\dot f t/f_\beta} + C$$
    Apply the boundary condition. At \(t = 0\) the bond has just formed and is certainly attached, \(P(0) = 1\), and \(\ln 1 = 0\):
    $$0 = -\frac{k_0 f_\beta}{\dot f}\,e^{0} + C\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad C = \frac{k_0 f_\beta}{\dot f}$$
    Put \(C\) back and factor the common \(k_0f_\beta/\dot f\):
    $$\ln P = -\frac{k_0 f_\beta}{\dot f}\left(e^{\dot f t/f_\beta} - 1\right)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P(t) = \exp\!\left[-\frac{k_0 f_\beta}{\dot f}\left(e^{\dot f t/f_\beta} - 1\right)\right]$$
    Check it three ways: \(P(0) = e^{0} = 1\); \(P\) only falls, because the exponent is never positive; and for a load that barely grows (\(\dot f \to 0\), so \(e^{\dot f t/f_\beta} - 1 \approx \dot f t/f_\beta\)) it becomes \(e^{-k_0 t}\), the unloaded bond.
  4. Read it at the threshold. Talin's threshold is reached when \(\dot f\,t_T = F_T\), at \(t_T = F_T/\dot f\). Put \(t_T\) in; the product \(\dot f t_T/f_\beta\) is \(F_T/f_\beta\):
    $$P(\text{reach }F_T) = \exp\!\left[-\frac{k_0 f_\beta}{\dot f}\left(e^{F_T/f_\beta} - 1\right)\right]$$
    The bracket is fixed by the bond and the threshold, and the rate \(\dot f\) sits underneath: load faster and the exponent shrinks, so the odds rise; load slower and it grows, so they fall.
Written as a definite integral, the exponent is \(\int_0^{t_T} k_{off}(t)\,dt\): the rate of letting go, accumulated over the whole ramp. The harness checks this closed form against that integral done numerically and against thousands of simulated bonds.

Now put the ground's spring in. The ground enters only through \(\dot f\). With one bond \(\dot f = \kappa_{eff}v_0\), and eq. 6.1.1 with \(n = 1\) says \(1/\kappa_{eff} = 1/\kappa_c + 1/\kappa_s\), so the reciprocal of the rate splits into a clutch part and a ground part:

$$\frac{1}{\dot f} = \frac{1}{\kappa_{eff}\,v_0} = \frac{1}{v_0}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa_c} + \frac{1}{\kappa_s}\right)$$

Put that into the exponent. Everything in front of \(1/\kappa_c + 1/\kappa_s\) is fixed by the bond, by talin and by the flow, so give it one name, \(K\). The exponent becomes \(-K/\kappa_c - K/\kappa_s\), and the exponential of a sum is a product:

eq. 6.1.3 — the odds a ramped bond lives to reach talin's threshold, in the ground's spring $$P(\text{reach }F_T) = \underbrace{e^{-K/\kappa_c}}_{\text{the clutch: rigid ground}}\;\times\;\underbrace{e^{-K/\kappa_s}}_{\text{what the ground costs}},\qquad K = \frac{k_0 f_\beta}{v_0}\left(e^{F_T/f_\beta}-1\right)$$

Read the two factors. \(K\) is a stiffness: 0.67 pN/nm for the cast (\(k_0 = 0.1\) s⁻¹, \(f_\beta = 2\) pN, \(F_T = 12\) pN, \(v_0 = 120\) nm/s). The first factor is the odds on rigid ground, where \(\kappa_s \to \infty\) and the second factor is 1: \(e^{-0.67/0.8} = 43\) %. The second factor is the ground's whole effect, and it depends on nothing but \(\kappa_s\) measured against \(K\). A ground much stiffer than \(K\) costs almost nothing: at 1000 pN/nm the factor is 0.999. A ground as stiff as \(K\) cuts the odds to \(1/e\), 37 % of the rigid value. A ground much softer than \(K\) all but ends them: at 0.1 pN/nm the factor is 0.0012. \(K\) is larger for a bond that lets go faster (\(k_0\)) or a talin threshold that sits higher (\(F_T\)), and smaller for a faster flow (\(v_0\)).

Read where \(\dot f\) sits in the exponent, underneath: slower loading gives the bond more time to fall off before the load is high enough to matter. Under free flow, \(v_{retro} \approx v_0 = 120\) nm/s, a bond on rigid ground is loaded at 96 pN/s, reaches 12 pN in an eighth of a second, and does so 43 % of the time. On a ground of 1 pN/nm the rate falls to 53 pN/s and the odds to 22 %. On 0.1 pN/nm, eight times softer than the clutch, the rate is 11 pN/s and the odds are 0.05 %: about one bond in two thousand. That is how a softer ground changes the force on an integrin. It does not change the force the motors can make; it slows the loading of every bond, so that the bond loses its race with its own lifetime. Elosegui-Artola and colleagues measured exactly this: a rigidity threshold below which "integrins unbind and release force before talin can unfold". They showed that a clutch model with talin's unfolding kinetics predicts where the threshold sits.

1.4 · An adhesion on a spring — and the best ground

One bond was the warm-up. An adhesion is seventy-five clutches on one patch of ground, and they do not all bind at once. Clutches bind and let go all the time, so at any moment \(n\) of them are bound, and each has been dragged by the actin for a different time. Write \(\Delta_i\) for how far the actin has slid since clutch \(i\) bound: §1.2's \(\Delta\), one per clutch. They all pull the same patch of gel, so they share one displacement \(x_s\), and the balance of forces under the adhesion fixes it:

eq. 6.1.4 — the shared ground $$F_{FA} = \kappa_s\,x_s = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_c\,(\Delta_i - x_s) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_s = \frac{\kappa_c\sum_{i}\Delta_i}{\kappa_s + n\,\kappa_c}$$

Here each clutch's own stretch is \(\Delta_i - x_s\), the actin's travel minus the gel's give, so its force is \(\kappa_c(\Delta_i - x_s)\), and the force the gel carries, \(\kappa_s x_s\), is the adhesion's force \(F_{FA}\) (Week 5 called it the traction). If every clutch had bound at the same moment, as in §1.2, every \(\Delta_i\) would be the same \(\Delta\), and the box gives back §1.2's chain: \(x_s = n\kappa_c\Delta/(\kappa_s + n\kappa_c)\), so \(F_{FA} = \kappa_s x_s = \kappa_{eff}\Delta\) (eq. 6.1.1). Letting \(\kappa_s \to \infty\) sends \(x_s \to 0\) and gives Week 5's rigid model back exactly, which the harness checks to a nanometre per second. This relation ran Week 5 §5.3's bench without ever getting a number. It gets one here, because §1.1 is where you watched the ground's give, \(x_s\), shared by every green spring.

Now follow one clutch letting go. Its pull on the gel vanishes, so the gel springs part way back and \(x_s\) falls. Every survivor is still attached to the actin at the same \(\Delta_i\), so its own stretch \(\Delta_i - x_s\), and with it its force, rises. A failure re-loads the survivors. That is why the whole adhesion loads and fails together on a ground that gives, and why the same bench on a rigid floor only fuzzes: there is no \(x_s\) to share.

1.5 · Measuring it — traction force microscopy

Everything so far pulled the ground with a force you set. In an experiment nobody knows the force; that is the thing you want. What you can see is the ground. Seed a gel with fluorescent beads, let a cell adhere and pull, and photograph the beads. Then release the cell (trypsin, or a drug that relaxes the motors) and photograph them again. Each bead's displacement between the two pictures is a measurement of how far the ground moved. Pelham and Wang built the flexible gel in 1997; Dembo and Wang turned the bead field into a map of the cell's pull in 1999, at about 5 µm resolution; Butler and colleagues made the calculation exact and fast in 2002. The method is traction force microscopy, and everything it needs is on this page already. This section builds it in two halves: forwards, what one adhesion does to the beads around it; then backwards, from many beads to the forces that moved them.

First, forwards: what one adhesion does to the gel. Reading the beads backwards needs the forward answer first. Push on a gel with a needle and the surface dents; pull sideways and it drags. Two questions, answered with nothing but forces and displacements: how far does the gel move under the pull, and how far away is the pull still felt?

The first answer is the one §1.1 already drew. Pull an adhesion sideways with a force \(F\) and the gel under it moves by \(u_0\); pull twice as hard and it moves twice as far. The gel under the adhesion is a spring — Week 5 §5.3's ground spring \(\kappa_s\), the green spring under every clutch in §1.1:

eq. 5.5.1 again — the gel under the adhesion is a spring $$F = \kappa_s\,u_0$$

But the gel under the adhesion is not a spring on its own. It is joined to the gel all around it, so the pull drags the neighbourhood along too: every bead in the gel moves, less and less the further it is from the adhesion. The bench below measures how much less, and the rule it finds is short. For an adhesion of radius \(a\), a bead at distance \(r\) from its centre moves

eq. 6.1.5 — how far the pull reaches $$u(r) = u_0\,\frac{a}{r}\times\begin{cases}\tfrac23 & \text{in line with the pull}\\[3pt] \tfrac13 & \text{beside the pull}\end{cases}\qquad\qquad u_0 = \frac{F}{\kappa_s}$$

Three things to read off it. It falls as \(1/r\): a bead twice as far away moves half as much, so the pull is felt everywhere, just less and less. It depends on direction: a bead in line with the pull moves twice as far as a bead the same distance beside it. And it is all in proportion to \(u_0\): make the ground softer (a smaller \(\kappa_s\)) and every bead moves more by the same factor. At \(F = 100\) pN on a ground of \(\kappa_s = 1\) pN/nm under a 1 µm adhesion, the gel under the adhesion moves 100 nm, and eq. 6.1.5 gives 33 nm for a bead 2 µm in front of it and 17 nm for a bead 2 µm beside it. (The bench's beads, moved by the exact answer, read 33 and 18.)

Eq. 6.1.5 is a rule for beads that are not too close. In line with the pull it is right to about a tenth of a percent from two adhesion radii out; beside the pull it reads 9 % low at two radii and 2 % low at four. Closer in, the adhesion's own shape matters, and the bench draws the exact answer there. The Derive box shows where the rule comes from: a solution from the 1880s for a gel that fills everything below a flat surface. That solution has the gel's own stiffness in it, and the stiffness divides out, which is why eq. 6.1.5 needs only \(u_0\) and distances.

What sets \(\kappa_s\)? Two things: how stiff the gel is, and how big the adhesion is. A bigger grip on the same gel makes a stiffer spring. How to put a number on the gel itself, one that does not depend on the grip, is Tab 3's question. Here the ground is simply \(\kappa_s\), in pN/nm, the unit the clutch is in.

A sideways point force \(F\) at the surface of a linear elastic gel that fills everything below a flat surface (a half-space) moves the surface a distance \(r\) away, in the direction of the pull, by an amount that depends on the angle \(\theta\) between the pull and the line to that point (Cerruti, 1882, in the form traction force microscopy uses):
    $$u_x(r,\theta) = \frac{(1+\nu)\,F}{\pi E\,r}\Big[(1-\nu) + \nu\cos^2\theta\Big]$$
  2. Set \(\nu = \tfrac12\). Every biological gel is mostly water, cannot change its volume, and has \(\nu = \tfrac12\); Tab 3 §3.4 says why (polyacrylamide measures 0.48). The bracket becomes \(\tfrac12(1 + \cos^2\theta)\):
    $$u_x(r,\theta) = \frac{3F\,(1 + \cos^2\theta)}{4\pi E\,r}$$
  3. Spread the force over the adhesion and add up at its centre. Put the force \(F\) uniformly over a disc of radius \(a\), so each patch \(dA = \rho\,d\rho\,d\theta\) carries \(q\,dA\) with \(q = F/\pi a^2\). In polar coordinates about the centre the \(1/\rho\) cancels the \(\rho\) of the area element, the radial integral is \(a\), and \(\int_0^{2\pi}(1+\cos^2\theta)\,d\theta = 3\pi\):
    $$u_0 = \frac{3q}{4\pi E}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{a}\frac{1+\cos^2\theta}{\rho}\,\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta = \frac{9\,q\,a}{4E} = \frac{9F}{4\pi E\,a} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \kappa_s = \frac{F}{u_0} = \frac49\,\pi E\,a$$ That last result is the ground's spring, with the gel's stiffness and the adhesion's size in it. Tab 3 §3.1 comes back to it.
  4. Divide step 2 by step 3. \(F\), \(E\) and \(\pi\) all cancel:
    $$\frac{u(r,\theta)}{u_0} = \frac{a}{r}\cdot\frac{1+\cos^2\theta}{3} \;=\; \frac{a}{r}\times\begin{cases}\tfrac23 & \theta = 0\ \text{(in line)}\\ \tfrac13 & \theta = 90^\circ\ \text{(beside)}\end{cases}$$ That is eq. 6.1.5.
Step 2 is a point pull; step 3 is the whole adhesion. Far from the adhesion the two look the same, which is why eq. 6.1.5 is exact far away and approximate close in. The beads on this page are moved by the exact adhesion, not by eq. 6.1.5. The disc integral can be done at any point, not just the centre: in polar coordinates about the bead it becomes one integral over angle, with no singularity anywhere. The harness holds that integral at the centre to step 3's closed form to nine figures, and holds eq. 6.1.5 against it in line with the pull and beside it.
Uniform pull over the disc is one choice. 2.1 · Series and parallel — two ways to join any two elements

Press your thumb into four things at the same speed: honey, cheese, a block of metal, a diamond. Same thumb, same speed, four different answers. The diamond does not move at all; it pushes back the instant you touch it. The metal pushes back in proportion to how far you have dented it, and springs back when you let go. The honey pushes back in proportion to how fast you are moving, and never stops giving way. The cheese does some of each: it resists hard at first, then slowly eases off. Tendon and bone answer like the metal; brain and fat, over a second or so, answer more like the honey. Tab 1's ground was the metal — a spring — and Week 5 warned you at the end that real matrix "creeps and relaxes". This tab builds the cheese, out of two parts: a spring, which answers how far, and a damper, which answers how fast.

Everything in this tab is one force against one displacement. A force \(F\) is in piconewtons (pN); a displacement \(x\) — how far the end of an element has moved from where it started — is in nanometres (nm); time \(t\) is in seconds (s). Before either part appears, one question comes first: if you join two elements, what does the pair do? Call them A and B. Each has its own rule relating the force on it to its own displacement, whatever is inside it. There are only two ways to join them: end to end, called series, or side by side between two bars, called parallel. Each way has one rule that says what the two share and what adds up:

eq. 6.2.1 — the two rules $$\text{series:}\quad F = F_A = F_B,\;\; x = x_A + x_B \qquad\qquad \text{parallel:}\quad x = x_A = x_B,\;\; F = F_A + F_B$$

Here \(F_A\) is the force on A and \(x_A\) is A's own displacement, its change in length; \(F\) and \(x\) with no letter belong to the pair as a whole. In series the two carry one force and their displacements add. In parallel they share one displacement and their forces add.

Derive the two rules, from a massless joint and two rigid bars
  1. Series: look at the joint between A and B. It is a point with no mass. A pulls it one way with \(F_A\), B pulls it the other way with \(F_B\), and Newton's second law says the net force is mass times acceleration, which is zero when there is no mass. So the two pulls balance: A and B carry the same force, and so does the far end of B, where you hold the pair.
    $$F_B - F_A = m_{joint}\,a = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; F = F_A = F_B$$
  2. Series: the lengths add, by geometry. B sits on the end of A, so the far end moves by A's change in length plus B's.
    $$x = x_A + x_B$$
  3. Parallel: both hang between the same two rigid bars. A rigid bar does not bend, so when the moving bar shifts by \(x\), A and B each stretch by exactly \(x\). The moving bar is massless too, so your pull on it balances the two elements pulling back on it.
    $$x_A = x_B = x, \qquad F - F_A - F_B = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; F = F_A + F_B$$
Neither argument used what A and B are. The rules hold for springs, for dampers and for anything built out of them — which is why every element in this tab, however complicated, can be built one joint at a time.

There are also two ways to load anything, and they ask opposite questions. Under force control you set the force \(F(t)\) and ask how far the element moves, \(x(t)\): a weight hung on it, a motor pulling at its stall force, a cell's clutches pulling on the ground. Under displacement control you set the displacement \(x(t)\) and ask what force it takes, \(F(t)\): a stage moved by a set amount, a flow that drags at a set speed.

So read \(F\) as the adhesion's pull on the gel, Tab 1's \(F_{FA}\), and \(x\) as the gel's give under it, Tab 1's \(x_s\). The four loadings this tab uses are not a textbook's list. 2.2 · Hooke's spring — force control and displacement control

The first part is the one you have used since Week 5's §5.1: a spring, whose force is proportional to how far it is stretched,

eq. 5.5.1 again — the spring $$F = k\,x$$

with \(k\) its stiffness, or spring constant, in pN/nm: the force it takes to stretch it by one nanometre. This is Hooke's law, and using it takes one step under either control.

Force control. You set \(F(t)\); the spring's answer is \(x(t) = F(t)/k\). Divide.

Displacement control. You set \(x(t)\); the force it takes is \(F(t) = k\,x(t)\). Multiply.

Either way the spring has no time in it. Its answer depends only on what is being done to it now — not on what was done a second ago, and not on how fast. So a step gives a step and a ramp gives a ramp, under either control, and the answer arrives at the same instant as the load. By §2.1's rules two springs joined either way make one spring, so anything built from springs alone behaves the same way.

Let go. Set \(F = 0\) and the law answers \(x = 0/k = 0\), at once: the spring is back where it started the instant the load is gone, whatever the load was and however long it was held. It keeps nothing.

Under the stall force it holds \(x_s = N_mF_m/\kappa_s\), 200 nm on a 1 pN/nm ground, for as long as the motors do. Read backwards, under displacement control, it is traction force microscopy on an elastic gel: a bead's displacement is the force, \(F = \kappa_s x\), at that instant, which is what §1.5 assumed about every bead. And when the adhesion lets go, a spring ground is back at once, with no trace of the pull left in it — which is what §1.5's second picture, taken after the cell is released, assumed about the gel. 2.3 · The Newtonian damper — force control and displacement control

The second part answers not how far but how fast. A damper — engineers draw it as a dashpot: a piston in a cylinder of oil — does not care how far it has been pulled, only how fast it is being pulled right now:

eq. 6.2.2 — the damper $$F = \eta\,\dot x$$

Here \(\dot x = dx/dt\) is the rate of stretching in nm/s (a dot over a letter means its rate of change in time), and \(\eta\) (eta) is the damper's viscosity in pN·s/nm: the force it takes to move the piston at one nanometre per second. Newtonian means the force is proportional to the rate, with \(\eta\) a constant: double the speed, double the force. Honey and water behave this way.

Force control. You set \(F(t)\). The damper answers with a rate, \(\dot x = F/\eta\), and its displacement is what that rate adds up to over time, \(x(t) = \frac{1}{\eta}\int_0^t F\,dt\). Integrate. A held force \(F_0\) gives a steady drift, \(x = F_0 t/\eta\), that never stops. A force ramp \(F = \dot F t\) gives \(x = \dot F t^2/(2\eta)\), a parabola, because the damper moves faster every second.

Displacement control. You set \(x(t)\). The force is \(\eta\) times its rate, \(F = \eta\,dx/dt\). Differentiate. A displacement ramp \(x = vt\) gives a constant force \(\eta v\) from the first instant. A true displacement step would need an infinite force, because a jump has an infinite rate. So in this tab a displacement "step" is always a fast ramp and a hold: the displacement rises to \(x_0\) over a short ramp time \(t_r\), then stays there. During the ramp the damper moves at \(x_0/t_r\) and pushes back with \(\eta x_0/t_r\); the instant the ramp ends it stops moving and its force drops to zero. From here on \(t_r\) is on a slider. It is a real parameter, not a numerical convenience: shorten it, and the force during the ramp grows without limit.

Hold a damper still and it exerts nothing, however far it has been moved. See that on the bench before anything else in this tab.

Let go. Set \(F = 0\) and the law says \(\dot x = 0\): the damper stops dead, wherever it is, and stays there for ever. It keeps every nanometre — the drift under a held force, the parabola under a ramp, the whole \(x_0\) of a displacement step. Letting go of a displacement step changes nothing at all, because the damper was carrying no force already. A damper has no length it returns to.

In the cell. No gel is a bare damper, but a matrix with no crosslinks — collagen before it has gelled, mucus, the cytoplasm over long times — comes close. Put the adhesion on it and the stall force sinks the adhesion for ever, at \(N_mF_m/\eta\); the clutches' ramp sinks it faster every second; and no bond on it ever holds a load, because nothing holds. Read backwards, a bead moving at a steady speed means a steady force \(\eta v\), whatever the distance, and a bead that has stopped means no force, however far it has been moved. That last sentence is the one to carry into §2.8. And when the adhesion lets go, it leaves its dent in such a matrix for good.

Derive the damper under the four loadings, by the four moves — and the step that costs an infinite force

Move 1, the law: \(F = \eta\,\dot x\). The law has a rate in it. Under force control the rate is what you know, so the solve is an integral: undo the rate. Under displacement control the displacement is what you know, so the solve is a derivative: take its rate.

  1. Step force: \(F = F_0\) for \(t > 0\). Put it in: \(F_0 = \eta\dot x\), so \(\dot x = F_0/\eta\), a constant rate. Integrate from 0 to \(t\), with \(x(0) = 0\):
    $$x(t) = \int_0^t \frac{F_0}{\eta}\,dt' = \frac{F_0}{\eta}\,t$$
    Read: zero at the start, growing for ever at \(F_0/\eta\); no clock and no end.
  2. Force ramp: \(F = \dot F\,t\). Put it in: \(\dot x = \dot F t/\eta\). Integrate:
    $$x(t) = \int_0^t \frac{\dot F\,t'}{\eta}\,dt' = \frac{\dot F}{2\eta}\,t^2$$
    Read: a parabola. The damper moves faster every second because the force does.
  3. Step displacement: \(x = x_0\) for \(t > 0\), reached over \(dt\). The law wants \(\dot x\), so take the rate of the loading. During the climb the displacement rises by \(x_0\) in a time \(dt\), so \(\dot x = x_0/dt\); after it \(x\) is constant, so \(\dot x = 0\). Put both in:
    $$F = \eta\,\frac{x_0}{dt}\ \text{ during the climb},\qquad F = 0\ \text{ after it}$$
    Read: now let the step become a true step, \(dt \to 0\). The force during the climb, \(\eta x_0/dt\), grows without limit, while the time it acts for shrinks to nothing. Their product, the impulse \(\int F\,dt = \eta x_0\), stays finite. So a damper asked for a true step answers with an infinitely high, infinitely brief force, and then with nothing at all, however far it has been moved. Mathematicians call that limit a delta function, \(F = \eta x_0\,\delta(t)\). The bench cannot draw it, so it keeps \(dt\) finite, calls it \(t_r\) and puts it on a slider: halve \(t_r\) and the force during the climb doubles.
  4. Displacement ramp: \(x = v\,t\). Take the rate, \(\dot x = v\), and put it in:
    $$F(t) = \eta\,v$$
    Read: a constant force from the first instant, set by the speed and by nothing else.
  5. Let go at \(t_{off}\): \(F = 0\) for \(t > t_{off}\), under either control. Put it in: \(0 = \eta\dot x\), so \(\dot x = 0\). Integrate from \(t_{off}\), where the loading left it at \(x(t_{off})\):
    $$x(t) = x(t_{off}) + \int_{t_{off}}^{t} 0\,dt' = x(t_{off})$$
    Read: it stops dead and keeps everything. No jump back, no clock, no return.
The damper's four answers are the spring's four with one more derivative or integral in each: a step force gives a ramp, a force ramp gives a parabola, a displacement ramp gives a step, and a step displacement gives the one thing a spring never gives, a force that is infinite for an instant. Let go, and it gives the other thing a spring never gives: it keeps all of it.
Note from Dr. McGhee — on the word "stiffness". A spring has one: ask it for force per displacement, \(F/x\), and it answers \(k\) whenever you ask. A damper does not: ask it the same question and the answer depends on how fast you moved it and how long you have waited since. That is the whole reason this tab exists. When Week 5 said "stiffness is not one number, it depends how fast you pull", it was saying that real matrix has a damper in it somewhere. Everything below is about where, and joined how.

2.4 · Maxwell — a spring and a damper in series

Join a spring \(k\) and a damper \(\eta\) end to end and you have a Maxwell element. §2.1's series rule says what to expect: one force through both parts, and the displacements add, \(x = x_s + x_d\), where \(x_s\) is the spring's stretch and \(x_d\) is how far the damper's piston has slid.

Force control is direct. You set \(F(t)\), and series shares the force, so each part gets it and answers on its own: the spring with \(F/k\) (§2.2), the damper with \(\frac{1}{\eta}\int_0^t F\,dt\) (§2.3). Add the two answers:

$$x(t) = \frac{F(t)}{k} + \frac{1}{\eta}\int_0^t F\,dt$$

A held force \(F_0\) gives \(x = F_0/k + F_0 t/\eta\): a jump (the spring), then a drift that never stops (the damper). A force ramp \(F = \dot F t\) gives \(x = \dot F t/k + \dot F t^2/(2\eta)\).

Displacement control needs an equation. Now you set only the total \(x(t)\). Both parts still carry the same force, but how the displacement divides between them — how much is spring stretch and how much is piston slide — is not known in advance; it depends on everything that has happened so far. So write the series rule for rates instead, one move per line:

  1. The series rule, and its rate. \(x = x_s + x_d\). Take the rate of both sides; the rate of a sum is the sum of the rates:
    $$\dot x = \dot x_s + \dot x_d$$
  2. The spring's rate. \(x_s = F/k\) (eq. 5.5.1), and \(k\) is a constant, so its rate is the force's rate over \(k\):
    $$\dot x_s = \frac{\dot F}{k}$$
  3. The damper's rate. Eq. 6.2.2 gives it directly, with the shared force:
    $$\dot x_d = \frac{F}{\eta}$$
  4. Put both in, and multiply through by \(k\). Then \(k/\eta\) is \(1/\tau\):
    $$\dot x = \frac{\dot F}{k} + \frac{F}{\eta}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k\dot x = \dot F + \frac{k}{\eta}F$$
eq. 6.2.3 — Maxwell $$\dot F + \frac{F}{\tau} = k\,\dot x, \qquad \tau = \frac{\eta}{k}$$

\(\tau\) (tau) \(= \eta/k\) is the element's relaxation time, in seconds (pN·s/nm divided by pN/nm). Give eq. 6.2.3 the \(x(t)\) you impose and it returns \(F(t)\). Stretch the element by \(x_0\) with a fast ramp and hold it: the piston slowly slides, the spring shortens by just as much, and the force relaxes, \(F = k x_0 e^{-t/\tau}\), all the way to zero. Ramp it, \(x = vt\): the force climbs like a spring's at first and levels off at the damper's \(\eta v\), \(F = \eta v\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\). Measure both on the bench; the Derive box below it solves them. A Maxwell element held stretched ends up carrying no force at all: it is a fluid, one that answers a fast pull like a spring.

Let go. Set \(F = 0\), and series hands the zero to both parts. The spring answers at once, \(x_s = 0/k = 0\): it springs back by whatever it was holding, \(F(t_{off})/k\). The damper answers \(\dot x_d = 0\): it stays where it slid. So a Maxwell element let go jumps back by \(F(t_{off})/k\) and keeps the rest for ever — a permanent set, the damper's slide. How much it keeps depends on how long it was loaded: after a held force, \(F_0 t_{off}/\eta\); after a held stretch, \(x_0(1 - e^{-t_{off}/\tau})\), which after a few \(\tau\) is nearly all of it. That is the fluid again, seen from the other end: it keeps whatever its damper slid.

In the cell. A Maxwell ground is a matrix whose crosslinks let go: it answers the clutches' first fraction of a second as a spring, then flows. Under the stall force the adhesion sinks at \(N_mF_m/\eta\) and never stops, so a cell on such a gel can never hold a load, only keep pulling. Under the actin's steady drag the force levels off at \(\eta v\): a ceiling set by the flow, so if \(\eta v\) is below talin's 12 pN no bond on this ground ever reinforces, however long it holds on. Read backwards: a bead that moved and stopped is carrying a force that fades on \(\tau\), and a bead moving steadily reports its speed, not its distance. And when the adhesion lets go, its beads come back only by the spring's share; the gel keeps the rest of the dent. §2.8 puts this gel beside the others under one bead track.

Derive Maxwell under the four loadings, by the four moves

Move 1, the law: the series rule written for rates, \(\dot x = \dot F/k + F/\eta\), which is eq. 6.2.3 before it is multiplied through by \(k\). Under force control the whole right-hand side is known, so integrate: direct. Under displacement control the left-hand side is known and \(F\) appears twice on the right, so it is a differential equation for \(F\).

  1. Step force: \(F = F_0\) for \(t > 0\). Put it in. The jump at \(t = 0\) is taken by the spring alone, which stretches at once by \(F_0/k\); the damper cannot jump. After the jump \(\dot F = 0\), so \(\dot x = F_0/\eta\). Integrate from \(0^+\):
    $$x(t) = \frac{F_0}{k} + \frac{F_0}{\eta}\,t$$
    Read: starts at \(F_0/k\), drifts for ever at \(F_0/\eta\): the spring's jump plus the damper's ramp, §2.2 and §2.3 added.
  2. Force ramp: \(F = \dot F\,t\). Put it in: \(\dot x = \dot F/k + \dot F t/\eta\). Integrate:
    $$x(t) = \frac{\dot F}{k}\,t + \frac{\dot F}{2\eta}\,t^2$$
    Read: the spring's line plus the damper's parabola; from \(t = 2\tau\) on, the parabola is the larger.
  3. Step displacement: \(x = x_0\) for \(t > 0\), reached over \(dt\). During the climb the damper is asked to move fast, and §2.3 says its force would be \(\eta x_0/dt\), far more than the spring's \(kx_0\) when \(dt\) is short; so the damper stands nearly still and the spring takes the whole \(x_0\). That fixes the boundary condition: at the end of the climb, \(F(0^+) = kx_0\). After the climb \(\dot x = 0\), so the law reads
    $$0 = \frac{\dot F}{k} + \frac{F}{\eta}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dot F = -\frac{k}{\eta}\,F = -\frac{F}{\tau},\qquad \tau = \frac{\eta}{k}$$
    This is a differential equation for \(F\): the unknown and its own rate in one line. Solve it in four moves. Separate the variables: put everything with \(F\) on the left and everything with \(t\) on the right, by dividing both sides by \(F\) and multiplying both by \(dt\):
    $$\frac{dF}{F} = -\frac{dt}{\tau}$$
    Integrate both sides. The integral of \(1/F\) is \(\ln F\); the integral of a constant is the constant times \(t\); and an indefinite integral carries a constant of integration, \(C\), that the equation on its own cannot fix:
    $$\int\frac{dF}{F} = -\frac{1}{\tau}\int dt\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ln F = -\frac{t}{\tau} + C$$
    Undo the logarithm by raising \(e\) to both sides, and call the constant \(e^{C}\) by a shorter name, \(A\):
    $$F = e^{-t/\tau + C} = e^{C}\,e^{-t/\tau} = A\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
    Every value of \(A\) solves the equation; the equation does not know which one is ours. Apply the boundary condition: at \(t = 0^+\), \(e^{0} = 1\), so \(F(0^+) = A\); and we know \(F(0^+) = kx_0\). So \(A = kx_0\), and
    $$F(t) = k\,x_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
    Check it three ways: at \(t = 0^+\) it is \(kx_0\); as \(t \to \infty\) it goes to 0; and its rate is \(\dot F = -(kx_0/\tau)\,e^{-t/\tau} = -F/\tau\), the equation we started from. Read: starts at \(kx_0\), falls to zero on the clock \(\tau\). No infinite force this time: the damper has a spring in series to take the step for it.
  4. Displacement ramp: \(x = v\,t\). Take the rate, \(\dot x = v\), and put it in: \(v = \dot F/k + F/\eta\). Multiply through by \(k\) and write it with the unknown's terms on the left:
    $$\dot F + \frac{F}{\tau} = k\,v$$
    The left side is step 3's equation; the right side is a constant instead of zero. An equation like this is solved in three parts. The homogeneous part: set the right side to zero and it is step 3's equation, whose solution we have, \(F_h = A\,e^{-t/\tau}\), for any \(A\). A particular part: any one function that satisfies the whole equation. The right side is a constant, so try a constant, \(F_p\), whose rate is zero:
    $$0 + \frac{F_p}{\tau} = kv\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F_p = kv\tau = \eta\,v$$
    Add them. The equation is linear, so the sum of a particular solution and any homogeneous solution satisfies it, and every solution has that form:
    $$F(t) = \eta v + A\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
    Apply the boundary condition. At \(t = 0\) nothing has been stretched yet, so \(F(0) = 0\): \(0 = \eta v + A\), so \(A = -\eta v\), and
    $$F(t) = \eta\,v\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$
    Check: \(F(0) = 0\); \(F \to \eta v\); and \(\dot F + F/\tau = (\eta v/\tau)e^{-t/\tau} + (\eta v/\tau)(1 - e^{-t/\tau}) = \eta v/\tau = kv\), the equation. Read: for \(t \ll \tau\), \(e^{-t/\tau} \approx 1 - t/\tau\) and \(F \approx kvt\), a spring; for \(t \gg \tau\), \(F \to \eta v\), a damper; the bend is at \(\tau\).
  5. Let go at \(t_{off}\): \(F = 0\) for \(t > t_{off}\), under either control. Now the force is set again — to zero — so this is force control, and direct. At the instant of letting go the force drops from \(F(t_{off})\) to 0. The damper cannot jump, so the drop is taken by the spring alone, which shortens at once by \(F(t_{off})/k\). After it \(F = 0\) and \(\dot F = 0\), so the law reads \(\dot x = 0 + 0/\eta = 0\):
    $$x(t) = x(t_{off}) - \frac{F(t_{off})}{k} = x_d(t_{off})\qquad\text{for every } t > t_{off}$$
    Read: a jump back by \(F(t_{off})/k\), then nothing, for ever. What is kept is the damper's slide: \(F_0 t_{off}/\eta\) after a held force, \(\dot F t_{off}^2/(2\eta)\) after a force ramp, \(x_0(1 - e^{-t_{off}/\tau})\) after a held stretch (put step 3's \(F(t_{off}) = kx_0e^{-t_{off}/\tau}\) in), and \(vt_{off} - v\tau(1 - e^{-t_{off}/\tau})\) after a drag.
Under force control a Maxwell element is a spring plus a damper, answers added. Under displacement control it is a spring for times much shorter than \(\tau = \eta/k\) and a damper for times much longer. Let go, and it gives back the spring's share and keeps the damper's.
The bench's step is a ramp of \(t_r\), so the damper slides a little during it: after the ramp the force sits a few per cent below \(kx_0\) and decays with the same \(\tau\). Shorten \(t_r\) and the two meet.

2.5 · Kelvin–Voigt — a spring and a damper in parallel

Put the same spring and damper side by side between two bars and you have a Kelvin–Voigt element. §2.1's parallel rule: one displacement for both parts, and their forces add.

Displacement control is direct. You set \(x(t)\), and parallel shares it, so each part answers on its own — the spring with \(kx\) (§2.2), the damper with \(\eta\dot x\) (§2.3) — and you add:

eq. 6.2.4 — Kelvin–Voigt $$F = k\,x + \eta\,\dot x$$

A ramp \(x = vt\) gives \(F = kvt + \eta v\): the spring's climb on top of the damper's constant. A step, made as §2.3's fast ramp, costs more: while the displacement climbs, the damper adds \(\eta x_0/t_r\) to the spring's force, so at the end of the ramp the force peaks at \(k x_0 + \eta x_0/t_r\) — a spike that grows without limit as the ramp shortens — and from the instant the ramp ends it is \(k x_0\), for ever. An ideal Kelvin–Voigt element cannot be stepped at all.

Force control needs an equation. Now you set only the total \(F(t)\). Both parts share the displacement, but how the force divides between spring and damper is not known in advance. Solve eq. 6.2.4 for the rate and divide by \(\eta\):

$$\dot x + \frac{x}{\tau} = \frac{F}{\eta}, \qquad \tau = \frac{\eta}{k}$$

— the same relaxation time as Maxwell's, from the same two parts. Hold a force \(F_0\): at first the spring is unstretched and carries nothing, so the damper takes all of it; as \(x\) grows the spring takes over, the damper slows, and \(x\) creeps up to the spring's answer and stops there, \(x = (F_0/k)(1 - e^{-t/\tau}) \to F_0/k\). Ramp the force, \(F = \dot F t\): \(x = (\dot F/k)\bigl(t - \tau(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\bigr)\), which ends up following the spring's \(\dot F t/k\), a fixed \(\dot F\tau/k\) behind it. A Kelvin–Voigt element under a held load comes to rest at a finite stretch: it is a solid, with a delay.

Let go. Set \(F = 0\) in eq. 6.2.4: \(0 = kx + \eta\dot x\). The total force is zero, but the two parts are not idle: the spring, still stretched, pulls back with \(kx\), and the damper resists with exactly as much the other way. \(x\) cannot jump, because the damper will not allow it, so it creeps back, \(x = x(t_{off})\,e^{-(t - t_{off})/\tau}\), all the way to zero on the same \(\tau\) it crept out with. Kelvin–Voigt keeps nothing in the end — a solid comes home — but it takes its time, and it never jumps.

In the cell. A Kelvin–Voigt ground is a crosslinked gel with its water counted in. Under the stall force the adhesion's beads keep moving after the force has stopped changing, creeping toward \(N_mF_m/k\) on \(\tau\), and only then does the ground hold. Under the clutches' ramp the beads lag the force by \(\dot F\tau/k\). Read backwards, a bead moving at \(v\) is carrying \(\eta v\) more than a spring would say, and a bead that has stopped is carrying exactly what a spring would say: the damper's share was only there while the bead moved. When the adhesion lets go, its beads do not snap back; they creep back on \(\tau\).

Move 1, the law: the parallel rule, \(F = kx + \eta\dot x\), eq. 6.2.4. Under displacement control \(x\) and \(\dot x\) are both known, so add: direct. Under force control \(F\) is known and \(x\) appears twice on the right, so it is a differential equation for \(x\).

  1. Step force: \(F = F_0\) for \(t > 0\). Put it in: \(F_0 = kx + \eta\dot x\). Divide through by \(\eta\) and put the unknown's terms on the left:
    $$\dot x + \frac{x}{\tau} = \frac{F_0}{\eta},\qquad \tau = \frac{\eta}{k}$$
    This is §2.4's equation with \(x\) in the place of \(F\), so solve it the same way. Homogeneous part: \(\dot x_h = -x_h/\tau\); separating the variables and integrating exactly as in §2.4 step 3 gives \(x_h = A\,e^{-t/\tau}\). Particular part: the right side is a constant, so try a constant, \(\dot x_p = 0\): \(x_p/\tau = F_0/\eta\), so \(x_p = F_0\tau/\eta = F_0/k\), the spring's own answer. Add: \(x = F_0/k + A\,e^{-t/\tau}\). Boundary condition: the damper cannot jump, so at \(t = 0\) nothing has moved, \(x(0) = 0\): \(0 = F_0/k + A\), so \(A = -F_0/k\), and
    $$x(t) = \frac{F_0}{k}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$
    Check: \(x(0) = 0\); \(x \to F_0/k\); and \(\dot x + x/\tau = (F_0/k\tau)\,e^{-t/\tau} + (F_0/k\tau)(1 - e^{-t/\tau}) = F_0/(k\tau) = F_0/\eta\), the equation. Read: starts at zero, creeps to the spring's answer \(F_0/k\) on the clock \(\tau\), and never passes it.
  2. Force ramp: \(F = \dot F\,t\). Put it in, \(\dot F t = kx + \eta\dot x\), and divide through by \(\eta\):
    $$\dot x + \frac{x}{\tau} = \frac{\dot F}{\eta}\,t$$
    Homogeneous part: \(x_h = A\,e^{-t/\tau}\), as before. Particular part: the right side is now a line, so try a line, \(x_p = at + b\), with \(\dot x_p = a\):
    $$a + \frac{at + b}{\tau} = \frac{\dot F}{\eta}\,t$$
    Two unknowns, \(a\) and \(b\), and the equation must hold at every \(t\), so match the terms in \(t\) and the constant terms separately. Terms in \(t\): \(a/\tau = \dot F/\eta\), so \(a = \dot F\tau/\eta = \dot F/k\). Constants: \(a + b/\tau = 0\), so \(b = -a\tau = -\dot F\tau/k\). Add: \(x = \dot F t/k - \dot F\tau/k + A\,e^{-t/\tau}\). Boundary condition: \(x(0) = 0\), so \(0 = -\dot F\tau/k + A\) and \(A = \dot F\tau/k\):
    $$x(t) = \frac{\dot F}{k}\left[t - \tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\right]$$
    Read: ends on the spring's line \(\dot F t/k\), a fixed \(\dot F\tau/k\) behind it; the damper carries \(\eta\dot x \to \dot F\tau\) of the force.
  3. Step displacement: \(x = x_0\) for \(t > 0\), reached over \(dt\). Direct. Take the rate of the loading, \(\dot x = x_0/dt\) during the climb and 0 after it, and add the two parts:
    $$F = k\,x + \eta\,\frac{x_0}{dt}\ \text{ during the climb},\qquad F = k\,x_0\ \text{ after it}$$
    Read: at the end of the climb the force peaks at \(kx_0 + \eta x_0/dt\), then drops to \(kx_0\) for ever. The second term is §2.3's damper under a step, unchanged: let \(dt \to 0\) and the peak is infinite for an instant, with the impulse \(\eta x_0\). In parallel there is no spring to take the step for the damper, so this element cannot be stepped at all; the bench keeps \(dt = t_r\) finite and draws the spike.
  4. Displacement ramp: \(x = v\,t\). Direct: \(\dot x = v\), so
    $$F(t) = k\,v\,t + \eta\,v$$
    Read: the damper's constant \(\eta v\) from the first instant, with the spring's climb at \(kv\) on top; it never levels off.

2.6 · Anything more complex is just another element added

Neither Maxwell nor Kelvin–Voigt is a gel. Maxwell flows away under a held load; Kelvin–Voigt cannot be moved quickly at all. A crosslinked gel does three things: it answers a fast pull at once, like a spring; it holds a load for ever, like a spring; and in between it relaxes, over a relaxation time of its own. That needs no new part, only one more joint. The recipe for any element, however complicated: take an element you already understand, add one more part in series or in parallel, apply §2.1's rule at the new joint, and reuse the answers of §2.2–§2.5.

The first build is the standard linear solid, also called the Zener element. Take §2.4's Maxwell element — call its spring \(k_1\) and its damper \(\eta\) — and add a lone spring \(k_\infty\) beside it, in parallel. The new joint is parallel: it shares the displacement, and the forces of the two branches add.

Under displacement control nothing new is needed. The \(x\) you set goes straight to both branches, so add §2.2's answer for the lone spring to §2.4's answer for the Maxwell arm. For a held stretch \(x_0\), with \(\tau = \eta/k_1\):

$$F(t) = k_\infty x_0 + k_1 x_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$$

It starts at \((k_1 + k_\infty)\,x_0\) and relaxes — but only to \(k_\infty x_0\), because the lone spring holds its share for ever.

Under force control the new joint does not share the force. The \(F\) you set is split between the lone spring and the arm, and the split changes as the arm's damper slides. So the two laws have to be combined into one, one move per line:

  1. The parallel rule at the new joint. The forces add and the displacement is shared: \(F = F_{spring} + F_{arm}\), with the same \(x\) in both branches. The lone spring obeys eq. 5.5.1, \(F_{spring} = k_\infty x\), so the arm carries what is left:
    $$F_{arm} = F - k_\infty x$$
  2. The arm's own law. The arm is §2.4's Maxwell element with spring \(k_1\) and damper \(\eta\), so it obeys eq. 6.2.3 with its own force and the shared \(x\):
    $$\dot F_{arm} + \frac{F_{arm}}{\tau} = k_1\dot x,\qquad \tau = \frac{\eta}{k_1}$$
  3. Put the arm's force in. Its rate is \(\dot F_{arm} = \dot F - k_\infty\dot x\), because \(k_\infty\) is a constant:
    $$\big(\dot F - k_\infty\dot x\big) + \frac{F - k_\infty x}{\tau} = k_1\dot x$$
  4. Multiply through by \(\tau\) and collect. Force terms on the left, displacement terms on the right, and name \(k_1 + k_\infty = k_0\):
    $$\tau\dot F - \tau k_\infty\dot x + F - k_\infty x = \tau k_1\dot x\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F + \tau\dot F = k_\infty x + \tau\,(k_1 + k_\infty)\,\dot x$$
eq. 6.2.5 — the standard linear solid $$F + \tau\,\dot F = k_\infty\,x + \tau\,k_0\,\dot x, \qquad k_0 = k_1 + k_\infty,\quad \tau = \frac{\eta}{k_1},\quad \tau_\varepsilon = \tau\,\frac{k_0}{k_\infty}$$

Read its two stiffnesses. Pull it fast and the damper has no time to move, so both springs act at once: \(k_0 = k_1 + k_\infty\), the instantaneous stiffness. Wait, and the damper lets the arm go slack, leaving the lone spring: \(k_\infty\), the equilibrium stiffness. It has two times as well. Hold it stretched and the force relaxes from \(k_0 x_0\) to \(k_\infty x_0\) on \(\tau = \eta/k_1\). Hold a force \(F_0\) on it and it creeps from \(F_0/k_0\) to \(F_0/k_\infty\) — on a longer time, \(\tau_\varepsilon = \tau k_0/k_\infty\), longer by the ratio of the two stiffnesses. (Put \(F = F_0\) into eq. 6.2.5: \(\dot F = 0\), and the equation for \(x\) is \(\dot x + x/\tau_\varepsilon = F_0/(\tau k_0)\).) It is easy to assume a material has one relaxation time. The simplest solid that relaxes already has two, and which one you see depends on which quantity you control.

Let go. Put \(F = 0\) into eq. 6.2.5: \(0 = k_\infty x + \tau k_0\dot x\), so \(\dot x = -x/\tau_\varepsilon\) — the creep clock again, because letting go is force control. At the instant of letting go the arm's damper is rigid, so the element answers the drop in force as a spring of \(k_0\): \(x\) jumps back by \(F(t_{off})/k_0\). Then it creeps the rest of the way to zero on \(\tau_\varepsilon\). The same two moves work for anything you build, and give one rule. Let go, and \(x\) jumps back at once by \(F(t_{off})/k(0^+)\), every damper rigid for that instant — no jump at all if some path from end to end is dampers alone. Then, if \(k(\infty) > 0\), because some path from end to end is springs alone, it creeps all the way back to zero; if \(k(\infty) = 0\), it keeps a permanent set, whatever its dampers slid. A solid forgets the load; a fluid keeps a record of it. (The harness checks the rule on 200 random trees.)

Everything Week 5's §5.4 hand-waved is in this one element: the ground a clutch feels depends on how fast it pulls, and the two limits are \(k_0\) and \(k_\infty\). Chaudhuri and colleagues made gels that start equally stiff but relax faster or slower, and found that cell spreading, proliferation and stem-cell fate all track the relaxation, not the starting stiffness alone. The second bench below puts the clutch on this element to see why it might.

Two more builds need no new equation at all. Burgers: a Maxwell element in series with a Kelvin–Voigt element. The joint is series, so under force control you add §2.4's answer to §2.5's: a jump, a delayed creep toward a limit, and a drift that never stops. Generalised Maxwell: several Maxwell arms, each with its own relaxation time, side by side with one lone spring. Every joint is parallel, so under displacement control you add every arm's relaxing force to the spring's: after a held stretch the force falls in stages, one per arm, and settles on the lone spring's share.

Whatever you build, one last number puts it back under a clutch, which only knows how to pull on a spring. It is the single-spring surrogate,

eq. 6.2.6 — the single-spring surrogate $$k_{eff}(t) = \frac{F(t)}{x(t)}$$

— the stiffness a spring would need to give this element's force at this displacement, at this moment. For a spring it is \(k\), for ever. For anything with a damper in it, it changes with time, and its two ends are the instantaneous stiffness \(k(0^+)\) (every damper rigid) and the equilibrium stiffness \(k(\infty)\) (every damper slack), which the bench reads straight off the tree you build. For §2.4's worked example it puts a number on Week 5's "stiffness is a rate": dragged at a steady speed, the Maxwell ground has \(k_{eff} = \eta(1 - e^{-t/\tau})/t\), which is 0.95 pN/nm after 0.2 s and 0.2 pN/nm after 10 s. A pull that lasts 0.2 s meets a ground nearly five times stiffer than one that lasts 10 s.

Derive how the page solves any tree you build

The page never writes down an equation like 6.2.3 or 6.2.5 for your tree. It applies §2.1's two rules at every joint, over and over, one short time step \(\Delta t\) at a time.

  1. Every part reports one line for the next step. The force it will carry after the step, if it moves by \(\Delta x\) during it, is \(F_{n+1} = K\,\Delta x + F_h\), where \(F_h\) is the force it would carry if it did not move. A spring gives \(K = k\) and \(F_h = k x_n\), its present force. A damper, stepped implicitly, gives \(K = \eta/\Delta t\) and \(F_h = 0\).
  2. Each joint combines its parts' lines by eq. 6.2.1. A parallel joint shares \(\Delta x\) and adds forces, so it adds the \(K\)'s and the \(F_h\)'s. A series joint shares the force and adds the \(\Delta x\)'s, so it adds the inverse stiffnesses:
    $$\text{parallel: } K = \sum K_i,\;\; F_h = \sum F_{h,i} \qquad \text{series: } \frac{1}{K} = \sum \frac{1}{K_i},\;\; F_h = K\sum\frac{F_{h,i}}{K_i}$$
  3. Impose, solve, hand down, repeat. At the top of the tree, set \(\Delta x\) (displacement control) or \(F\) (force control) — \(F = 0\) before the load arrives and after it is let go — and solve the top line for the other. Hand each part its share — the same \(\Delta x\) to every branch of a parallel joint, the same \(F\) to every part of a series joint — and step again.
That is eq. 6.2.1 at every joint, once per step — and it is why the builder can run any tree you make, under either control.

Check yourself

1. A Maxwell element and a Kelvin–Voigt element have the same \(k\) and \(\eta\). Under a held stretch, which relaxes? Under a held force, which creeps to a limit? Which could be a crosslinked gel?

Answer

Maxwell relaxes (held stretch → force to zero) and creeps without limit. Kelvin–Voigt creeps to \(F_0/k\) and, once the fast ramp is over, does not relax at all under a held stretch. Neither is a gel on its own; the standard linear solid — a spring beside a Maxwell arm — is the smallest element that relaxes to a nonzero force and creeps to a finite stretch.

2. Two dampers in series, then two in parallel. What are the equivalent viscosities?

Answer

Series: one force, and the rates add, so \(1/\eta = 1/\eta_1 + 1/\eta_2\). Parallel: one rate, and the forces add, so \(\eta = \eta_1 + \eta_2\). The same rules as springs, with rates in place of stretches.

3. Under which control is the standard linear solid direct, and why?

Answer

Displacement control. Its top joint is parallel, which shares the displacement, so you add the lone spring's \(k_\infty x\) to the Maxwell arm's answer. Under force control that joint splits the force between its branches, which is why eq. 6.2.5 is needed.

4. An element's \(k_{eff}\) under a force step falls from 4 to 1 pN/nm. What can you say about its tree?

Answer

It starts finite, so no path from one end to the other is dampers alone (a fast pull would meet a rigid rod). It ends nonzero, so some path from end to end is springs alone (a held force stays balanced). At least a spring in parallel with something that contains a damper — a standard linear solid or richer.

5. Two elements are each let go after the same held force. Both jump back by the same amount at once; one then creeps all the way home, the other stops partway and stays. What do the two have in common, and what tells them apart?

Answer

The same jump back means the same \(k(0^+)\): the drop in force is \(F(t_{off})\) in both, and the jump is \(F(t_{off})/k(0^+)\). The one that comes home has \(k(\infty) > 0\), a path of springs alone that pulls it back; the one that stays has \(k(\infty) = 0\), every path through a damper, and keeps what its dampers slid. Letting go tells a solid from a fluid in one run.

Note from Dr. McGhee — where even this element fails. A real gel does not have one \(\tau\). It has a spread of them — many Maxwell arms in parallel, each a different piece of the network letting go on its own relaxation time — and the generalised Maxwell preset is the smallest honest version. Two more things are missing altogether. A hydrogel is mostly water, and its relaxation is partly the water moving through the mesh, which is poroelasticity: a relaxation time set by how far the water has to go, so it scales with the square of the size of the thing pushing. And collagen stiffens as it is stretched, which no linear element does. The capstone's ledger lists all three.

Derive the damper's \(k_{eff}(t)\) under the four loadings — \(\eta\) over a time, and the loading picks the time

From §2.3's Derive box. A damper has no stiffness of its own; eq. 6.2.6 manufactures one out of \(\eta\) and whatever time the loading supplies.

  1. Step force. \(x = F_0 t/\eta\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{F_0}{F_0 t/\eta} = \frac{\eta}{t}$$
    Read: \(\infty\) at \(0^+\) (nothing has moved yet), falling to \(0\) as the piston drifts.
  2. Force ramp. \(x = \dot F t^2/(2\eta)\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{\dot F t}{\dot F t^2/(2\eta)} = \frac{2\eta}{t}$$
    Read: twice the step force's value at the same \(t\), because the ramp's force was smaller earlier and less has moved. Same element, same instant, a different number.
  3. Step displacement, reached over \(dt\). During the climb \(x = x_0 t/dt\) and \(F = \eta x_0/dt\), so \(k_{eff} = \eta/t\), which at the end of the climb is \(\eta/dt\), and grows without limit as \(dt \to 0\): §2.3's delta function, now as a stiffness. After the climb \(F = 0\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{\eta}{dt} \to \infty\ \text{as the climb ends},\qquad k_{eff} = 0\ \text{after it}$$
  4. Displacement ramp. \(F = \eta v\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{\eta v}{v t} = \frac{\eta}{t}$$
    The same as the step force's — a coincidence of this element, not a rule, as Maxwell's box shows.
\(\eta/t\), \(2\eta/t\), \(\eta/dt\) then \(0\), \(\eta/t\): the surrogate is \(\eta\) divided by a time, and the time is the loading's, not the element's. A damper has no \(k\).
Derive Maxwell's \(k_{eff}(t)\) under the four loadings — four different functions

From §2.4's Derive box, with \(\tau = \eta/k\). Where the ratio has \(1/k + t/\eta\) in it, multiply top and bottom by \(k\) and use \(k/\eta = 1/\tau\).

  1. Step force. \(x = F_0/k + F_0 t/\eta\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{F_0}{F_0/k + F_0 t/\eta} = \frac{1}{1/k + t/\eta} = \frac{k}{1 + t/\tau}$$
    Read: \(k\) at \(0^+\), falling to \(0\) as \(1/t\) — a hyperbola.
  2. Force ramp. \(x = \dot F t/k + \dot F t^2/(2\eta)\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{\dot F t}{\dot F t/k + \dot F t^2/(2\eta)} = \frac{1}{1/k + t/(2\eta)} = \frac{k}{1 + t/2\tau}$$
    Read: \(k\) to \(0\) again, but half as fast — the ramp has pushed less fluid through the damper by the same \(t\).
  3. Step displacement. \(F = k x_0 e^{-t/\tau}\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{k x_0 e^{-t/\tau}}{x_0} = k\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
    Read: \(k\) to \(0\), exponentially, on \(\tau\). This is the relaxation stiffness \(k_x(t)\).
  4. Displacement ramp. \(F = \eta v\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{\eta v\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)}{v t} = \frac{\eta}{t}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) = k\,\frac{\tau}{t}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$
    Read: at \(0^+\), \(1 - e^{-t/\tau} \approx t/\tau\), so \(k_{eff} \to k\); at long times it falls as \(\eta/t\). This is §2.6's "0.95 pN/nm after 0.2 s, 0.2 after 10 s".
One element, two constants, four surrogates: an exponential, two hyperbolas and one in between. At \(t = \tau\) they read \(0.50k\), \(0.67k\), \(0.37k\) and \(0.63k\) — nearly two to one apart for the same element at the same moment. None of the four is a property of the element alone.
Derive Kelvin–Voigt's \(k_{eff}(t)\) under the four loadings — every column starts at infinity

From §2.5's Derive box, with \(\tau = \eta/k\).

  1. Step force. \(x = (F_0/k)\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{F_0}{(F_0/k)\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)} = \frac{k}{1 - e^{-t/\tau}}$$
    Read: \(\infty\) at \(0^+\) (the damper has not let anything move), settling to \(k\).
  2. Force ramp. \(x = (\dot F/k)\left[t - \tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\right]\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{\dot F t}{(\dot F/k)\left[t - \tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\right]} = \frac{k\,t}{t - \tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)}$$
    Read: expand the exponential to second order and the bracket is \(t^2/(2\tau)\) at small \(t\), so \(k_{eff} \approx 2k\tau/t = 2\eta/t \to \infty\) — the damper's own ramp answer, because at first the Kelvin–Voigt element is its damper; settles to \(k\).
  3. Step displacement, reached over \(dt\). §2.5's answer during the climb is the spring's \(k x\) plus the damper's \(\eta x_0/dt\), so at the end of the climb
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{k x_0 + \eta x_0/dt}{x_0} = k + \frac{\eta}{dt} \to \infty\ \text{as } dt \to 0,\qquad k_{eff} = k\ \text{after it}$$
  4. Displacement ramp. \(F = k v t + \eta v\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{k v t + \eta v}{v t} = k + \frac{\eta}{t}$$
    Read: \(\infty\) to \(k\).
Every column starts infinite and ends at \(k\). Put into eq. 6.1.1, an infinite \(k_{eff}\) makes the ground Week 5's rigid floor, \(\kappa_{eff} = n\kappa_c\), for the instant the loading has just begun — with an infinite force behind it in the two step columns.
Derive the standard linear solid's \(k_{eff}(t)\) under the four loadings — from \(k_0\) to \(k_\infty\) by four roads

From §2.6's Derive box, with \(k_0 = k_1 + k_\infty\), \(\tau = \eta/k_1\) and \(\tau_\varepsilon = \tau k_0/k_\infty\).

  1. Step force. \(x = F_0/k_\infty - (F_0/k_\infty - F_0/k_0)\,e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{1}{1/k_\infty - (1/k_\infty - 1/k_0)\,e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}}$$
    Read: at \(0^+\) the denominator is \(1/k_0\), so \(k_{eff} = k_0\); at \(\infty\) it is \(1/k_\infty\). From \(k_0\) to \(k_\infty\) on the creep clock \(\tau_\varepsilon\). This is the creep stiffness \(k_F(t)\).
  2. Force ramp. \(x = \dot F t/k_\infty - (\tau\dot F k_1/k_\infty^2)\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\right)\); divide \(\dot F t\) by it and cancel \(\dot F\):
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{1}{1/k_\infty - \dfrac{\tau k_1}{k_\infty^2\,t}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\right)}$$
    Read: at \(0^+\), \(1 - e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon} \approx t/\tau_\varepsilon\), and \(\tau/\tau_\varepsilon = k_\infty/k_0\), so the denominator is \(1/k_\infty - k_1/(k_\infty k_0) = (k_0 - k_1)/(k_\infty k_0) = 1/k_0\): \(k_{eff} = k_0\). At \(\infty\), \(k_\infty\).
  3. Step displacement. \(F = (k_\infty + k_1 e^{-t/\tau})\,x_0\), so
    $$k_{eff} = k_\infty + k_1\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
    Read: \(k_0\) to \(k_\infty\) on the relaxation clock \(\tau\). This is the relaxation stiffness \(k_x(t)\).
  4. Displacement ramp. \(F = k_\infty v t + k_1 v\tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\), so
    $$k_{eff} = \frac{k_\infty v t + k_1 v \tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)}{v t} = k_\infty + k_1\frac{\tau}{t}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$
    Read: \(k_0\) to \(k_\infty\), the arm's share falling as \(\eta/t\).
All four run from \(k_0\) to \(k_\infty\) — the two numbers the drop-in used — but on different clocks (\(\tau\) under displacement, \(\tau_\varepsilon = \tau k_0/k_\infty\) under a held force) and along different curves. For the worked example's element (\(k_0 = 4\), \(k_\infty = 1\) pN/nm, \(\tau = 2\) s, \(\tau_\varepsilon = 8\) s) the four columns read \(2.40\), \(2.97\), \(2.10\) and \(2.90\) pN/nm at \(t = 2\) s.

The problems with doing this

1 · There is no one \(k_{eff}(t)\). The table has four columns, and in every row but the spring's they are four different functions. Maxwell's surrogate is an exponential if you held a displacement and a hyperbola if you held a force; at \(t = \tau\) the four columns read \(0.50k\), \(0.67k\), \(0.37k\) and \(0.63k\) for the same element at the same moment. A spring's \(k\) is a property of the spring; \(k_{eff}(t)\) is a property of the experiment that produced it. Even the two named ones disagree: the relaxation stiffness never exceeds the creep stiffness, \(k_x(t) \le k_F(t)\), with equality only for a spring (the harness checks every element on this page), so a gel looks softer when you hold its displacement than when you hold its force. Before "plugging in \(k_{eff}(t)\)" you must therefore say which \(k_{eff}(t)\) — and the cell's loading is none of the four. It is a sawtooth of load and release that the clutches and the gel settle between them, and it has no column in the table.

2 · It multiplies where the physics adds up a history. Writing \(F(t) = k_{eff}(t)\,x(t)\) treats the surrogate as a spring constant that happens to drift. But \(k_x(t)\) means something else: it is how much force a step made \(t\) seconds ago is still carrying. An element with a damper in it remembers every step it has been given, and its force now is the sum over all of them, each weighted by how much of its stiffness is left:

eq. 6.2.7 — Boltzmann's superposition: the honest use of a relaxation stiffness $$F(t) = \int_0^t k_x(t - t')\,\dot x(t')\,dt'$$

Here \(\dot x(t')\,dt'\) is the small step of displacement made at the earlier time \(t'\), and \(k_x(t - t')\) is what is left of it now. For a loading that starts with a jump \(x(0^+)\), that first step adds \(k_x(t)\,x(0^+)\). This is the only legitimate way to use a relaxation stiffness on a loading other than the one it was measured under; the Derive box below gets it from nothing but the element being linear. The bottom chart shows the difference on the standard linear solid. Hold 10 pN on the worked example's solid: after 8 s it has crept to 7.2 nm, the relaxation stiffness has fallen to 1.05 pN/nm, and the product says 7.6 pN — a quarter below the 10 pN that is plainly there. The sum says 10.0 pN. On Maxwell under a steady drag the failure is total: the product \(k e^{-t/\tau}\cdot vt\) goes to zero while the real force levels off at \(\eta v\) (§2.4); after five \(\tau\) the product is thirty times too small.

3 · Whose clock? Every \(k_{eff}(t)\) has a \(t = 0\): the instant its loading began. Tab 1's equations have no such instant. Eq. 6.1.2 was derived with \(\kappa_{eff}\) a constant — its differential equation had a constant \(\tau_L\) — and the gel under an adhesion has been loaded by every earlier cycle: when a bond lets go, a Maxwell damper never slides back, and a standard linear solid's takes a few \(\tau_\varepsilon\) to (the benches' release shows both). Each bond has its own binding time; the adhesion has another; the gel's memory runs on a third. "Which \(t\) goes in" is a choice the mathematics does not make for you, and different choices give different tractions. The drop-in did not choose. It carried the element's state — the damper's position — alongside the clutches and let eq. 6.2.1 settle the shared displacement at every step. That is what plugging an element into Tab 1 honestly costs: not a new number, but a state.

4 · It hands you infinities and zeros where the model has run out. The damper's and Kelvin–Voigt's columns start at infinity (§2.3's delta): put into eq. 6.1.1 that is Week 5's rigid ground, \(\kappa_{eff} \to n\kappa_c\), for the instant \(dt\) lasts, with an infinite force behind it. The damper's and Maxwell's columns end at zero: \(\kappa_{eff} \to 0\), \(\tau_L \to \infty\), \(\dot f \to 0\), and eq. 6.1.3 gives the bond no chance — a clutch on a fluid never loads. Both are correct as limits. But a formula that hands you \(\infty\) or \(0\) is telling you the question has changed: it is no longer "what is the stiffness" but "who wins the race between the bond's lifetime and the gel's clock", and a single number cannot hold a race.

5 · The bond wants a rate, and that is the way out. What eq. 6.1.3's bond actually needs is \(\dot f\) during its own life: the time to reach talin's threshold, \(t_T = F_T/\dot f\) (0.1–0.3 s at Tab 1's rates), and the second or so it then holds. Compare those two times with the gel's \(\tau\) and three honest answers appear. Both much shorter than \(\tau\): the damper has not moved, use \(k(0^+)\) — Tab 1 on the spring \(k_0\). Both much longer than \(\tau\): the damper is slack, use \(k(\infty)\) — Tab 1 on \(k_\infty\). In between, no single number is right, and the drop-in's result says how wrong the best one would be: the measured traction sits above both spring lines, which no \(k_{eff}\) between \(k_0\) and \(k_\infty\) can produce. That excess is the gel being stiff while a bond loads and soft while it holds — a rate, not a number — and it is why §2.6 rebuilt the bench instead of plugging in.

Derive eq. 6.2.7 from the element being linear — and two checks
  1. Every element in this tab is linear. A spring's \(F = kx\) and a damper's \(F = \eta\dot x\) are both proportional laws, and eq. 6.2.1's two rules only add forces or add displacements, so any tree of them is linear too: double the loading and the answer doubles; apply two loadings together and the two answers add.
  2. Any displacement history is a sum of small steps. Between \(t'\) and \(t' + dt'\) the displacement grows by \(\dot x(t')\,dt'\). The whole history up to \(t\) is all those small steps, made one after another, plus the jump \(x(0^+)\) if the loading started with one. A step back — the jump when a load is let go, the creep home after it — is a step of negative size, and counts the same way.
  3. The element's answer to one small step is known. A step of size \(\Delta x\) made at time \(t'\) is, on its own, a step displacement; by the definition of the relaxation stiffness it is carrying \(k_x(t - t')\,\Delta x\) at the later time \(t\).
  4. Add them. By step 1 the answers to the separate steps add:
    $$F(t) = k_x(t)\,x(0^+) + \sum_{\text{steps}} k_x(t - t')\,\dot x(t')\,dt' \;\longrightarrow\; k_x(t)\,x(0^+) + \int_0^t k_x(t - t')\,\dot x(t')\,dt'$$
    That is eq. 6.2.7, with the jump written out. The page computes it exactly as the sum on the left, bin by bin, from the relaxation stiffness the solver measures for the element on the bench.
  5. Check it against §2.4. Maxwell dragged at \(v\): \(x(0^+) = 0\), \(\dot x = v\), \(k_x(s) = k e^{-s/\tau}\). Then
    $$F(t) = v\int_0^t k\,e^{-(t-t')/\tau}\,dt' = k v\tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) = \eta v\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$
    — §2.4's ramp answer, obtained from the step answer alone. The product \(k_x(t)\,x(t) = k e^{-t/\tau}\cdot vt\) is not it, and goes to zero instead of to \(\eta v\).
  6. Check it against a held force. Under a step force the force is \(F_0\) at every \(t > 0\), so feeding the creep \(x(t) = F_0/k_F(t)\) into eq. 6.2.7 must return the flat line \(F_0\) — the sum and the true force coincide in the bottom chart, and the harness holds them within 1 % on the standard linear solid. Multiplying instead gives \(k_x(t)\,x(t) = F_0\,k_x(t)/k_F(t)\), which is below \(F_0\) by exactly problem 1's ratio.
The relaxation stiffness is a weight over the past, not a stiffness in the present. The two step columns of the table are tied to each other through this integral and through nothing simpler, which is why they are different functions and why neither can be "plugged in".
What you may legitimately put into Tab 1. Two springs — \(k(0^+)\) where the bond's loading and its lifetime are both short beside the gel's \(\tau\), and \(k(\infty)\) where both are long — and, between them, a state rather than a number. Everything a \(k_{eff}(t)\) says that those two limits do not is a statement about one particular loading, and the cell's loading is not in the table.
Check yourself

1. Maxwell's surrogate under a held displacement is \(k e^{-t/\tau}\); under a steady drag it is \(k(\tau/t)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\). Get the second from the first without §2.4's equation.

Answer

Eq. 6.2.7 with \(\dot x = v\): \(F = v\int_0^t k e^{-s/\tau}ds = k v\tau(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\). Divide by \(x = vt\). The step answer contains the ramp answer; the product \(k e^{-t/\tau}\cdot vt\) does not.

2. Someone measures \(k_F(t)\) by creep and uses it as the kernel in eq. 6.2.7. Which way is every force they compute biased?

Answer

High. \(k_F(t) \ge k_x(t)\) at every \(t\), so each past step is weighted by too much, and a held force is "recovered" as more than \(F_0\). The kernel of eq. 6.2.7 is the relaxation stiffness and only that.

3. Where in Tab 1 is there a legitimate place for \(k(0^+)\) on its own?

Answer

Wherever the bond's whole life — loading to \(F_T\) and holding — is over before the gel can relax: \(t_T\) and the Bell lifetime both \(\ll \tau\). Then \(\dot f = \kappa_{eff}v_0\) with \(\kappa_s = k_0\) in eq. 6.1.1, and the drop-in agrees: at its longest \(\tau\) the traction sits on the \(k_0\) line.

2.8 · From the beads back to the force — displacement control is what we measure

Every bench so far let you choose what to set. The cell does not offer that choice, and neither does the microscope. The motors set a relation between force and speed, not a force; the clutches set nothing on their own; and what an experiment records is the gel's displacement, bead by bead, frame by frame. So in the lab the problem is always the same one: here is \(x(t)\); what was \(F(t)\)? That is displacement control, read backwards, and Tab 1 §1.5 already did it once, for one gel. It took the gel to be a spring, so that every bead's displacement was a force at that instant, \(F = \kappa_s x\), with no time in it. This section asks what happens to that reading when the gel is not a spring.

The bench imposes one bead track on four gels at once. The track is either a step, a bead that moves \(x_0\) in a short time \(t_r\) and then sits still, which is what a bead does when an adhesion forms over it and holds, or when a pipette pushes and holds; or a ramp, a bead moving at a steady \(v\), which is what a bead under a clutch does while the actin drags it. The four gels have the same \(k\) and \(\eta\): Hooke's spring (§2.2), Maxwell (§2.4), Kelvin–Voigt (§2.5), and the standard linear solid of §2.6 with \(k_\infty = k\) and an arm of \(3k\), so that three of the four hold the same force at rest. Each gel turns the same track into a different force, by its own law. Then, like every bench in this tab, the run ends by letting go (§2.1): the cell releases the adhesion, the force on every gel is zero from then on, and the beads, which until then all followed one track, report four different gels:

3.1 · From a spring to a modulus — force becomes stress, displacement becomes strain

Everything so far has been one force against one displacement. Pull on something with a force \(F\), in piconewtons; its end moves by \(x\), in nanometres; and over the range this course works in, the two are proportional, \(F = kx\) (Week 5's eq. 5.5.1). The constant \(k\) is the stiffness, in pN/nm: the slope of the force–displacement line. The clutch had one, \(\kappa_c\). The gel under an adhesion had one, \(\kappa_s\). Tab 2 wired elements out of them. This tab starts from a fact Tab 1 kept meeting and never resolved: a stiffness belongs to an object, not to a material. Grip the same gel through an adhesion twice as wide and \(\kappa_s\) doubles, though the gel is the same gel. Two labs pulling on one gel through different grips would report two different springs. What is the number that belongs to the gel itself?

The way to find it is to build a piece of gel out of parts whose stiffness you know, and watch what the parts' arrangement does to the whole. Think of the gel as made of identical small cubes, each 1 µm on a side. One cube, pulled along one of its edges, is a spring: it has some stiffness \(k_1\), and \(F = k_1x\) holds for it. Now Tab 2's two rules (eq. 6.2.1) say what happens when cubes are joined. Cubes side by side are in parallel: they share one displacement and their forces add, so \(n_A\) cubes side by side are \(n_A\) times as stiff as one. Cubes end to end are in series: they share one force and their displacements add, so \(n_L\) cubes end to end are \(n_L\) times as soft as one. A bar that is \(n_A\) cubes across and \(n_L\) cubes long should therefore be a spring of

$$k = k_1\,\frac{n_A}{n_L}$$

The bench measures it before you believe it. Pull a bar of cubes with a force \(F\), read how far its end moves, and the stiffness is the ratio, \(k = F/x\). Change the shape and \(k\) changes. Then look at the two other numbers the stats work out, the force per square micron of cross-section and the stretch per micron of length, and watch what their ratio does. The last slider picks the gel. Its number, \(E\), is exactly the stiffness of one cube (\(k_1 = E \times 1\) µm), and by the end of this section it will have a name.

  1. One cube, 50 pN, on the 1 kPa gel. Read \(x\) and \(k = F/x\). Add a second cube side by side: \(k\) doubles and \(x\) halves. Put the second cube end to end instead: \(k\) halves and \(x\) doubles. Say which of Tab 2's two rules each move used.
  2. For each of those three shapes read the stress \(F/A\) and the strain \(x/L\) in the stats. Their ratio does not move. Now change the gel: the ratio moves, and nothing else you did could move it.
  3. Visit four or five shapes on one gel. On the left chart every shape has its own force–displacement line. On the right chart all of them fall on one line. Read its slope and compare it with the gel you set.
  4. Set 4 cubes along, 1 across, 200 pN, on the softest gel. The strain is beyond ten per cent. Say why the bench's straight-line rule stops being a safe guide there, and what a real gel might do instead.

What the bench showed, and what to do with it. Every shape has its own \(k\), and \(k_1n_A/n_L\) predicts each one. But shapes are not what we want to know about. The following moves take the shape out, one at a time.

  1. Write the counts as lengths. Each cube has side \(\ell\) (1 µm here). The bar's cross-section area is \(A = n_A\ell^2\) and its length is \(L = n_L\ell\), so \(n_A = A/\ell^2\) and \(n_L = L/\ell\). Put them in:
    $$k = k_1\,\frac{n_A}{n_L} = k_1\,\frac{A/\ell^2}{L/\ell} = \frac{k_1}{\ell}\cdot\frac{A}{L}$$
  2. Name what is left. The bar's stiffness is a number that belongs to the cubes, \(k_1/\ell\), times a shape, \(A/L\). The number is the same for every bar cut from the same gel, however many cubes it has. Call it the gel's modulus, \(E\) (Young's modulus, after the person who first used it):
    $$E = \frac{k_1}{\ell}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k = \frac{E\,A}{L}$$
    The cubes were a way to count. Real gel has no cubes in it. The point is only that any piece of gel, of any size, can be thought of as made of smaller pieces, and the two rules do the rest.
  3. Put the bar's \(k\) into the spring law. \(F = kx\) becomes \(F = (EA/L)\,x\). Divide both sides by \(A\), and write the \(L\) under the \(x\):
    $$F = \frac{EA}{L}\,x\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{F}{A} = E\,\frac{x}{L}$$
    Both sides are now shape-free. On the left is the force divided by the area that carries it: the same pull spread over twice the area is half as hard on each part of it. On the right is the stretch divided by the length being stretched: the same stretch spread over twice the length is half as much stretch for each part.
  4. Give the two ratios names. Force per area is stress, \(\sigma\). Stretch per length is strain, \(\varepsilon\). The spring law, with the shape divided out, is \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\): the same straight line as \(F = kx\), with \(E\) as its slope.
eq. 5.5.2 again — stress, strain, and the modulus $$\sigma = \frac{F}{A}, \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L}, \qquad \sigma = E\,\varepsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = \frac{EA}{L}$$

Week 5 wrote this box as a preview and asked you to take it on trust. You have now built it from a spring and the two rules. \(\Delta L\) is the bar's stretch, the \(x\) above. Units. A piconewton over a square micron is a pascal (Pa), the unit of stress; a thousand of them is a kilopascal, and soft gels are quoted in kPa. Strain is a length over a length, so it has no units, and the page writes it in per cent. \(E\) is a stress over a strain, so it is in pascals too. And the exchange between the two languages is exact: 1 kPa times 1 µm is 1 pN/nm, which is why a 1 µm cube of a 1 kPa gel was a spring of exactly 1 pN/nm on the bench.

Now the gel under an adhesion. It is not a bar. The adhesion pulls sideways on the top of a thick block, and there is no single cross-section carrying the force and no single length being stretched. Compare it with the bar. For the bar, \(k = E \times (A/L)\): the modulus times a shape, and the shape \(A/L\) is an area over a length, which is a length. For the adhesion, \(\kappa_s = E \times (\tfrac49\pi a)\): the modulus times a shape again, and the shape is the adhesion's radius with a number in front. The number, \(\tfrac49\pi\), comes from the gel being unable to change its volume, which §3.4 explains. So \(\kappa_s\) is "the stiffness of a grip of size \(a\) on a gel of modulus \(E\)", exactly as \(k\) was "the stiffness of a bar of shape \(A/L\) cut from it". The units work out kindly: a 1 kPa gel under a 1 µm adhesion is a spring of 1.40 pN/nm, the same size as the clutch's own 0.8.

The optimum is set by \(E\cdot a\), not by \(E\).

3.2 · Strain, read between two beads — a stretch is a difference of two displacements

§3.1 defined strain on a bar you hold: the stretch of the whole bar over its length. Under a cell there is no bar to hold. There is a gel with beads in it, and each bead reports one displacement. So this section asks how strain is read from displacements alone.

Start with something you have already done. In Tab 1 a clutch's own stretch was never a single displacement. It was the difference of two: the actin end of the clutch moved by \(x_i\), the gel end by \(x_s\), and the clutch was stretched by \(x_i - x_s\) (eq. 6.1.4). A piece of gel between two beads is the same. Bead 1 moves by \(\mathbf u_1\), bead 2 by \(\mathbf u_2\), and the piece of gel between them has been stretched by the difference, \(\mathbf u_2 - \mathbf u_1\). Or rather by the part of that difference that lies along the line joining the beads, because a sideways shift of one bead does not make the line any longer; the unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf e}\) along the line picks that part out. A bead that has moved 100 nm by itself tells you nothing: the whole gel could have slid 100 nm, and nothing would be stretched. Only differences between neighbours stretch anything.

That difference is a stretch, §3.1's \(x\), of a piece of gel whose length is the beads' separation, §3.1's \(L\). The strain is the one over the other:

eq. 6.3.2 — strain is a derivative of displacement $$\varepsilon = \frac{(\mathbf u_2 - \mathbf u_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}}{L} \;\;\xrightarrow{\;L\to0\;}\;\; \varepsilon_{xx} = \frac{\partial u_x}{\partial x},\quad \varepsilon_{yy} = \frac{\partial u_y}{\partial y},\quad \gamma_{xy} = \frac{\partial u_x}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_y}{\partial x}$$

The arrow says what happens as the two beads are taken closer and closer together. The strain stops depending on which pair you chose and becomes a property of the point between them: how fast the displacement changes with position. That is all a derivative is here, and the bench uses beads 1 µm apart as the closest pair it has. Two beads along \(x\) read the stretch along \(x\), \(\varepsilon_{xx}\); two along \(y\) read \(\varepsilon_{yy}\). Both are §3.1's \(\varepsilon\), pointed in a direction.

The third strain has no bar to compare with, so take it slowly. Put four beads at the corners of a small square. If the top two move sideways, along \(x\), by more than the bottom two, the square's vertical sides tilt, by that difference over the square's height: \(\partial u_x/\partial y\). If the right-hand two move up, along \(y\), by more than the left-hand two, the horizontal sides tilt as well, by \(\partial u_y/\partial x\). The right angle at each corner has changed by the sum of the two tilts. That change of angle is the shear strain, \(\gamma_{xy}\): the square has leaned without any side getting longer. (Lean a square and one diagonal does get longer while the other gets shorter; §3.3 and §3.4 use that.) The sum \(\varepsilon_{xx} + \varepsilon_{yy}\) is the fractional change in the square's area.

Week 5 §3.2 already showed why strain, not stretch, is the right variable: a filament broke at the same 1.4 % whatever its length, because strength and stiffness both scale with the amount of material and the ratio does not. On a grid of beads the derivatives are difference quotients between neighbours, and the bench computes them on §1.5's cell. Pick a pair of beads with the sliders and read the strain between them; then switch the map to any of the three components and see where the gel is stretched, squeezed and sheared. Under the middle of the cell the area strain is negative: the adhesions pull the gel in toward the centre, and it is squeezed.

  2. Move the pair out to the edge of the field. The displacements are not zero there (eq. 6.1.5's \(1/r\)), but the strain nearly is. Say why, using the words "difference between neighbours".
  3. Switch the map to the area strain. Where is the gel squeezed, and where is it stretched? Then to the shear: where is the shear largest, and why there?

3.3 · Force per area, with a direction — the traction on a cut

§3.1 divided a force by an area once, and it was easy: the bar had one pull along one direction and one cross-section across it. The gel under a cell is not like that. It is pulled sideways at its surface, squeezed under the middle of the cell and stretched beyond its edges, all at once. To divide force by area inside such a body you have to say which area, and which way it faces.

Here is the picture. Tab 2's series rule came from a joint: a massless joint between two elements carries the whole force through it. Now imagine cutting the gel along a line (in the flat picture below) or a plane (in a real gel). The material on the far side of the cut was pulling on the material on the near side, through every spring that crosses the cut: the cut is a whole row of joints. Add up the pulls of all the springs that cross it, and divide by the cut's length (its area, in three dimensions). That force per area is the traction on the cut, \(\mathbf t\). It is a force, so it has a direction. Part of it points straight across the cut, pulling the two sides apart or pushing them together: the normal part, \(t_n\). Part of it runs along the cut, sliding one side past the other: the shear part, \(t_s\). Turn the cut and both parts change, because a different set of springs crosses it.

Two cuts are special: the one whose normal points along \(x\) and the one whose normal points along \(y\). Name what they carry. The \(x\)-cut carries \(\sigma_{xx}\) across itself and \(\sigma_{xy}\) along itself; the \(y\)-cut carries \(\sigma_{yy}\) across itself and \(\sigma_{yx}\) along itself. The two shears are the same number, \(\sigma_{xy} = \sigma_{yx}\), or a small square of gel would spin (the Derive box says why). So three numbers, and those three are the stress. They are enough: the traction on every other cut follows from them by nothing more than the force balance that gave Tab 2 its rules, done on a small triangle. The box does it; the result is

eq. 6.3.3 — the traction on a cut $$\mathbf t = \boldsymbol\sigma\,\mathbf n:\qquad t_x = \sigma_{xx}n_x + \sigma_{xy}n_y,\quad t_y = \sigma_{xy}n_x + \sigma_{yy}n_y,\qquad t_n = \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf n,\quad t_s = \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf s$$

Here \(\mathbf n = (n_x, n_y) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) is the unit vector pointing straight across the cut, at an angle \(\theta\) from the \(x\)-axis, and \(\mathbf s\) is the unit vector along the cut. Read the first line as a recipe: the traction's \(x\)-part is the \(x\)-cut's numbers weighted by how much of the cut faces \(x\), plus the \(y\)-cut's numbers weighted by how much of it faces \(y\).

Derive eq. 6.3.3 from force balance on a small triangle — and why the two shears are one number
  1. Cut out a small right triangle. Its long side is the cut you care about, of length \(\ell\), with unit normal \(\mathbf n = (n_x, n_y)\). Its two short sides lie along the axes: the vertical side faces \(-x\) and has length \(\ell\,n_x\); the horizontal side faces \(-y\) and has length \(\ell\,n_y\). (Check with \(\theta = 0\): \(\mathbf n = (1,0)\), the cut is vertical, the vertical side is the whole \(\ell\) and the horizontal side has no length.)
  2. Write the force on each side. On the cut, the material outside pulls with the traction times the length: \((t_x\ell,\ t_y\ell)\). On the vertical side the material outside is on the \(-x\) side, and it pulls with the opposite of what the \(x\)-cut carries (the joint argument again: what one side exerts on the other is equal and opposite): \(-(\sigma_{xx},\ \sigma_{xy})\,\ell n_x\). On the horizontal side, likewise, \(-(\sigma_{xy},\ \sigma_{yy})\,\ell n_y\).
  3. The triangle has almost no mass, so the forces balance. Its mass goes as its area, \(\ell^2\); the forces on its sides go as \(\ell\). Make it small enough and mass times acceleration is nothing beside the side forces, exactly as for Tab 2's joint. Along \(x\):
    $$t_x\,\ell - \sigma_{xx}\,\ell n_x - \sigma_{xy}\,\ell n_y = 0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t_x = \sigma_{xx}n_x + \sigma_{xy}n_y$$
    and along \(y\), the same moves give \(t_y = \sigma_{xy}n_x + \sigma_{yy}n_y\). Every term had one \(\ell\), so the triangle's size dropped out: the result holds at a point.
  4. Why \(\sigma_{yx} = \sigma_{xy}\). Cut out a small square of side \(a\) instead. The shears on its two \(x\)-faces, \(\sigma_{xy}\), form a pair of forces \(\sigma_{xy}a\) a distance \(a\) apart, which twist the square one way; the shears on its two \(y\)-faces, \(\sigma_{yx}\), twist it the other way. The net twist is \((\sigma_{xy} - \sigma_{yx})\,a^2\). The square's resistance to being spun goes as \(a^4\). As \(a\) shrinks, any difference between the two shears would spin it faster and faster, so they must be equal.
  5. Split the traction into its two parts. Its component straight across the cut is \(t_n = \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf n\), and its component along the cut is \(t_s = \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf s\) with \(\mathbf s = (-n_y,\ n_x)\).
Nothing here used what the gel is made of. The three stresses are just the tractions on two cuts, and force balance hands you every other cut. Stress is not a force and not a vector: it is the rule that gives you a force per area once you say which way you cut.

The bench measures it rather than asserting it. The block is a lattice of springs, a genuine two-dimensional elastic material, which §3.4 will stretch and shear to find its constants. Load it with the two sliders, then cut it along a line at any angle and add up the forces of the springs that cross the cut, per unit length of cut: that is \(\mathbf t\). Rotate the cut and watch \(\mathbf t\) swing while the three stress numbers, read from every spring in the middle of the block, sit still; the dashed curves are eq. 6.3.3 evaluated from those three numbers.

And this is where the cell comes back in. The surface of the gel is a cut, the one whose normal points up out of the gel. A cell pulling sideways on it exerts a traction on that cut, and almost all of it is the shear part: the cell slides the surface, it does not lift it. That force per area on the gel's own surface is what §3.1 renamed "traction", and it is what a bead-seeded gel reports.

  1. Stretch only (\(\gamma = 0\)). Rotate the cut from 0° to 90°. Where is the traction largest, where is it zero, and where is its shear part largest?
  2. Shear only. Find the two cuts on which the traction is purely normal — one pulling apart, one pushing together. Those are the principal directions, and they are at 45° to the shear. §3.4 uses them.
  3. Both at once. Read \(\sigma_{xx}\), \(\sigma_{yy}\), \(\sigma_{xy}\) off the stats, pick a cut, compute eq. 6.3.3 by hand, and compare with what the cut measured.
  4. Check: the normal tractions on any two perpendicular cuts add to \(\sigma_{xx} + \sigma_{yy}\). Try 30° and 120°.

3.4 · Two more numbers a material needs — the narrowing ν, the shear modulus G, and how the three are tied

A spring needs one number. §3.1 gave a material one number too, \(E\), for one kind of loading: a pull along a bar. But §3.2 found three strains in a plane and §3.3 found three stresses, and one number cannot connect three to three. Two things happen to a material that never happen to a spring, and each needs a number of its own.

It narrows. Stretch a block along \(x\) and its sides move in: it gets thinner as it gets longer, though nothing pulled it sideways. The narrowing comes with the stretch, in a fixed proportion for a given material: a strain of \(\varepsilon_{xx}\) along the pull comes with a strain of \(-\nu\,\varepsilon_{xx}\) across it, and \(\nu\) is Poisson's ratio, the letter §1.5's Derive box borrowed. For a gel the reason is water. Cells, tissues and the gels they are grown on are mostly water, and water barely compresses, so a stretched gel has to get thinner by exactly enough to keep its volume. That fixes \(\nu\). A block of sides \(L\), \(W\) and \(H\), stretched by \(\varepsilon\) along \(L\) and narrowed by \(\nu\varepsilon\) along each of \(W\) and \(H\), has volume

$$L(1+\varepsilon)\cdot W(1-\nu\varepsilon)\cdot H(1-\nu\varepsilon)\;\approx\;LWH\,(1 + \varepsilon - 2\nu\varepsilon)\qquad\text{(small strains)}$$

For the volume not to change, \(\varepsilon - 2\nu\varepsilon = 0\), so \(\nu = \tfrac12\). A material that cannot change its volume is called incompressible, and for biology, \(\nu = \tfrac12\). (Polyacrylamide measures 0.48; this module uses ½ throughout.) A material with nothing to keep its volume fixed narrows less. The spring lattice on the bench is one: it is not full of water, its area is free to change, and it narrows by only a third of its stretch, \(\nu = \tfrac13\).

It can be sheared. There is a second way to load a block: hold its bottom, and push its top sideways. Nothing gets longer at first sight; the block leans. That lean is §3.2's shear strain \(\gamma\), the change in the corner angle, and the stress that goes with it is §3.3's \(\sigma_{xy}\), the force per area along the cut. They are proportional too, with a slope of their own: \(\sigma_{xy} = G\gamma\), and \(G\) is the shear modulus. This is the spring law for a third time. \(F = kx\) for a bar became \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\); for a lean it is \(\sigma_{xy} = G\gamma\).

The three are tied. A lean is not really a new kind of deformation. §3.2 noticed that a leaning square has one diagonal longer and the other shorter, and §3.3's second task found the same thing on the cut: a pure shear pulls the 45° cut straight apart and pushes the 135° cut straight together. So a shear is a stretch along one diagonal and a squeeze along the other, and \(G\) has to be whatever \(E\) and \(\nu\) say it is. The Derive box gets it in four moves:

eq. 6.3.4 — stress–strain equalities, and a lattice of springs as a material $$\sigma_{xx} = E\,\varepsilon_{xx},\qquad \varepsilon_{yy} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{xx},\qquad \sigma_{xy} = G\,\gamma_{xy},\qquad G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} \qquad\Big(\text{triangular lattice: } E = \tfrac{2k}{\sqrt3},\ \nu = \tfrac13,\ G = \tfrac{\sqrt3 k}{4}\Big)$$
Derive \(G = E/2(1+\nu)\) — a shear is a pull along one diagonal and a push along the other
  1. What a pure shear does to the two diagonals, in stress. Let the only stress be a shear, \(\sigma_{xy} = s\), with \(\sigma_{xx} = \sigma_{yy} = 0\). Put the 45° cut into eq. 6.3.3, \(\mathbf n = (1, 1)/\sqrt2\):
    $$t_x = s\,n_y = \frac{s}{\sqrt2},\quad t_y = s\,n_x = \frac{s}{\sqrt2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t_n = \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf n = s,\quad t_s = 0$$
    The 45° cut is pulled straight apart by \(s\), with nothing along it. The 135° cut, \(\mathbf n = (-1, 1)/\sqrt2\), gives \(t_n = -s\): pushed straight together. So along one diagonal the gel is under a pull \(s\), and along the other a push \(s\).
  2. What the same shear does to the two diagonals, in strain. A lean by \(\gamma\) moves every point sideways in proportion to its height: \(u_x = \gamma\,y\), \(u_y = 0\). Take two points a distance \(L\) apart along the 45° diagonal. They are \(L/\sqrt2\) apart in \(y\), so their displacements differ by \(\gamma L/\sqrt2\), all of it along \(x\). Eq. 6.3.2 projects that onto the diagonal (another factor \(1/\sqrt2\)) and divides by \(L\):
    $$\varepsilon_{45^\circ} = \frac{(\gamma L/\sqrt2)\cdot(1/\sqrt2)}{L} = \frac{\gamma}{2},\qquad \varepsilon_{135^\circ} = -\frac{\gamma}{2}$$
  3. Hooke along the diagonal, with the narrowing. The pull \(s\) along the 45° diagonal stretches it by \(s/E\) (§3.1). The push \(s\) along the other diagonal, on its own, would make the 45° diagonal wider by \(\nu s/E\): that is the narrowing rule run backwards, since a squeeze along one direction widens the directions across it. The material is linear, so the two effects add:
    $$\varepsilon_{45^\circ} = \frac{s}{E} + \frac{\nu s}{E} = \frac{(1+\nu)\,s}{E}$$
  4. Equate the two, and read off \(G\). Steps 2 and 3 describe the same diagonal:
    $$\frac{\gamma}{2} = \frac{(1+\nu)\,s}{E}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad s = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}\,\gamma\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}$$
Check it on the lattice, whose constants are known exactly: \(E = 2k/\sqrt3\) and \(\nu = \tfrac13\) give \(G = (2k/\sqrt3)/(2\cdot\tfrac43) = \sqrt3k/4\), which is what shearing it measures. For a gel, \(\nu = \tfrac12\) gives \(G = E/3\): a 1 kPa gel has a shear modulus of 333 Pa, the number §3.5 needs.

The bench lets you check the tie on a material whose constants are known exactly. A triangular lattice of identical springs is isotropic, the same in every direction, with \(E = 2k/\sqrt3\), \(\nu = \tfrac13\) and \(G = \sqrt3k/4\). Stretch it and measure \(E\) from the stress and \(\nu\) from the narrowing; shear it and measure \(G\); then check the tie with the three numbers you measured, not the three the formula predicts. The block settles in front of you, the solver relaxing it a few steps per frame, and the stress is read the way §3.3 read it, from the springs in the middle. Nothing is read off the formula.

What \(\nu = \tfrac12\) does to every formula that carries it. It makes them simpler. The tie gives \(G = E/3\). The half-space's answer to a point pull in §1.5's Derive box loses its bracket and becomes \(u_x = 3F(1+\cos^2\theta)/(4\pi E r)\). And the ground's spring, which for a general material is \(\pi E a/\big((1+\nu)(2-\nu)\big)\), becomes eq. 6.3.1's \(\tfrac49\pi E a\): that is where the \(\tfrac49\) came from.

  1. Stretch the block by 1 %. Wait for it to settle and read \(\sigma_{xx}\), \(E = \sigma_{xx}/\varepsilon\), and the narrowing. Compare \(E\) and \(\nu\) with the lattice's values.
  2. Stretch by 2 %. \(E\) does not change; the stress does. Change the spring constant instead: now \(E\) changes and \(\nu\) does not. Say which is geometry and which is material.
  3. Shear by 1 % and read \(G\). Compute \(E/2(1+\nu)\) from your measured \(E\) and \(\nu\); how close is it to your measured \(G\)?
  4. Watch the stretched block's sides. A gel would narrow more (\(\nu = \tfrac12\)). Which tie would it obey, and what would its \(G\) be at 1 kPa?

3.5 · Everything back to tractions — and why bother

Now the chain closes. A bead-seeded gel reports displacements; strain is their difference over a length (eq. 6.3.2); stress is the material law applied to the strain (eq. 6.3.4); and the traction the cell exerts is the stress on the cut that is the gel's own surface (eq. 6.3.3). How much of that chain you have to run depends on one thing: how thick the gel is compared with the adhesion pulling on it.

A thin film is a bar loaded sideways, and every step of §3.1 works for it. Take a patch of film of area \(A\) and thickness \(h\), glued to a rigid floor, and push its top sideways by \(u\). The patch leans by \(\gamma = u/h\): §3.2's shear strain, with the film's thickness as the length being deformed. It answers with a shear stress \(\sigma_{xy} = G\gamma\) (§3.4). And the force per area on its top surface, a cut facing up, is that shear stress (§3.3): the traction the cell put there. Multiply by \(A\) and it is a spring again, \(F = (GA/h)\,u\), which is §3.1's \(k = EA/L\) with \(G\) in place of \(E\) and \(h\) in place of \(L\). Divide the spring by its area and what is left is

eq. 6.3.5 — a thin gel reads locally $$\gamma = \frac{u}{h},\qquad \sigma_{xy} = G\gamma,\qquad t = \frac{G\,u}{h}$$

Each bead reports the traction directly above it, with no inversion at all; a localisation error of \(\delta u\) becomes an error of \(G\,\delta u/h\) and nothing worse. A thick gel, a half-space, spreads every pull over its neighbours by eq. 6.1.5, so each bead reports a sum (eq. 6.1.6) and the map has to be inverted, which is the ill-posed problem §1.5 met. Merkel and colleagues solved the layer of finite thickness in between and showed that films of 5–10 µm improve the resolution of the method for exactly this reason. Whichever gel you use, one check is free: a cell adhering to nothing but the gel exerts no net force and no net moment on it, so the recovered tractions must sum to zero,

eq. 6.3.6 — a free cell balances $$\sum_k \mathbf t_k\,\Delta A_k = \mathbf 0,\qquad \sum_k \mathbf x_k\times\mathbf t_k\,\Delta A_k = 0$$

— a residual that flags a bad reference image, a drifted stage, or an over-smoothed inverse. It is Tab 2's joint argument one more time: the cell is the massless joint, and the tractions are everything that pulls on it. The bench runs the same cell on both gels.

So why stress and strain, instead of force and displacement? Four reasons, and you have now met each of them. They belong to the material, not to the piece: \(E\), \(\nu\) and \(G\) describe collagen, while a force–displacement slope describes one particular lump of it, cut one particular way (§3.1 and §3.4). The cell's force is distributed, and a gel can only report a force per area: the adhesion is not a point, and the beads under it see a traction, not a force (§3.1, §3.3). The laws are local: stress and strain are defined at every point, which is what lets you write the material law once and solve for an unknown distributed load at all; the half-space answer behind §1.5's reach is that local law, added up (§3.2, §3.3). And they make cells and substrates comparable across sizes and labs: a traction of 1 kPa means the same thing under a fibroblast in Boston and a keratocyte in Barcelona, on a 10 µm gel or a 100 µm one; a displacement of 200 nm does not.

  1. Thin gel, no noise. The map is the cell's adhesions exactly. Add 20 nm of noise: the error is uniform speckle and no worse. Read the factor \(G/h\) that turns nanometres into pascals.
  2. Thick gel, no noise, then 20 nm. This is §1.5 again. Compare the two maps at the same noise and say which gel a careful experimenter would pour.
  3. Thin the film by half. The beads move twice as far for the same traction; the noise does not. What happened to the map?
  4. Read the net force on each map. Then push \(\lambda\) on the thick gel until the peaks shrink and check whether the net force noticed.

5.1 · Next week's headline, measured today — the cell itself gives

Everything this week deformed the ground. The cell was a shape that pulled. But the dermis is not a flat gel; it is a three-dimensional network of collagen with pores between the fibres, and to get to the bite the neutrophil has to squeeze itself through them — and, before that, through the wall of the vessel it left. The stiffest thing in it is the nucleus, several microns across, and a pore narrower than that is a problem eq. 6.2.5 already knows how to pose: the nucleus must be deformed by the difference \(\Delta = D_{nucleus} - D_{pore}\), and it deforms the way a standard linear solid creeps.

eq. 6.5.1 — the nucleus through a pore $$x(t) = F_0\left[\frac{1}{k_\infty} - \left(\frac{1}{k_\infty} - \frac{1}{k_0}\right)e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\right] = \Delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{pass} = -\tau_\varepsilon\,\ln\frac{F_0/k_\infty - \Delta}{F_0/k_\infty - F_0/k_0}$$

— instant if the instantaneous squeeze \(F_0/k_0\) already covers \(\Delta\); never if even the equilibrium squeeze \(F_0/k_\infty\) does not; and, in between, a wait, set by the creep time \(\tau_\varepsilon\), that grows without bound as the pore closes toward the limit. The bench pushes a nucleus into a channel with a set force and times it. The numbers for the nucleus are illustrative — k∞ = 0.6 and k0 = 3 pN/nm with τ = 5 s, chosen so that a nanonewton of push deforms it by microns — and next week replaces them with measured ones.

Derive eq. 6.5.1 — from the standard linear solid's creep to the time through the pore
  1. The law. The nucleus is Tab 2 §2.6's standard linear solid under a held push \(F_0\), and §2.6's Derive box solved that case with its boundary condition \(x(0^+) = F_0/k_0\):
    $$x(t) = \frac{F_0}{k_\infty} - \left(\frac{F_0}{k_\infty} - \frac{F_0}{k_0}\right)e^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon},\qquad \tau_\varepsilon = \tau\frac{k_0}{k_\infty}$$
  2. The condition. The nucleus is wider than the pore by \(\Delta\), so it passes at the moment its squeeze reaches \(\Delta\): \(x(t_{pass}) = \Delta\).
  3. Solve for the time. Put \(x = \Delta\) in and move the terms without the exponential to the left:
    $$\left(\frac{F_0}{k_\infty} - \frac{F_0}{k_0}\right)e^{-t_{pass}/\tau_\varepsilon} = \frac{F_0}{k_\infty} - \Delta$$
    Divide both sides by the bracket:
    $$e^{-t_{pass}/\tau_\varepsilon} = \frac{F_0/k_\infty - \Delta}{F_0/k_\infty - F_0/k_0}$$
    Take the logarithm of both sides, then multiply by \(-\tau_\varepsilon\):
    $$-\frac{t_{pass}}{\tau_\varepsilon} = \ln\frac{F_0/k_\infty - \Delta}{F_0/k_\infty - F_0/k_0}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t_{pass} = -\tau_\varepsilon\,\ln\frac{F_0/k_\infty - \Delta}{F_0/k_\infty - F_0/k_0}$$
  4. Read it. The ratio inside the logarithm is between 0 and 1 when \(F_0/k_0 < \Delta < F_0/k_\infty\): then the logarithm is negative and \(t_{pass}\) is a positive wait, set by the creep clock \(\tau_\varepsilon\). If \(\Delta \le F_0/k_0\) the ratio is 1 or more, the logarithm is zero or positive, and the nucleus is through at once: the instant squeeze already covers the gap. If \(\Delta \ge F_0/k_\infty\) the ratio is zero or negative, which has no logarithm: it never passes. As \(\Delta\) approaches \(F_0/k_\infty\) from below the ratio approaches zero and the wait grows without bound.
One equation, one boundary condition, one logarithm: the three regimes on the bench are the three signs the ratio can take.
  1. Leave the push at 2 nN and open the pore until the nucleus goes through at once. Read \(F_0/k_0\) and compare it with \(\Delta\).
  2. Close the pore until it never goes through. Read \(F_0/k_\infty\).
  3. Between the two, read the transit time and the creep time \(\tau_\varepsilon\). Halve the pore's width from there and watch the time blow up.
  4. Double the push. Which of the two limits moved, and which way did the transit time go?
