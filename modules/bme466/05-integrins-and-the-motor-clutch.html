1.1 · A filament cannot push. So how does it push?

Last week you built a cell that had everything it needed to move and did not move. It had a filament that grows at one end and shrinks at the other. It had motors that walk and pull. It had a compass that could tell which way the bite was. Week 4 ended by writing the edge speed down as a difference — \(v_{edge}=v_{pol}-v_{retro}\) — and then quietly setting the second term to zero, because it had no way to talk about it yet.

Start with something that should not be possible.

Tsai and colleagues watched a neutrophil-like cell crawl under agarose, with its actin and myosin fluorescently speckled. The cell moves at 5–10 µm per minute. Inside it, the actin network is streaming backwards relative to the cell at exactly the speed the cell is going forwards.

Now put those two together in the frame of the coverslip, which is the frame that matters. The speckles do not move at all. In their words, the puncta "appeared to be essentially static relative to the coverslip substrate." The actin is nailed to the ground, and the cell is flowing forwards over its own stationary cytoskeleton.

Nothing in Week 4 can produce that. A network that is being hauled backwards by motors should be visibly moving backwards. Something is holding it against the floor hard enough to win completely — and whatever that is, it is this week's subject. By §4.3 you will compute this cell's speed from Pollard's 1986 rate constants and find out how close you get.

(One honest caveat to carry: the speckles are static until the large contracting myosin clusters at the rear sweep them forward. "Static" describes the leading edge, not the whole cell.)

The word in the title. A clutch, in a car, is the thing that decides how much of a running engine reaches the wheels. Engage it and the engine's power goes into moving you; disengage it and the engine spins just as hard while you go nowhere, all the work lost to slipping. It is not a motor and it makes no power of its own — it is a connection, and its only job is to choose how much force gets transmitted.

That is exactly what an integrin-based adhesion does between the actin network and the ground, so the whole picture is called the motor–clutch model: myosin is the engine, the adhesion is the clutch, the matrix is the road. Keep the analogy in mind — the module uses "clutch", "anchor" and "linkage" for the same object, and the reason it earns a car part's name is that it slips.

Start with the first term, and with a problem that should bother you. A growing filament is a chain of subunits. It does not have a piston, or a hand, or anything that could shove. So when we say actin polymerization pushes the membrane forward, what is actually doing the pushing?

The answer is that nothing pushes. The membrane is jittering — it is a warm, floppy sheet, and warm floppy things move. Every so often that jitter opens a gap between the sheet and the filament tip. If the gap happens to be wider than one subunit, a monomer can slot in. And then the sheet cannot come back, because there is something in the way. Do that ten thousand times and you have a protrusion.

That is a ratchet: random motion, plus a catch that only lets it go one way. The panel below counts insertions as they happen, so the speed on screen is measured rather than asserted. Watch what a load does to it.

One thing to settle before you touch the slider: why is there a load at all? The sheet is not free. It is a closed bag under tension, the way a balloon's skin is — push a bump out anywhere and the whole surface has to stretch and be pulled in from elsewhere to supply it, and it resists. That resistance is membrane tension, and it is what the growing tips are pushing against. We are not going to derive it. It is a genuine piece of membrane mechanics, it belongs to a different lecture, and this course has not earned it — so it arrives here as a slider with a number attached and §4.4 lists it among the things this model cannot say for itself.

But we can bracket it, which is the next best thing and worth more than a confident number would be:

How hard does the membrane actually push back on one filament?

Tension is measured in force per unit length of edge, not force per filament, so getting from one to the other needs a count: \(\;f_{filament} = T / D\), where \(D\) is how many barbed ends are pushing per µm of leading edge. Both sides are measurable and both have been measured.

Tension \(T\): in neutrophil-like cells — our cell — Diz-Muñoz and colleagues pulled membrane tethers and got 64–69 pN/µm at rest. Treating the cells with latrunculin dropped it to 6 pN/µm, which tells you the tension is actin's doing rather than the lipid's. In fast-moving keratocytes it is higher: Lieber and colleagues measured 365 ± 26 pN/µm at the front against 280 at the rear.

Density \(D\): Week 4's cast has 241 ± 100 barbed ends per µm of margin.

Run those together and you get roughly 0.3 pN per filament for a neutrophil and 1.5 pN for a keratocyte — the right order, and a reminder that it is not one universal number.

There is a second, completely independent bracket. Footer and colleagues put a single actin filament against an optical trap and measured it stall at 0.76 ± 0.22 pN in vitro at 2 µM actin; the same paper works out that the most a single filament could ever push in a cell, at the most generous monomer supply, is about 9 pN. So any per-filament load we choose has to live between about 0.8 and 9 pN to be physical at all.

The module's default of 2 pN sits inside both brackets. That is the entire justification, and you should hold it loosely — it is a bracketed choice, not a measurement, and §4.3 shows you exactly how much the final answer moves when you change it.

  1. Set the load to zero and note the growth speed. Now walk the load up in steps of 1 pN and watch the red dot track down the grey curve.
  2. Find the load where growth stops. Then raise the G-actin concentration and find it again.
  3. Watch the "gap ≥ δ" readout as you raise the load. That single number is doing all the work.
  4. Drop the clock speed to ×0.01 and watch individual monomers arrive. Count how many bounce off for every one that gets in, then check that fraction against the "gap ≥ δ" readout — they are the same number.
  5. Now cool it to 5 °C. The sheet stops thrashing, gaps of a full δ open less often, and more monomers are turned away. Nothing about the chemistry changed — see the note below.

The chemistry has not changed. \(k_{on}\) and \(k_{off}\) are the same two numbers Week 4 measured. Monomers keep turning up at the tip at exactly the same rate whatever the load — the panel shows that, and it is worth watching, because it is the thing people get wrong. All the load does is decide how often a gap of width \(\delta\) is open when one arrives, and that is a Boltzmann question — you already know how to answer it.

What the temperature slider does, and what it deliberately does not.

It moves \(k_BT\), and nothing else. Cooler means the sheet jitters less, so a gap of a full \(\delta\) opens less often, so more arriving monomers are turned away — and the stall force drops, because the filament has less thermal energy to spend on pushing the load back.

It does not touch \(k_{on}\) or \(k_{off}\). Real rate constants are temperature-dependent, usually steeply so. But Pollard measured his at 22 °C and nobody has re-measured them across a range, so scaling them with some assumed activation energy would be inventing data. Holding them fixed is the honest option, and it has a useful side effect: what you are looking at is the pure Boltzmann part of the ratchet, isolated from the chemistry. That is exactly the factor eq. 4.5.1 carries, and nothing else in the panel is competing with it.

So read the temperature slider as a thought experiment about the gap statistics, not as a prediction of what a cell does at 5 °C. A real cold cell would also have slower chemistry, and this panel does not claim to know by how much.

eq. 5.1.1 — the gap probability $$P(\text{gap} \ge \delta) = e^{-F\delta/k_BT}$$

This is eq. 1.2.7 again — Week 1's Boltzmann distribution — with the energy being the work \(F\delta\) you have to do against the load to open the gap. Put it in front of the on-rate and you have the whole law.

eq. 4.5.1 again — previewed in Week 4, earned here $$v = \delta\!\left(k_{on}\,C\,e^{-F\delta/k_BT} - k_{off}\right)$$
Derive: why the load only touches the on-rate
  1. Write the unloaded law. A tip gains subunits at \(k_{on}C\) and loses them at \(k_{off}\), each step moving it by \(\delta\).
    \(v_0 = \delta(k_{on}C - k_{off})\) — this is eq. 4.1.1
  2. Ask what the load forbids. Adding a subunit needs room: the sheet must be at least \(\delta\) away. Removing one needs no room at all.
    on-pathway: gated. off-pathway: ungated.
  3. Price the room. Opening a gap \(\delta\) against \(F\) costs work \(F\delta\), and the sheet is in thermal equilibrium, so Boltzmann gives the odds.
    \(P = e^{-F\delta/k_BT}\)
  4. Gate the on-rate and nothing else.
    \(v = \delta(k_{on}C\,P - k_{off}) = \delta(k_{on}Ce^{-F\delta/k_BT} - k_{off})\)
The load appears in an exponent, not as a subtraction. That is the signature of a ratchet: force does not oppose the motion, it changes the odds of the step.
This is the rigid ratchet: the filament is stiff and only the sheet fluctuates. Mogilner & Oster's correction is that the filaments' own bending is the bigger effect in a real lamellipodium — the elastic Brownian ratchet. The shape of the law survives; the microscopic story of what is fluctuating does not.
Nothing pushes. A ratchet does not add energy — the thermal bath already had it. All the filament does is stop the sheet from coming back.

1.2 · The ceiling, and who sets it

Set \(v=0\) in eq. 4.5.1 and you get the load at which the tip gives up. It is worth staring at, because of what is not in it.

eq. 4.5.1 again — solved for the stall $$F^* = \frac{k_BT}{\delta}\,\ln\!\frac{C}{C_c}, \qquad C_c = \frac{k_{off}}{k_{on}}$$

No motor. No ATP term. No mention of how strong anything is. The ceiling on what a growing filament can push against is set by a concentration ratio and the temperature — and it enters as a logarithm, which is about as weak a lever as nature offers.

A mismatch worth owning, since the temperature is right there in the equation. This module runs \(k_BT = 4.28\) pN·nm, the value at 37 °C, because that is the temperature of the cell we are modelling. But \(k_{on}\) and \(k_{off}\) come from Pollard's 1986 bench, and his Methods say plainly: "The polymerization reaction was carried out at room temperature (22 °C)." At 22 °C, \(k_BT\) is 4.08 pN·nm. So the module is mixing a 37 °C thermal energy with 22 °C chemistry, and there is no fully consistent option available — nobody has re-measured those rate constants at 37 °C. Rather than hide it: running the whole module at 4.08 instead lowers the stall force below from 7.00 to 6.65 pN (−5 %) and the capstone's answer from 5.29 to 5.01 µm/min, which is still inside the measured band. The inconsistency is real and it is small. Knowing which of those two things is true is the point of checking.
  1. Read the stall force at 10 µM. Now double the concentration to 20 µM. How much did the ceiling rise?
  2. Use the right-hand panel: pick a load, and read off the concentration the tip would need just to hold station.
  3. Drop the concentration below \(C_c\) and watch what happens to the whole idea of a stall force.

eq. 5.1.2 — the loaded critical concentration $$C^*(F) = C_c\,e^{F\delta/k_BT}$$
Worked: how hard can one filament push?
  1. Write what you know. Week 4's verified barbed-end numbers, and a leading-edge monomer pool.
    \(k_{on}=11.6\;\mu\text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1},\; k_{off}=1.4\;\text{s}^{-1},\; \delta=2.7\) nm, \(k_BT=4.28\) pN·nm, \(C=10\) µM
  2. Get the critical concentration.
    \(C_c = k_{off}/k_{on} = 1.4/11.6 = 0.121\) µM
  3. Assemble the stall force symbolically.
    \(F^* = (k_BT/\delta)\ln(C/C_c)\)
  4. Substitute.
    \(F^* = (4.28/2.7)\,\ln(10/0.121) = 1.585 \times 4.42\)
\(F^* \approx 7\) pN per filament. A lamellipodium runs a few hundred filaments per micron of edge, so the edge as a whole can push on the order of a nanonewton per micron.

Where this number comes from. It is derived here from Pollard's rate constants, which Week 4 checked against the paper's own table. It is not a value quoted from Peskin or Mogilner & Oster — both of those papers survive only as scanned images, so nothing in them was machine-readable and no per-filament force was taken from either. Cite them for the mechanism, not for the number.

One geometric correction before this term ever gets compared with anything, because §1.3 is about to put it up against the motors and the comparison has to be fair. Every filament so far has been drawn head-on. In a real lamellipodium they are not.

eq. 5.1.3 — the step the membrane actually sees $$\delta_{eff} = \delta\cos\theta, \qquad \theta \approx 35^\circ \;\Rightarrow\; v_{pol} = \delta_{eff}\left(k_{on}Ce^{-F\delta_{eff}/k_BT} - k_{off}\right)$$

Why \(\cos\theta\) appears twice — and why that matters

  1. The geometry. Arp2/3 branches at 70°, so lamellipodial filaments run at about \(\pm35^\circ\) to the membrane normal. A monomer still lengthens its filament by the full \(\delta\) along the filament's own axis.
  2. The advance. The membrane only moves by the component along its normal, \(\delta\cos\theta\). So the edge gains less per monomer than a head-on filament would.
  3. The work. But the load is pushed back by that same shorter distance, so the work per insertion is \(F\,\delta\cos\theta\), not \(F\delta\). The Boltzmann penalty shrinks too.
  4. Both, or neither. Replace \(\delta\) by \(\delta_{eff}\) everywhere it appears. A shorter step is partly paid for by a cheaper step.
At 2 pN this gives 88 nm/s. Multiplying the whole velocity by \(\cos\theta\) instead — putting the factor in one place only — gives 70 nm/s and double-counts the penalty by 18 %. Week 4's eq. 4.4.3 wrote it that way; this is the correction, and §4.3 depends on it.
Note from Dr. McGhee. The logarithm is the part to carry out of this section. Doubling the monomer pool buys you \(({k_BT}/{\delta})\ln 2 \approx 1.1\) pN, and doubling it again buys another 1.1 pN. A cell cannot concentrate its way out of a real mechanical load. If it needs more force, it needs a different mechanism — which is the rest of this module.
The ceiling is chemical, not mechanical. Concentration and temperature set how hard a filament can push. That is why the motors are about to matter so much: they are not bound by this.

1.3 · Meanwhile, the motors are pulling the other way

Here is the term Week 4 set to zero. The same myosin you met last week is not only contracting the cell body — it is hauling the entire lamellipodial actin array rearward, away from the edge. This is retrograde flow, and it is one of the most reliably observed things in cell biology: mark the actin network and the marks travel backwards, into the cell, while the cell itself goes forwards.

So the tip is growing forwards and the ground under the tip is sliding backwards. The panel below is drawn the way this is actually measured — as speckle microscopy, with a fixed lab-frame ruler, the membrane edge as one marker, and marked actin subunits as another.

Start on the chip marked motors off. Then turn them on with nothing anchored, and watch carefully: the tip is still growing at exactly the same speed.

  1. Run motors off and note the edge speed. Now switch to motors on, nothing anchored.
  2. Check the polymerization readout. Did it change? It did not — the tip is growing at the same speed it always was. Now watch where the membrane ends up, and read "membrane has moved". It goes backwards. The network is sliding rearward faster than the tip can build forward, so the edge is carried back with it.
  3. Try to rescue the cell by lowering the membrane load — that is, by making the tips grow as fast as they possibly can. You can — the verdict will tell you the load where it happens. Write that number down, then ask yourself whether a real membrane pushes back harder or softer than that.
  4. Put the load back to 2 pN and switch the anchors on. Watch the tethers lean over as the network drags them — that lean is the integrin resisting. Only one of the three velocity readouts changes: find it. Then note that the membrane starts moving forward again, with no extra growth whatsoever.

eq. 4.4.3 again — both terms alive this time $$v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_{retro}$$

Week 4 minted this with \(v_{retro}\) set to zero and said so. Now it is not zero. §1.2 finished the first term; everything from here to the end of this section is about the second one, and about the single question the section exists to answer: what sets \(v_{retro}\)?

Week 4 already wrote the law. A myosin team does not run at one speed; it runs slower the harder it is pulling, linearly, from its unloaded speed \(v_0\) down to a dead stop at the team's stall force. Nothing about that changes when the thing being dragged is a whole actin network instead of a bead.

eq. 4.2.4 again — the load line, now dragging a network $$v = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F}{F_{stall}}\right), \qquad F_{stall} = N_m F_m \;\;\text{(eq. 4.2.6)}$$

So there is really only one unknown left, and it is not a property of myosin at all: what is \(F\)? What, exactly, are these motors pulling against? Answer that and \(v_{retro}\) follows immediately. It is worth doing the two cases in order, because the first one is the one that surprises people.

Case A — nothing is anchored

  1. List what touches the network. The motors, pulling it rearward. The membrane at the front, which the tips are pushing on — and which therefore pushes back on the tips, rearward. That is the whole inventory.
  2. Notice that both of them point the same way. The membrane is not resisting the motors; it is pushing the network in the same direction the motors are dragging it. There is nothing in the picture pulling forward.
  3. So the motors work against nothing:
    \(F = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; v_{retro} = v_0\)
  4. And read what that means on the load line. At \(v = v_0\) the motor force is zero. The network slides backwards as fast as myosin can possibly move it, and myosin is exerting no force while it does so.
A free network runs at the maximum retrograde speed and the motors do no work at all. Both halves of that are worth sitting with: it is the fastest the actin ever slides, and it is the least useful the motors ever are. Switch the panel to motors on, nothing anchored and read the motor force — it is zero, and now you knew it would be.

Which tells us what the anchor is for. Not to push the cell forward — nothing here pushes — but to give the motors something to pull against. So draw the free body, because §1.3 is usually taught as a two-sided tug-of-war and it is really a three-sided one. Take a single filament and ask what pushes and pulls on it. Forward is the way the cell is going.

  • The membrane pushes the tip rearward. This is the one people miss. The tip pushes the sheet forward; by Newton's third law the sheet pushes the tip back, with exactly the load \(F_{mem}\) that §1.1's ratchet was fighting. The ratchet is not only a source of advance — it is a source of rearward force on the filament.
  • The motors drag the network rearward, and this filament carries its share of whatever the adhesion module is resisting.
  • The integrin holds it forward — and it is the only thing that does.
eq. 5.1.4 — the force triangle, on one filament $$f_{anchor} = \underbrace{F_{mem}}_{\text{the sheet pushing back}} \;+\; \underbrace{F_{FA}/N_{fil}}_{\text{this filament's share of the motors}}$$

Read what the triangle says. The anchor has to carry both. Not the motors alone — the motors plus the very load the ratchet is working against. Push the membrane harder and you do two things at once: you slow the ratchet (eq. 4.5.1) and you add to what the anchor must hold. Remove the anchor and nothing balances either term, so the filament slides rearward. That is the triangle failing to close, and it is what the panel draws.

And one piece of bookkeeping, before the symbol turns up in an equation. One integrin is a small thing: it holds a few pN before it lets go, and §1.5 will pin that number down. A lamellipodium does not anchor itself on one. It anchors on a patch of them — \(N_{int}\) integrins side by side, each carrying its own \(f_{int}\), all bolted to the same piece of matrix and all pulling on the same network. That patch is what cell biologists call a focal adhesion, and the force it transmits is just the sum:

the focal adhesion, defined $$F_{FA} = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{int}} f_{int,i} = N_{int}\,\bar{f}_{int}$$

That is all the word means for now — a number of integrins and a force each. Tab 2 puts a patch like this under one piece of leading edge and asks what it does to the flow directly above it. Here we only need the total.

Case B — anchored, and now the load line has a load

  1. Take the load the motors feel from the free body. The anchorage is the only forward force in the picture, so the force resisting the motors is exactly what the focal adhesion transmits into the ground:
    \(F = F_{FA}\)
  2. Put it into the load line (eq. 4.2.4, above), with the team's stall force \(F_{stall}=N_mF_m\):
    \(v_{retro} = v_0\left(1 - F_{FA}/(N_m F_m)\right)\)
  3. Check the two ends. \(F_{FA}=0\) recovers Case A, \(v_{retro}=v_0\). \(F_{FA}=N_mF_m\) stalls the motors outright and the flow stops dead.
There it is — the integrin slows retrograde flow by giving the motors something to pull against, and the load line converts that resistance into lost speed at a fixed exchange rate. Nothing was added to the motors and nothing was added to the growth; a force was simply given somewhere else to go.
eq. 5.1.5 — the retrograde speed, with the adhesion carrying the load $$v_{retro} = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_m F_m}\right)$$

Every pN the adhesion takes is a pN the motors no longer spend on sliding actin rearward. And note what \(F_{FA}\) is not: it is not everything the anchor holds. By eq. 5.1.4 the anchor's own burden also includes the membrane term, which is why the integrin's job is harder than this equation alone suggests. \(F_{FA}\) is the part that comes off the motors.

Now the comparison is fair, and the consequence is stark. Under a plausible couple of pN of membrane load the tips manage well under \(v_0 \approx 120\) nm/s, so with a free network the motors win and the cell runs on the spot.

More growth is almost never the fix — and the exception is worth finding. The panel will let you drop the membrane load until the ratchet out-runs the motors unaided; the verdict will tell you the exact load where that happens. Notice how low it is. Above it — which is where any real lamellipodium lives — raising \(v_{pol}\) cannot save you, because it is bounded by its own unloaded ceiling while the motors are not. Something has to happen to the other term.

1.4 · The integrin is a force balance

Bolt the network to the ground. Not with anything clever — just a protein that spans the membrane, grips a molecule of matrix on the outside, and holds actin on the inside. That is an integrin, and this is the section the whole week turns on.

When the network is anchored, the motors are still pulling exactly as hard as before. What changes is where that pull goes. Force that used to slide actin rearward now runs down through the linkage and into the matrix, and the matrix — rigid, immovable — simply absorbs it. The motors are working against the ground instead of against the network, so the network stops sliding.

§1.3 already did the algebra, so nothing here is new machinery — this section is about reading what it means:

eq. 5.1.5 again — the retrograde speed, derived in §1.3 $$v_{retro} = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_m F_m}\right)$$

And here is where Week 4's parting question gets its answer. Week 4 ended by pointing at Lämmermann's result — leukocytes engineered with no functional integrins at all still crawl perfectly well through three-dimensional tissue — and naming it as this week's opening problem. If integrins were what made cells move, that result would be impossible.

Read the equation again and you can see why it is not. Nothing in it says the integrin generates anything. \(F_{FA}\) is a force the adhesion takes off the motors — \(N_{int}\) integrins' worth of it, by §1.3's definition — and all it does is divert that force away from sliding the network. Any mechanism that can absorb that force does the same job: on a flat dish it has to be a molecular bolt through the membrane, but inside a 3-D mesh the geometry itself can do it — Lämmermann's cells push off the walls around them by sheer network expansion. So the honest statement, and the line this module exists to earn, is: integrins are not what makes a cell move. They are what lets a surface push back. On a coverslip there is nothing else that will.

  1. Leave the adhesion at 0 pN and press measure. The bench times one marked subunit and the membrane for two seconds and reads two speeds off the ruler — how far the mark slid back, how far the edge went. A dot lands on each line of the chart. Raise the adhesion force in steps of about 40 pN and measure again each time. The dots draw two straight lines. Only once you have two dots does eq. 5.1.5 appear, dashed, underneath them.
  2. Find where the pink dots cross zero — that is the cell starting to move. Bracket it: one dot going backwards, one going forwards. The derivation below (eq. 5.1.6) says where the crossing has to be; check it against your bracket. At that force and a few pN per integrin, ask roughly how many integrins the patch needs; §1.5 is about whether one of them can hold, and Tab 2 about what a patch does to the flow.
  3. Read off what fraction of the motors' total stall force the adhesion absorbs before anything happens. The stall is where the amber dots reach zero.
  4. Now change the membrane load and measure again. Your old dots go grey. The new pink line has the same slope and a different height — the load only moved \(v_{pol}\). The amber line does not move at all.
  5. Halve the motors. Now the amber line hits zero at 100 pN, both lines are twice as steep, and the crossing has moved. The grip a cell needs is set by what it is fighting.

There is one question the panel makes obvious and the algebra answers exactly: how much grip does a cell need before it moves at all? Not before it moves well — before it stops going backwards. The answer takes three lines, and the whole of it is setting \(v_{edge}\) to zero and asking what \(F_{FA}\) must have been.

Where break-even comes from

  1. Define the moment you want. The cell is on the knife edge — not advancing, not retreating:
    \(v_{edge} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; v_{pol} = v_{retro}\)
    The tips are building forward exactly as fast as the network is sliding back. Every monomer added is cancelled.
  2. Substitute eq. 5.1.5 for \(v_{retro}\).
    \(v_{pol} = v_0\left(1 - F^*_{FA}/(N_mF_m)\right)\)
  3. Solve for the grip. Divide by \(v_0\), rearrange:
    \(\dfrac{F^*_{FA}}{N_mF_m} = 1 - \dfrac{v_{pol}}{v_0} \;\Rightarrow\; F^*_{FA} = N_mF_m\left(1-\dfrac{v_{pol}}{v_0}\right)\)
Read the shape of it before any numbers go in. It is a force — the motor team's stall force \(N_mF_m\) — multiplied by a pure number between 0 and 1. That number is the only thing doing any work in the formula, and it is a race between two speeds: how fast the tips can build, against how fast myosin would slide the network if nothing stopped it.
eq. 5.1.6 — break-even $$F^*_{FA} = N_m F_m\left(1 - \frac{v_{pol}}{v_0}\right)$$

Three things are worth reading straight off it, before the arithmetic.

  • The bracket is the fraction of the motors you have to defeat. If the filaments could grow as fast as myosin slides \((v_{pol}=v_0)\) the bracket is zero and no grip is needed at all — growth alone outruns the flow. If they could not grow at all \((v_{pol}=0)\) the bracket is one and you would have to absorb the motors' entire stall force just to stand still. Real cells live between those, and much nearer the second.
  • It scales with the motors, not with the adhesion. \(F^*_{FA}\) is directly proportional to \(N_mF_m\): the requirement is set by what you are fighting, not by what you are fighting with. This is why myosin inhibitors make cells easier to move on weak adhesion, and it is the handle behind half the experiments in this field.
  • It is a threshold, not a target. Below it \(v_{edge}<0\) and the edge is dragged backwards no matter how good the chemistry is. Above it the exchange rate is fixed and linear — differentiate \(v_{edge}=v_{pol}-v_{retro}\) with respect to \(F_{FA}\) and every extra pN of grip buys \(v_0/(N_mF_m) = 120/200 = \mathbf{0.6}\) nm/s of edge speed, all the way until the motors stall.
Worked: how much grip does a cell need before it moves at all?
  1. Write what you know.
    \(N_m=100\) motors, \(F_m=2\) pN, \(v_0=120\) nm/s; membrane load 2 pN per filament

    \(v_{pol} = \delta_{eff}(k_{on}Ce^{-F\delta_{eff}/k_BT}-k_{off}) = 88\) nm/s

    \(F^*_{FA} = N_mF_m(1 - v_{pol}/v_0)\)

\(F^*_{FA} \approx 53\) pN — about 27 % of everything the motor team can produce — before the cell advances by a single nanometre. Past that point every extra pN buys 0.6 nm/s, so the panel's default 120 pN of grip returns \(v_{edge} \approx 40\) nm/s.

Now put that in integrins. §1.3 defined \(F_{FA}=N_{int}\bar{f}_{int}\), and §1.5 is about to show that a single integrin–ligand bond gives out somewhere around a few tens of pN at best. So 53 pN is not one integrin working hard — it is a patch, and 120 pN is a bigger one. Divide it out: at 6 pN each you need about 20 integrins holding at once just to break even, at 3 pN each about 40. That number — how many bonds are gripping simultaneously, not how many are present — is what §2.3's strip measures under each adhesion as it forms, and Tab 3 makes a variable rather than a setting.

The integrin never pushes. It contributes no force of its own. It is a force divider — it decides how much of the motors' output goes into sliding actin and how much goes into the ground. Week 4 promised this line and here it is earned: integrins are not what makes a cell move, they are what lets a surface push back.
Check yourself

1. A drug halves the number of myosin motors. What happens to the break-even adhesion force, and why?

Answer

It halves, because \(F^*_{FA}\) is proportional to \(N_mF_m\). Fewer motors means less retrograde flow to overcome, so less grip is needed. Counter-intuitively, a cell with weaker motors needs weaker adhesion to start moving — which is a real and much-used experimental handle.

2. Lämmermann and colleagues showed that leukocytes with no integrins at all still crawl through three-dimensional tissue. Does that break this section?

Answer

No — it sharpens it. Everything here assumes a flat surface, where the only way to stop the network sliding is to bolt it down. In a three-dimensional mesh a cell has another option. Lämmermann's group call it flowing and squeezing: the leading edge is driven forward by actin-network expansion alone, and myosin contraction is needed only to squeeze the stiff nucleus through narrow gaps. So the geometry supplies what the integrin supplied here. (Be careful with the tempting gloss “it braces against the walls” — that is not what the paper reports; the propulsion is the network's own expansion.) The force balance is the same; what closes it is different.

1.5 · Reinforcement — and the limit one integrin runs into

Start with the problem, because the machinery in this section is an answer to it. §1.4 needed the adhesion to take about 53 pN off the motors before the cell moved at all, and the panel's working point was 120 pN. That force does not reach the integrin by magic — it arrives along actin, through whatever protein is gripping the filament. So ask the question the section is really about: how much can one of those grips actually hold?

Huang and colleagues put a single vinculin molecule on a single actin filament in an optical trap and measured how long the bond survived under load. The answer is not encouraging for a lone tether:

  • Below about 3 pN the bond lasts under a second. Too slack to matter.
  • Between 7 and 10 pN it lasts about ten seconds — it is a catch bond, and this is its sweet spot. (Same trick the integrin pulls in §2.2, on the other end of the chain.)
  • Above about 15 pN it is back under a second. The catch has let go and it is a slip bond again.

Now put the two numbers side by side. Hang the whole 53 pN break-even load on one vinculin–actin tether and you are asking a bond that is already failing at 15 pN to hold three and a half times that. It does not stretch, it does not adapt — it simply lets go, in well under a second, and the adhesion never gets built at all. A single actin tether is not a weak solution to this problem; it is not a solution.

So the arithmetic writes the requirement. If each tether has to sit near its best operating point of ≈ 8 pN, then carrying \(F\) takes roughly \(N \approx F/8\) tethers at once: about 7 to reach break-even, about 15 at the panel's 120 pN. Not one grip holding hard — a crowd of grips each holding gently, and each of them replaced every few seconds as its ten seconds run out. Everything in the rest of this section exists to build that crowd and keep it stocked.

Which brings us to how. The linkage is not a rigid hook. Between actin and the integrin sits talin, a long rod of folded helical bundles, and pulling on it does something remarkable: bundles unfold, and unfolding exposes binding sites that were buried inside them. Vinculin binds those sites. Vinculin brings more actin. The force trying to tear the anchor off is the same force that strengthens it. That is the cell's mechanism for growing the crowd, and — crucially — it is driven by the very load that made the crowd necessary.

Two honest caveats on those numbers. First, Huang's assay used T12 vinculin, a mutant whose autoinhibition is disrupted so that its tail is free to bind actin — the same autoinhibition §1.5's own animation opens on, and the same one Atherton's paper is about. It is the right construct for measuring the tail–actin bond and it is not full-length vinculin in a cell. Second, the bond is directional: over 1–30 pN its mean lifetime is about ten times longer when the load pulls toward the filament's pointed end than toward its barbed end (5.6 s versus 0.54 s in the raw event data). So a tether's strength depends on which way its filament happens to be facing — an asymmetry this module does not model anywhere, and one of the more interesting things it leaves out.

eq. 5.1.8 — what reinforcement buys $$F_{link}(f) = F_0\left(1 + \gamma\,P_{open}(f)\right)$$

Three details people skip, and the animation below is built around all three.

First: talin does not start out as a rod bolted to the integrin. In the cytosol it is autoinhibited — folded back on itself so that its own rod covers the F3 head. Goksoy and colleagues mapped the interaction by NMR and found that the rod's middle region masks the very surface on F3 that the integrin β tail binds, and competes with the tail for it; full-length talin is a much weaker integrin activator than the isolated head, and PIP2 at the membrane is one of the things that lets go. So the rod is not a passive cable waiting on the tail — it is the thing doing the inhibiting. Notice what follows: the domain that later unfolds under load to build the adhesion is the same domain that was keeping it shut. And the order runs inside-out — talin's head docks on the β tail, and the integrin extends into its high-affinity shape because of that, not before it. That is why the animation opens with talin closed and in solution, and an integrin bent over holding nothing.

Second: the sites are cryptic, and unfolding is what reveals them. A vinculin site is not sitting on the outside of the rod waiting to be noticed — it is buried inside a folded helical bundle, and the only way to expose it is to pull the bundle apart. So the reveal and the extension are the same event. Watch the rod lengthen in the panel: every bundle that opens adds contour, and because the integrin end is pinned to the ground, that extra contour shows up as the actin end having slipped further rearward. Talin unfolding is the cell paying out rope in exchange for a place to build.

Third: the reinforcing machinery is not folded up inside the linkage waiting to deploy, and neither is the actin. Vinculin and the rest of the adaptors are dissolved in the cytoplasm, drifting. Load reveals a site; a diffusing partner has to find that site and dock on it; and only then does a filament polymerise off that vinculin and run into the retrograde bundle. The reinforcement is not a spare rope being clipped on — it is a new line of force grown in place, off the linkage, subunit by subunit. Reveal, recruit from solution, grow — and repeat.

Where this is still argued. §1.5 tells a force-gated story: no load, no exposed site, no vinculin. That is what del Rio and Yao measured on single talin rods in a magnetic trap. But Atherton and colleagues, working in cells rather than on isolated molecules, found that simply relieving talin's and vinculin's autoinhibition is sufficient for the two to bind tightly, with no force required — and that paxillin can hold either one before talin is involved at all. Read §1.5 as the mechanics of the force-bearing linkage, not as the only route by which vinculin ever reaches talin.

That middle step is a diffusion problem, which means reinforcement has a speed limit — watch for that wait in the cascade below.

One thing to watch for in the panel: the bundle is not on a clock. Its rearward motion is computed from the state of the clutch, not animated for effect. Unbound, it runs. Bound, it is arrested — and the small creep you see while a talin bundle is unravelling is not the actin slipping past the anchor, it is the extra contour talin has just paid out. That distinction is the difference between a clutch and a greased pole, and it is the same quantity §1.4 called \(v_{retro}\).

Note from Dr. McGhee. The numbers here are worth knowing. Talin's rod carries up to eleven vinculin binding sites, and its weakest bundle — R3, identified by mutating it and watching the unfolding peak move from 5 to 8 pN — opens at around 5 pN, barely more than the thermal noise floor. About 12 pN is enough to take vinculin binding from one site to three. So this is not an exotic high-force phenomenon; it is happening constantly, at forces a single myosin can supply.

And there is a detail that changes what kind of machine this is. Yao's group stretched talin, let vinculin bind, then relaxed the force — and the rod did not fold back up. Bound vinculin blocks refolding, and under 7 pN is enough to set that going. So reinforcement is not a spring that relaxes when you stop pulling; it is a latch. The adhesion remembers having been loaded. That is a very different object from the elastic picture, and it is why an adhesion can mature under a transient pull and stay matured.

The same work found vinculin binding is biphasic — suppressed below about 5 pN because the bundles are still folded, and suppressed again above about 25 pN because the bound vinculin heads get stripped off. There is a force window in which reinforcement works at all. Hold that next to §2.2, where the integrin at the other end of the chain turns out to have a force window of its own.

Reinforcement is self-limiting for a lone integrin. Strengthening a chain from the top does nothing for the link at the bottom. The only way out is to stop being alone.

2.2 · One integrin under flow — loaded until it breaks, or holds

Before a patch, one integrin, and the question §1.5 left open: a single tether cannot hold the load — but what exactly happens to it while the flow is pulling? A bond held at a constant force has a lifetime — that is Bell, eq. 4.2.5. A bond in a cell is not held at a constant force. It is ramped: actin flows rearward, the linkage stretches, and the load climbs from zero at a rate that is not a free parameter. It is the linkage stiffness times the flow:

eq. 5.2.2 — the loading rate $$r = \frac{df}{dt} = \kappa\,v$$

How the bond answers a ramp depends on which kind of bond it is — and before this section adds a second kind, it owes you the first one back with its letters named. Tab 1 ended on Bell's law for the integrin–ligand bond; Week 4 minted it for a myosin head letting go. Here it is once more, written as the quantity the panel below actually plots: not the rate of letting go, but how long the bond lasts, \(\tau = 1/k_{off}\).

eq. 4.2.5 again — Bell's law, written as the lifetime the panel plots $$\tau(f) = \tau_0\,e^{-f/f_\beta} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k_{off}(f) = \frac{1}{\tau(f)} = k_0\,e^{f/f_\beta}, \qquad \tau_0 = \frac{1}{k_0}, \qquad f_\beta = \frac{k_BT}{x_\beta}$$

Three letters, and two axes. \(\tau\) is the panel's vertical axis and \(f\) its horizontal one. \(k_0\) is the off-rate with nothing pulling, so \(\tau_0 = 1/k_0\) is how long the bond lasts on its own — 10 s at the \(k_0 = 0.1\) s⁻¹ of Tab 3's clutch cast. \(f_\beta\) is the force that cuts the lifetime by a factor of \(e\): it is \(k_BT\) spread over the bond's own give, \(x_\beta\), the distance the load has to drag the bond before it is over its barrier (Week 4, §2.3). At the cast's \(f_\beta = 2\) pN, 4 pN makes the bond 7 times more likely to break and 10 pN makes it 150 times more likely, so a 10 s bond lasts 0.07 s. That is a slip bond: every piconewton shortens its life, with no exceptions and no window. It is the only kind of bond Week 4 knew, and it is the \(k_{off}\) Tab 1 warned you about — the one that tears a reinforced linkage off at the bottom.

The integrin–fibronectin bond does something Bell's law cannot. It has two routes out of the bound state, and force opens one while closing the other. The two rates add, so the lifetime is one over their sum — and because one term rises with \(f\) while the other falls, that lifetime has a peak:

eq. 5.2.3 — two ways out of one bound state, and how long the bond lasts $$k_{off}(f) = \underbrace{k_s\,e^{f/F_s}}_{\text{slip: force opens it}} + \underbrace{k_c\,e^{-f/F_c}}_{\text{catch: force closes it}}, \qquad \tau(f) = \frac{1}{k_{off}(f)}, \qquad f^{*} = \frac{F_sF_c}{F_s+F_c}\,\ln\frac{k_cF_s}{k_sF_c}$$

On the panel, \(\tau(f)\) is the green curve and \(f^{*}\) — where \(dk_{off}/df = 0\), the closing route's fall exactly cancelling the opening route's rise — is the dashed vertical through its peak. In this fit the bond lasts 0.8 s with nothing pulling and 2.4 s at \(f^{*} = 23\) pN; the red curve beside it is eq. 4.2.5's \(\tau_0 e^{-f/f_\beta}\), which can only fall.

Read the first term again. \(k_s e^{f/F_s}\) is eq. 4.2.5: \(k_s\) is Bell's \(k_0\) and \(F_s\) is Bell's \(f_\beta\), renamed only so the two routes can be told apart. Set \(k_c = 0\) and eq. 5.2.3 collapses back to eq. 4.2.5 — the whole of Week 4's picture is the slip term. Everything new is the second term, an exit that force shuts, and it is what makes \(k_{off}\) fall before it rises. The panel runs both: the slip bond with Tab 3's cast (\(k_0 = 0.1\) s⁻¹, \(f_\beta = 2\) pN), and the catch bond with \(k_s = 0.1\) s⁻¹, \(F_s = 22\) pN, \(k_c = 1.1\) s⁻¹, \(F_c = 11\) pN — four numbers fitted so the window lands where Kong measured it, and labelled as a fit. Notice the two slip force-scales, 2 pN against 22 pN: same law, and in this fit the integrin's own slip route needs eleven times the force per \(e\)-fold that a generic clutch does. At 15 pN the catch route still takes 59 % of the exits; by 50 pN it takes 1 %, and the integrin is a Bell bond with \(F_s = 22\) pN again.

Between roughly 10 and 30 pN the closing route wins and the bond lives longer the harder it is pulled — a catch bond. Below that window it is short-lived; above it the catch route is shut and it slips like anything else. Kong and colleagues measured the window directly; the panel below lets you measure it too, and the histogram is the evidence.

Why there is no single rupture force to quote, and what Li actually measured

A rupture force is not a property of a bond. It is a property of a bond and the rate you loaded it at. Li and colleagues measured exactly this for α5β1–fibronectin across three orders of magnitude of loading rate, and the same bond broke at 69 ± 2 pN at ~2,000 pN/s and around 120 pN at 18,000 pN/s. Same bond. Same lab. Same afternoon. There is no single answer to quote, and now you can see why there could not be.

They found something else worth knowing: the spectrum has two regimes — a slow one below 10,000 pN/s and a fast one above it — because the complex has two activation barriers, an outer and an inner. Below the crossover the outer barrier is rate-limiting; above it, the outer barrier is effectively gone and the steeper inner one takes over.

What is anchored and what is not. The crossover at 10,000 pN/s and the two-regime finding are from the paper's abstract. Its fitted barrier parameters were never retrieved, so no slopes are drawn or quoted here.

And one more, which is the same trap in a different coat: Li's method was a force ramp analysed with Bell's law — a method that assumes the off-rate rises with force and therefore cannot detect a catch bond. Both results are correct inside their own frame.

This is the unit the rest of the tab is built from. A clutch is a bond being ramped at \(\kappa v\) until it breaks, then rebinding at zero force and starting again. §2.3 runs this seventy-five times side by side under one piece of membrane. Tab 3 lets the failures feed back on \(v\).

2.3 · A patch of integrins slows the flow under it — and when it does not

Now put \(N\) of §2.2's integrins under one short piece of leading edge and ask a purely local question: what does that patch do to the flow directly above it, and therefore to the membrane directly in front of it?

Alexandrova and colleagues watched exactly this happen. Retrograde flow at a cell's edge runs in two zones — fast in the lamellipodium (0.5–7 µm/min), slow in the lamellum behind it (0.05–2.5 µm/min) — with an abrupt drop of two-fold or more at the boundary. Nascent adhesions appear in the fast zone, and within seconds of one appearing the flow immediately behind it slows and the fast/slow boundary jumps forward to meet it. Take the adhesions away — spread cells on poly-L-lysine, which integrins cannot bind — and the flow is uniform from edge to centre, with no boundary at all. Their proposed mechanism is the simplest one: mechanical resistance from actin anchored to the new adhesion. That is this module's clutch, and this section builds it.

What "a patch" physically is. Not a blob. Kanchanawong's group mapped a focal adhesion in three dimensions and found a plaque under 200 nm thick with three stacked layers about 40 nm apart — integrin tails, FAK and paxillin at the membrane; talin and vinculin in the middle, carrying the force; actin regulators on top. Changede and Sheetz found its modular unit: a cluster about 100 nm across holding roughly 50 activated integrins, forming on soft substrates and stiff ones alike. A mature adhesion is many of those modules side by side. So when the panel below drops forty to a hundred and fifty integrins into a few hundred nanometres of edge, that is one to a few modules' worth — the biological unit, not Tab 1's lone integrin.

Each segment of edge in the panel is one clutch module — Tab 3's cast, \(N_m\) motors pulling, and the integrins of whatever adhesion has formed under it as the clutches. Each segment runs its own Monte Carlo of §2.2's cycle, so how many of its integrins are gripping at this instant is measured, not assumed; its summed grip is the local \(F_{FA}\); eq. 5.1.5 turns that into a local \(v_{retro}\); eq. 4.4.3 turns that into how fast that segment of membrane advances. The load each bound integrin carries is simply the total divided among those bound:

eq. 5.2.4 — load sharing, locally $$\bar f(x) = \frac{F_{FA}(x)}{N_{bound}(x)}$$

— on average. The clutch model shares load unevenly by construction, because each bond carries \(\kappa\) times however far the actin has dragged it since it bound, and a bond that bound a second ago is carrying more than one that bound just now. Uneven sharing is not a complication added to this model; it falls out of it, and it is why a patch loses bonds one at a time rather than all at once.

Three things the panel has to declare, none of them physics the page reports a number from. The actin network is a connected sheet, so a patch's grip reaches a little way sideways — one segment here, a modelling choice; Alexandrova's boundary is "festooned", with its apexes at the adhesions: local, but not point-like. The membrane is a connected sheet too, so a segment that runs ahead pulls its neighbours after it and is held back by them — that is how one finger becomes a whole edge advancing, and the panel draws it with a relaxation term that moves membrane sideways without making any. And the cell body follows the front, never backwards and never faster than the rear lets go — §2.4's \(v_{rear}\) — which is why the body lags the finger and then catches up. When the adhesions are nucleated, and how big they are, is the panel's clock, not the cell's; everything that happens under and in front of them is the clutch.

When does the patch do better or worse? Gardel and colleagues measured traction force and actin flow at the same adhesions at the same time, and found the relationship is biphasic. Near the edge, where flow is fast (~25 nm/s) and adhesions are forming, traction is inversely related to flow — slower flow, more traction, the clutch engaging. In larger adhesions where flow is slow (~2 nm/s) the relation is direct — traction falls with the flow. The switch sits at a threshold of 8–10 nm/s, and it did not move when they changed adhesion size, age, protein density, position, or Rho and myosin signalling. Flow speed, not adhesion anatomy, is what decides.

eq. 5.2.5 — what one more integrin buys $$\frac{dv_{edge}}{dN} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{v_0\,\bar f}{N_mF_m} & F_{FA} < N_mF_m \\[6pt] 0 & F_{FA} \ge N_mF_m \end{cases}$$

Three regimes, from one derivative

  1. Slipping. Few integrins, flow near \(v_0\), loading rate \(\kappa v_0\) high: each bond breaks in a fraction of a second and few are bound at any instant. \(F_{FA}\) is small, the flow is barely touched, and the patch builds almost no traction. It is not that the integrins are weak — they are being pulled off faster than they can grip.
  2. Engaging. Enough grip to slow the flow; slower flow means a lower loading rate, so bonds last longer, so more are bound, so the flow slows further. Every integrin added buys \(v_0\bar f/N_mF_m\) of edge speed and adds traction. This is Gardel's inverse branch.
  3. Saturated. \(F_{FA}\) reaches the motors' stall; the load line is pinned at zero flow and \(v_{edge} = v_{pol}\). Another integrin adds traction and nothing else. And now the per-bond force is \(\kappa v\) times the time bound, so it falls as the flow falls — Gardel's direct branch, which appears when what changes is the motor drive rather than the grip.
A patch is not a knob you turn up. Too few integrins and it slips; enough and each one you add moves the edge; past the stall it only pulls on the ground. Where the kink sits is set by the flow, not by the adhesion — which is what Gardel found.

2.4 · Two sides, and the adhesions that steer them

Week 4 ended with a cell crawling over a bead grid and a promise: next week the beads will move. Here is that cell again, seen from above with the camera locked to the cell — it holds still on the screen, heading up, and the ground moves under it — and this time the ground is part of the story. Every adhesion it makes is fixed to the substrate. It is born as a small cluster just behind the leading edge, and it does not travel with the cell: the cell moves over it. The steering is one line. Two unequal sides advance at different speeds, and a cell whose left outruns its right turns:

eq. 5.2.6 — turning from a speed difference $$\omega = \frac{v_L - v_R}{W}, \qquad v_{side} = \min\!\left(v_{edge,side},\; v_{rear,side}\right)$$

— each side of a cell of width \(W\) moving at the slower of its own front protrusion and its own rear release (eq. 5.2.1's rule, applied per side), and the difference across the width rotating the cell at \(\omega\). The front of a side protrudes at \(v_{pol}\) minus the flow that side's front adhesions leave running (§2.3's patch, now split into the adhesions you can see); its rear lets go at the body's unopposed speed reduced by its rear adhesions' grip through the load line (eq. 4.2.4). No new physics; the module's equations composed once more, with the adhesions drawn where they actually are.

Now read what the evidence says, because it is not quite what the opening sentence assumed. Allen, Barnhart, Theriot and Mogilner followed fish keratocytes turning spontaneously — 29 of 38 cells circled persistently, "knots on a string" — and asked where the asymmetry that steers them lives. At the rear. Turning starts with asymmetric actin flow created by asymmetry in adhesion and myosin contractility at the cell rear; that rotates the corners of the lamellipodium and so rotates the whole protruding front. They did see faster protrusion on the outer side of a turning cell's leading edge — but "these asymmetries in protrusion at the leading edge appear to play only a minor role", becoming dominant only when myosin was inhibited. Cells steer from the rear.

That is §2.3's regime map applied to a whole cell, and eq. 5.2.6 says how. A keratocyte's front runs fast and its grip there is saturated, so \(v_{edge}\) is pinned at \(v_{pol}\) on both sides — more adhesion at the front buys traction and no speed. The rear is where grip still changes a speed, so the rear is where an asymmetry bites. Asymmetric adhesion steers; where it bites depends on which end of each side is doing the limiting. Take one side of the front out of saturation and the front asymmetry gets its vote back — which is what myosin inhibition does in their cells.

3.2 · The motors, the linkage — and what breaks first

The motor side of this problem is Week 4's, in form. A team that runs at \(v_0\) against nothing and stalls against a load it cannot beat, with speed falling linearly in between. That is the load line, and it is the same line:

eq. 5.1.5 again — the load line, now driving the network $$v_a = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_{FA}}{N_m F_m}\right), \qquad v_a \equiv v_{retro}$$

Two pieces of bookkeeping before we go on, because both are the kind of thing that quietly wrecks a calculation.

First, \(v_a\) is \(v_{retro}\). Same quantity, and from here on the clutch literature's name for it. It is the speed the actin network moves rearward, which is also the speed a bound clutch gets stretched at. Nothing new — just a shorter subscript for something Tab 1 spent five sections on.

Where the stretch lives. Now the other end of the chain — and this is the part that makes "ignore the matrix" an honest modelling choice rather than something swept under the rug, so it is worth being explicit.

The ground is rigid. It does not move, ever, by assumption. So when a clutch is bound, its outer end is pinned and its inner end is being dragged rearward with the actin — and the difference has to go somewhere. It goes into stretching the linkage itself. Talin is a spring; that is what the spring is for.

What breaks first. Everything above says how force builds in a chain that is being pulled: the motors set a ceiling, the linkage stretches, and the stretch is the force. Nothing yet says when the chain gives. This week the bonds answered that on a clock of their own — a lifetime, not a limit. Next week's solids answer it differently, with a strength: a force at which the piece fails, full stop. Put a strength next to Hooke's law and you get a third number for free — how far the piece stretches before it goes. That is the idea to carry into Week 6, and the actin filament is the right place to meet it, because both of its numbers have been measured on single filaments.

eq. 5.3.2 again — Hooke, now for the filament $$f = \kappa_a\,x, \qquad \kappa_a = 43.7\ \text{pN/nm for one micron of filament}$$

Kojima, Ishijima and Yanagida hung single actin filaments between glass microneedles and stretched them: 43.7 ± 4.6 pN/nm for a micron of bare filament, 65.3 with tropomyosin along it. Twice the length is half the stiffness — the same stretch shared over twice as much filament — and next week says why in one line. Tsuda and colleagues, with the same needles, pulled filaments until they broke: about 600 pN for an untwisted filament, falling to 320 pN once it had been twisted through a quarter turn. So the filament has a stiffness and a strength, and dividing one by the other says how far it can be stretched at all:

eq. 5.3.3 — stretch to failure $$x_{fail} = \frac{F_{break}}{\kappa}, \qquad\text{and in a chain the force is shared, so the first to go is the smallest } F_{break}\text{ — not the largest stretch}$$

For a micron of actin, \(600/43.7 \approx 14\) nm: 1.4 % of its length, and no more. Kojima's own estimate for a working muscle is that its thin filaments stretch 0.23 % under isometric force — about a sixth of the way to breaking. Now do the same sum for the other element in the chain, the linkage. Its stiffness is \(\kappa_c = 0.8\) pN/nm, and the one integrin–ligand bond at its foot carries about 40 pN before it lets go — the peak Wang & Ha measured on a single bond in a living cell (§2.2): \(x_{fail} = 40/0.8 = 50\) nm. Put the two in series, pull, and read off who does what. At the 40 pN that ends the linkage, a micron of actin has stretched \(40/43.7 \approx 0.9\) nm — the linkage has taken 98 % of the stretch and all of the risk. That is this section's claim, the linkage is the only thing that stretches, as a number rather than an assumption. And the filament is nowhere near its own limit, nor will it ever be from here: the motors of this model stop at \(N_mF_m = 200\) pN, a third of what it takes.

Two things fall out that are less obvious, and the bench below is built to show them. First, stretch to failure does not depend on how much of the material there is. An adhesion of \(N\) bonds sharing the load is \(N\) springs in parallel: it holds \(N\) times the force and is \(N\) times as stiff, so it still fails at exactly 50 nm. A filament twice as long is half as stiff but no weaker, so it fails at the same 1.4 %. In both cases the size cancels and what is left belongs to the material — which is exactly the move Week 6 makes when it divides force by area and stretch by length. Second, the first thing to break is the weakest, not the one that has stretched most. Make the adhesion strong enough, and give the motors enough to pull with, and the filament — which has barely moved — is what snaps.

eq. 3.2.4 again — Langmuir, now counting clutches: \(n\) is the bound fraction $$\frac{dn}{dt} = k_{on}(1-n) - k_{off}(f)\,n, \qquad n = \frac{N_b}{N_c} \in [0,1]$$

eq. 5.3.4 — the traction: the bound clutches' forces, summed — which is their number times their mean $$F_{FA} = \sum_{i\,\in\,\text{bound}} \kappa_c x_i = N_b\,\langle f\rangle = N_c\,n\,\langle f\rangle$$

Three equations — and the boxes alone do not show how they hold hands, in particular where \(dn/dt\) ever touches \(F_{FA}\) or \(v_a\). So here is the wiring, every hand-off named:

the loop, wired — which equation feeds which $$\begin{aligned} &v_a \;\xrightarrow{\;\dot x_i = v_a\;}\; x_i \;\xrightarrow{\;f_i = \kappa_c x_i\;\;(5.3.2)\;}\; f_i \;\xrightarrow{\;k_{off}(f_i)\;\;(4.2.5\text{ or }5.2.3)\;}\; \frac{dn}{dt} = k_{on}(1-n) - k_{off}\,n \;\;(3.2.4)\\[6pt] &\frac{dn}{dt} \;\longrightarrow\; n \;\xrightarrow{\;N_b = N_c\,n\;}\; F_{FA} = N_b\,\langle f\rangle \;\;(5.3.4) \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{the load line}\;}\; v_a = v_0\!\left(1 - \tfrac{F_{FA}}{N_mF_m}\right)\;\;(5.3.5) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{back to the top} \end{aligned}$$

Read it as a circuit. The flow \(v_a\) is the input to every bound clutch: it stretches each one at \(\dot x_i = v_a\), so each carries \(f_i = \kappa_c x_i\) and is loaded at \(\kappa_c v_a\). That force sets the clutch's off-rate, \(k_{off}(f_i)\) — and that is where \(v_a\) enters eq. 3.2.4: faster flow means higher forces sooner, a larger \(k_{off}\), and a bound fraction that falls. The bound fraction is what eq. 3.2.4 integrates, and it enters the traction as a head count, \(N_b = N_c n\), each one carrying \(\langle f\rangle\) on average. The traction sets \(v_a\) through the load line, and \(v_a\) is back at the top. Eq. 5.3.5 is implicit — \(v_a\) appears on both sides — and that is not a flaw in the writing, it is the content: more bound clutches → more resisting force → slower actin → slower loading → longer-lived bonds → more bound clutches. Say that sentence twice; it is the motor–clutch model, and nothing in it was added by hand.

Where the clock is. Four of the five arrows are instantaneous: given \(v_a\), the forces, the off-rates and the traction follow at once, and given the traction so does \(v_a\). Only eq. 3.2.4 has a \(d/dt\) in it. That is why the loop has a time scale at all — about \(1/k_{on} \approx 1\) s here, plus however long a loaded bond lives — and why it can lag, overshoot, or in principle oscillate, which is §3.4's question. Set \(dn/dt = 0\) and eq. 3.2.4 hands you the bound fraction the bench settles to, \(\bar n = k_{on}/(k_{on} + \langle k_{off}\rangle)\), Langmuir's balance with a force-dependent off-rate, where \(1/\langle k_{off}\rangle\) is the mean time a clutch stays bound. Put that into eqs. 5.3.4 and 5.3.5 and you have the loop's steady state — which is what §3.4 solves in closed form.

What the bench does with all this. It runs the loop clutch by clutch, not as the mean-field \(n\): each of the \(N_c\) springs has its own \(x_i\), \(f_i\) and \(k_{off}(f_i)\), binds and unbinds on its own dice, and the panel simply counts. Eq. 3.2.4 is what those dice average to, and the ledger under the sliders prints its two fluxes live — how many clutches bind per second, \(k_{on}(N_c - N_b)\), and how many let go, \(\sum_{bound} k_{off}(f_i)\) — so you can watch them balance. Every clutch is drawn individually as its own spring: it stretches while bound, colours by load, and visibly snaps back when it goes.

3.4 · Three states — and something that should be here and is not

Sweep the clutch count and the system moves through three regimes. Free slip: too few clutches, each loaded fast and broken fast, flow near \(v_0\), almost no traction. Partial grip: real engagement, large fluctuations. Stalled: enough clutches that the actin stops, and the traction settles at exactly \(N_mF_m\) — the motors' own stall force, because clutches cannot pull harder than the thing pulling them.

Now the part worth your attention. If you have read about the motor–clutch model before, you were probably shown a dramatic load-and-fail oscillation: traction builds, every clutch fails at once, the network snaps back, and it starts over. Look for it here. Sweep every slider. It is not there.

That is not a bug, and it took measuring to be sure of. On rigid ground each clutch loads on its own private spring, and the only thing coupling them is the shared flow speed — a weak, slow channel. Nothing synchronises their failures, so what you get is fluctuation, not a cycle. Checking the autocorrelation of the traction confirms it: there is no periodicity to find.

  1. Find the clutch count where the traction curve flattens. Confirm the plateau equals \(N_mF_m\).
  2. Change the motor count and watch the whole ceiling move with it.
  3. Switch to catch bonds and note which way the regime boundaries slide.

Every clutch is replaced by an average clutch: it binds at \(k_{on}\), lives for the mean time \(\langle t_{on}\rangle\), and carries the mean force \(\langle f\rangle\) while it does. Multiply by how many are bound and you have the traction; the traction sets the flow; the flow sets the loading; round it goes until it stops changing.

A trap worth pointing at, because this module fell into it. \(\langle f\rangle\) is the force averaged over the time a clutch is bound — not the force it happens to be carrying when it breaks. Those are different numbers, and the second is much larger, because a bond is most likely to break exactly when it is most loaded. An earlier version of this page averaged the rupture forces and got a traction three times too high. Nothing on screen looked wrong; it simply disagreed with the Monte Carlo, which is why the two are now drawn on the same axes where you can see them agree.

Check yourself

1. In the stalled state the traction sits at \(N_mF_m\) no matter how many more clutches you add. Why can it not go higher?

Answer

Because the clutches are not generating force — they are resisting it, and the only thing pulling is the motors. A clutch can at most hold everything the motors produce. Past that, extra clutches just share the same total more thinly. This is also why traction is a bad readout for "how much adhesion a cell has" once it is stalled.

2. Catch bonds raise the traction sharply at the same clutch count. Trace the loop and say why.

Answer

Longer-lived bonds accumulate, so more are bound at once; more bound means more resisting force, which slows the actin; slower actin means a lower loading rate, so each bond sits at lower force for longer, which — for a catch bond in its window — makes it live longer still. The feedback that destroys a slip-bond adhesion stabilises a catch-bond one.

  4. Substitute.

Be careful about what this does and does not show, because it is easy to oversell. Two numbers were chosen rather than measured — the membrane load and the tip concentration \(C\) — and the answer moves with both. That matters: with two free inputs you can reach a fair part of the 5–10 band, so "it agrees" is a weaker statement than it looks. What is not free is everything else: the rate constants, \(\delta\), the 35° geometry, the form of the ratchet law. And the two free values did have to land somewhere physically sensible for the agreement to mean anything — a couple of pN is about one myosin, and 10 µM sits inside the range Week 4 derived for the tip after profilin recycling (its worked example used 14 µM against ~2 µM of bare free actin). Two more caveats worth stating plainly. The clutch state was given to us by the measurement, not predicted. And Pollard's constants were measured at 22 °C while \(k_BT\) here is the cell's 37 °C — using 4.07 pN·nm throughout instead gives 5.01 µm/min, still inside the band, which is the useful thing to know about that inconsistency. So: the model is consistent with the measurement and returns sensible values for the things we could not measure. That is a real but modest success. It is not a proof of mechanism, and it is not a parameter-free prediction.

Chemistry, two choices, and a measurement that does not contradict them. Two rate constants from 1986, a branch angle, a couple of pN of membrane tension and a tip concentration put a white blood cell's crawling speed in the right place to within the width of the measured band. 5.1 · Force against displacement

Everything this week rested on one sentence: the ground does not move. Before we take it away, we need a language for ground that does — and it starts somewhere deliberately unglamorous.

Push something. It moves. Push twice as hard and, over a useful range, it moves twice as far. The constant of proportionality is the stiffness, and it is the slope of a force–displacement line.

eq. 5.5.1 — Hooke $$F = k\,x$$

You have been using this all week without naming it. The clutch was a spring of \(\kappa_c = 0.8\) pN/nm, and §3.2's whole argument was that its stretch is where the force came from.

  1. Set the stiffness to 0.8 pN/nm — a talin linkage — and find the displacement that gives 12 pN, talin's unfolding threshold.
  2. Halve the stiffness. How far does it have to stretch now for the same force?

5.2 · Stress against strain — dividing the geometry out

Here is the trouble with \(k\). It belongs to an object, not to a material. A short fat piece of collagen and a long thin piece of the same collagen have completely different spring constants, and nothing about that tells you anything about collagen.

So normalise. Divide force by the area carrying it, and displacement by the length being stretched:

eq. 5.5.2 — stress, strain, and the modulus $$\sigma = \frac{F}{A}, \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L}, \qquad \sigma = E\,\varepsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = \frac{EA}{L}$$

Now \(E\) belongs to the material. Change the shape and \(k\) moves while \(E\) stays put — which is exactly what the two panels below do.

  1. Change the length and the area. Watch \(k\) move on the left and the stress–strain line refuse to move on the right.
  2. Set \(E\) to 1 kPa, which is in the range measured for brain tissue. Now think about the plastic dish most cell experiments are done on, at about \(3\times10^6\) kPa — six and a half orders of magnitude stiffer.
Two warnings about "tissue stiffness" before you trust any of these numbers.

It depends on the length scale you poke at. Elkin's group indented live rat hippocampus with an AFM tip and measured 0.14–0.31 kPa; Stewart's group indented brain at millimetre scale and got 1.56 ± 0.75 kPa. That is a factor of five or more on the same tissue, and neither is wrong — a cell-sized probe and a millimetre-sized probe are asking different questions of a material that is porous, fluid-filled and not uniform. When you read "brain is 1 kPa", ask at what scale.

And check numbers even when everyone repeats them. This module said "plastic dish ≈ 10⁶ kPa" until an audit caught it. Chasing the figure down, it traces to a widely cited paper whose abstract says 10⁶ kPa and whose own body text says ~3 GPa — which is 3 × 10⁶ kPa — citing a materials handbook. Both numbers are in the same paper. The handbook value for polystyrene is 3.0–3.5 GPa, so the body text is right and the abstract's shorthand is what propagated. A factor of three is not important for the point being made here. The habit of checking is.

5.3 · Now let the ground give

Here, and only here, the ground is allowed to move. One spring underneath the whole adhesion — nothing else about Tab 3's bench changes.

Two things happen, and both were invisible all week.

First, traction stops being monotonic in stiffness. There is a best ground, and the rigid floor you spent four tabs on is not it — it is worse than an intermediate one by a clear margin. Too stiff and the clutches take the full loading rate and snap almost as soon as they load — eq. 5.2.2 with nothing to soften it. On the soft side the falloff is gentler and has a different cause: the ground yields, so each clutch takes longer to reach a useful force and fewer are loaded at once. Be careful with the tempting version of that sentence — “too soft and nothing loads” is wrong, and an earlier, under-converged version of this very sweep said it. Run long enough, the softest ground here still out-pulls the rigid floor.

Second — and this is the one to enjoy — the load-and-fail cycle appears. The oscillation §3.4 went looking for and could not find shows up the moment the ground can move, at the same stiffness where the traction peaks.

That is not a coincidence. Both come from the same thing: once the ground is compliant, every bound clutch shares one displacement. A failure re-loads the survivors, so they go together, and the whole adhesion cycles. Rigid ground has no shared displacement — which is precisely why Tab 3 found no cycle and could not have.

It was incomplete in a specific, diagnosable way, and holding it strictly for four tabs is what made the missing piece obvious. That is usually how a good simplification retires.

5.4 · …and it is not even elastic

One more, briefly, because it changes the shape of next week. Real matrix is not a spring. Load it and it keeps deforming; hold it stretched and the force bleeds away. It creeps and it relaxes.

So "stiffness" is not one number. It depends how fast you pull — and look at the timescales in this module. A matrix takes seconds to settle. A clutch bond lives about a second. Those are the same timescale, which means the ground a clutch feels is not the ground a slow indenter would measure.
