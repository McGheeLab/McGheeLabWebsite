Last week a cell read a concentration. This week it acts on it. A neutrophil in the infected bite reads the gradient across its own body — a few percent more receptors occupied at its front than at its back — and then rebuilds itself: a chemical map forms under the membrane, actin grows where the map says front, motors pull the rest along, and a slow array of microtubules keeps the whole thing pointed. Every one of those parts is Week 3's door again — a subunit arrives at \(k_{on}C\), leaves at \(k_{off}\), and builds the next site as it binds — repeated at three sizes. This module takes the skeleton apart filament by filament, then runs the cell with every number earned.

1.1 · Actin polymerization: one end, one site, and a clock

Start with the simplest polymer: a filament with one growing end. In a bath of monomer at concentration \(C\) a subunit lands there at rate \(k_{on}C\) and the terminal subunit falls off at \(k_{off}\) — Week 3's door with Pollard's 1986 numbers for ATP-actin at the fast (barbed) end, \(k_{on} = 11.6\) µM⁻¹s⁻¹ and \(k_{off} = 1.4\) s⁻¹. Each arrival adds 2.7 nm; each departure removes it. The length is a random walk in time — Week 1's drunkard — whose two step directions have different rates, so the walk has a drift.

Can Einstein–Smoluchowski price the arrivals? Only if we are careful about which length we give it. The 2.7 nm above is what a subunit adds to the filament; it is not how big the molecule is. Sizing G-actin from its own 42-kDa mass gives a hydrodynamic radius \(a_G \approx 2.8\) nm (the first worked example does it), and a sphere that size diffuses at 81 µm²/s in water, so the encounter rate with a target of that size is \(k_s = 4\pi Da_G \approx 1{,}700\) µM⁻¹s⁻¹ (eq. 1.5.1). Pollard's 11.6 is one arrival in 150. Drenckhahn and Pollard settled what the missing factor is: the barbed-end rate scales as one over the solvent's viscosity, exactly as a diffusion-limited reaction must, so the door is diffusion-limited and the factor is orientation — a monomer must arrive at a small site in the right pose. The pointed end is different: its rate does not follow the viscosity, so it is limited by a slower chemical step.

Watch the filament itself on the left. New subunits arrive green (ATP); behind the tip they turn yellow (ADP·Pᵢ) and then orange (ADP) as the nucleotide is hydrolysed and the phosphate leaves. That colour is the clock, sped up for display — the real phosphate release takes minutes.

  1. One filament at 0.5 µM: the length climbs, but not smoothly. Read the slope against \(\delta(k_{on}C - k_{off})\), and watch the trace wander in and out of the shaded band.
  2. Forty copies: the mean climbs at the drift speed and the variance between copies grows as \(t\), so the spread grows as \(\sqrt{t}\) — a diffusion in length. Read the measured \(D_L\) against \((k_{on}C + k_{off})/2\).
  3. Watch the bath before you watch the filament. Most of what reaches the tip is grey and leaves again — wrong orientation, no bond. The stats print the true ratio, about 150 arrivals per subunit added; the drawing shows six, because 150 would be a fog. That ratio is the worked example below, happening on screen.
  4. Slide C to 10 µM: the drift grows twentyfold, the spread less. Then count the green cap against \((k_{on}C - k_{off})/k_{hyd}\) — the net arrival rate times the life of an unhydrolysed subunit. At high C the tip outruns the clock and the cap floods the window.

Name what the sim measured. Subunits arrive at \(k_{on}C\) per second and leave at \(k_{off}\); each is \(\delta = 2.7\) nm of length:

eq. 4.1.1 — the growth speed of a filament end $$v = \delta\,(k_{on}C - k_{off})$$

That is the mean. Now the wobble around it — and a word has to be rescued before it can be used.

When you met diffusion in Week 1 it was a particle being knocked about in space. The thing that moved was an object, and the axis it moved along was a direction you could point at in the room. Nothing in this panel is doing that. The filament is pinned; on screen its tip sits still and the beads pile up behind it. What wanders is not a position. It is the filament's length.

That is still diffusion, and not by analogy. Diffusion is not a claim about space — it is a claim about any quantity that changes by independent random increments. Any such quantity has the same two properties: a mean that moves at a steady rate, and a spread that grows as the square root of time. The number relating the spread to the time is called a diffusion coefficient whatever the quantity happens to be. Here the quantity is a length, so \(D_L\) carries units of length² per time exactly as a spatial \(D\) does — and the "space" it diffuses through is the one-dimensional line of every length this filament could have. A filament at 0.34 µm is one step from 0.3427 µm and one step from 0.3373 µm, and it takes those steps at random. That line is its world.

The two rate constants now enter twice, and differently:

eq. 4.1.2 — the length diffusion coefficient $$D_L = \frac{\delta^2\,(k_{on}C + k_{off})}{2}, \qquad \langle(L - vt)^2\rangle = 2D_Lt$$

Look at which combination appears where. The drift (eq. 4.1.1) is the difference of the two rates; the spread is their sum. Departures subtract from the length and yet they add to the noise, because noise does not care which way an event pushed — only that it happened and that nobody chose when. Both doors contribute, always.

That has a consequence worth stopping on. At \(C = C_c\) the two rates are equal, the drift is exactly zero, and the filament gets nowhere at all. It is not resting. It is at its noisiest relative to its own progress: both doors running at full rate, every arrival undone by a departure, the length wandering while the mean stays put. A polymer sitting at its critical concentration is not a polymer at peace — it is one thrashing in place, which is why the next section can build a conveyor belt out of two ends that individually go nowhere.

Derive: why the variances add when the means subtract

Over a time \(t\) the tip gains some number of subunits and loses some other number. Both counts are Poisson, and — this is the whole derivation — they are independent: a monomer arriving from the bath knows nothing about a subunit falling off.

  1. Two independent Poisson counts. A Poisson variable has its variance equal to its mean:
    \(N_+ \sim \text{Poisson}(k_{on}Ct), \qquad N_- \sim \text{Poisson}(k_{off}t)\)
  2. The net change in subunits is their difference, so its mean is the difference of the means:
    \(\langle n\rangle = \langle N_+\rangle - \langle N_-\rangle = (k_{on}C - k_{off})\,t\)
  3. But variances of independent variables add — even across a minus sign, because \(\mathrm{Var}(-N_-) = (-1)^2\mathrm{Var}(N_-)\):
    \(\mathrm{Var}(n) = \mathrm{Var}(N_+) + \mathrm{Var}(N_-) = (k_{on}C + k_{off})\,t\)
    This is the step that surprises people, and it is the reason the sum shows up in a formula about a difference.
  4. Convert subunits to length with \(L = \delta n\). A variance scales with the square of the conversion:
    \(\mathrm{Var}(L) = \delta^2 (k_{on}C + k_{off})\,t\)
  5. Match it to the definition of a diffusion coefficient. Week 1 wrote a one-dimensional random walk as \(\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt\). Demand the same form here and read off \(D_L\):
    \(2D_Lt = \delta^2(k_{on}C + k_{off})t \quad\Rightarrow\quad D_L = \dfrac{\delta^2(k_{on}C + k_{off})}{2}\)
\(D_L = \delta^2(k_{on}C + k_{off})/2\). At \(C = 0.5\) µM on the barbed end that is \(2.7^2 \times (5.8 + 1.4)/2 = 26\) nm²/s — so in a minute the spread is \(\sqrt{2D_Lt} = \sqrt{2\times26\times60} \approx 56\) nm, about twenty subunits.

The factor of 2 is a convention, not physics: it is there so that \(D_L\) matches the \(D\) in Week 1's \(\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt\) rather than in \(\langle x^2\rangle = Dt\). Different books put it in different places. What is not a convention is that the spread grows as \(\sqrt{t}\) and that it is set by the sum of the rates.

Eq. 4.1.1 is a straight line in \(C\), and a straight line crosses zero. Below that concentration the tip loses subunits faster than it gains them; above it the filament grows without limit. The crossing is \(k_{off}/k_{on}\), which is \(1/K_{eq}\) (eq. 3.2.7) — Week 3's equilibrium constant wearing a new hat. For a receptor, \(1/K_{eq}\) was the concentration at which the door was half full. For a filament it is the concentration at which the door is fair:

eq. 4.1.3 — the critical concentration (eq. 3.2.7 in a new hat) $$C_c = \frac{k_{off}}{k_{on}} = \frac{1}{K_{eq}} \qquad (0.12\ \text{µM for ATP-actin at the barbed end})$$
A filament is a drunkard with a destination, and ATP is its watch. Its mean length obeys eq. 4.1.1 exactly and its fluctuations obey Week 1's random walk exactly. The nucleotide does not make the strand stronger — hydrolysis is not glue. It marks each subunit's age: ATP-actin binds fast and leaves slowly (the tip wears an ATP cap), ADP-actin leaves five times faster from the barbed end, and ADP-actin is the form cofilin binds a hundredfold more tightly (Carlier 1997). §1.2 spends the clock; §1.4 reads it.
Einstein–Smoluchowski at a filament tip: about one arrival in 150 sticks
  1. First, do not confuse two lengths. \(\delta = 2.7\) nm is the rise: how much longer the filament gets when one subunit joins. It is a lattice spacing, a property of the polymer. Stokes–Einstein does not want that — it wants the size of the molecule that is swimming, and a free G-actin monomer is bigger than the step it makes. Using \(\delta\) as a radius is a mistake that happens to be nearly harmless for actin, for a reason worth seeing.
  2. Size the monomer from its mass. G-actin is a compact globular protein of 42 kDa. A protein's volume is \(V = M\bar{v}/N_A\) with \(\bar{v} \approx 0.73\) cm³/g:
    \(V = \dfrac{42{,}000 \times 0.73}{6.022\times10^{23}}\ \text{cm}^3 = 51\ \text{nm}^3 \ \Rightarrow\ a_{min} = \left(\dfrac{3V}{4\pi}\right)^{1/3} = 2.3\ \text{nm}\)
    That is the radius of an anhydrous sphere. A real protein carries a hydration shell and is not a sphere, which costs about another 20 %: \(a_G \approx 2.8\) nm.
  3. Stokes–Einstein (Week 1), with the monomer's own radius:
    \(D = \dfrac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta a_G} = \dfrac{4.28\times10^{-21}}{6\pi \times 10^{-3} \times 2.8\times10^{-9}} \approx 81\ \text{µm}^2/\text{s}\)
  4. The encounter rate (eq. 1.5.1):
    \(k_s = 4\pi D a_G = 4\pi \times 81 \times 0.0028 = 2.85\ \text{µm}^3/\text{s} \times 602\ \text{µm}^{-3}\text{µM}^{-1} \approx 1{,}700\ \text{µM}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}\)

1.2 · Actin dynamics: two ends, two thresholds, and a conveyor belt

An actin filament has two ends and they are not the same. The subunits are asymmetric and stack head to tail, so one end presents one face of the monomer and the other end the opposite face. Pollard measured all four ATP-actin rate constants: the barbed end adds at 11.6 µM⁻¹s⁻¹ and loses at 1.4 s⁻¹; the pointed end at 1.3 and 0.8 — nine times slower to add but only half as slow to lose, so its threshold is higher. Between the two thresholds the filament does something a receptor never could: it grows at one end while shrinking at the other. Hold the monomer at the one concentration where the two rates match and the length stays fixed while every subunit marches from the barbed end to the pointed end — Wegner's treadmilling. The sim marks one subunit red so you can follow it.

Why does actin have these different behaviours at all? Because of the clock. A chemical reaction has one equilibrium constant, and adding a monomer is one reaction whichever end it happens at — so at equilibrium the two ends would have to agree about \(C_c\). They disagree fivefold. The subunit that leaves the pointed end is not the subunit that arrived at the barbed end: it has spent its nucleotide on the way. Switch to ADP-actin, which has nothing left to spend, and watch the window between the thresholds close.

  1. Below both thresholds (0.05 µM): both ends shrink. Above both (1 µM): both grow, the barbed end nine times faster. Read the open rings landing on the two end lines, and the filled dot on the total.
  2. Treadmilling at \(C_{ss}\) ≈ 0.17 µM: the length holds, the red subunit rides from + to −. Read the flux J and the transit time.
  3. ADP-actin: the two thresholds nearly coincide, J is essentially zero, and the red subunit never moves. No energy, no conveyor belt.
  4. Now take the rate constants off Pollard and set them yourself — the four sliders are the two doors at each end. Raise the pointed end's \(k_{on}\) to the barbed end's and the two critical concentrations converge: the shaded window narrows to nothing and the belt stops at every concentration. A filament treadmills only because its two ends are chemically different. Make them the same and you have a polymer that can grow or shrink but never flow.
  5. Then break it the other way: drop the barbed end's \(k_{on}\) below the pointed end's. The window inverts, the belt runs backwards, and the marked subunit rides from − to +. Nothing in eq. 4.1.4 forbids it — it is simply not the actin you have. Any chip puts the measured numbers back.

For \(M + F_n \rightleftharpoons F_{n+1}\) at equilibrium, \(K_{eq} = k_{on}/k_{off} = e^{-\Delta G^0/k_BT}\) is a property of the bond, so \(C_c^B = C_c^P\). 1.3 · Actin reinforcement: crosslinking, and why the jiggle matters

A single filament cannot hold a shape. Its persistence length — the distance over which thermal bending randomizes its direction — is 17.7 µm (Gittes, Mickey, Nettleton & Howard 1993), so a 3-µm stretch sags by a couple of hundred nanometres just from the bath. That sag is a nuisance for a structure and a gift for a builder: a crosslinker is a two-armed molecule that binds one filament and then waits for a second filament to come within reach, and it is the filaments' own jiggle that brings it.

The crosslinker in this sim is α-actinin, and its shape is the mechanism. It is a dumbbell — an antiparallel homodimer of two ≈100-kDa chains carrying one actin-binding domain at each end, 200 kDa in all (Ribeiro et al. 2014 solved the full-length dimer) — and electron microscopy puts it at 35–37 nm from tip to tip, 36 nm once it is bound to a filament (Meyer & Aebi 1990). That length is the sim's reach \(r_X\), and it is not a free parameter: 36 nm is also the crossover repeat of the two-stranded actin helix, so both ends of the molecule meet equivalent faces of the filament and the molecules can stack one behind another along it. Meyer & Aebi's control is the proof — the Dictyostelium protein measures 28 nm bound, misses the repeat, and can still tie filaments into a network but can no longer pack them into a bundle. A crosslinker's own length decides what kind of structure it is able to build. Filamin (longer, V-shaped, orthogonal networks) and fascin (short, tight parallel bundles) are the other two names worth knowing; the same logic sorts them.

Arp2/3 shares this section and does something else entirely. It is not a crosslinker; it is a nucleator. Sitting on the side of an existing filament it grows a new one out of it at 70 ± 7°, and it stays behind on the daughter's pointed end, capping it with nanomolar affinity (Mullins, Heuser & Pollard 1998). So the two molecules move different variables in the same network. Arp2/3 changes N — how many filaments there are, and which way they point. Every branch is a new barbed end aimed within 70° of its mother, so a network fed by Arp2/3 becomes a polar, forward-facing tree: the "dendritic brush" Svitkina & Borisy (1999) found filling the leading micron of a lamellipodium, the complex sitting at every Y-junction with the pointed ends behind it protected from coming apart. That structure pushes. α-actinin changes ℓ — the free length between pins. It nucleates nothing and adds no ends; the filament it binds is exactly as floppy as it was, with the same \(EI\) and the same \(L_p\). All it does is cut each filament into shorter free segments — and the sag law below (eq. 4.1.8) converts that into stiffness at no cost, because the sag falls as ℓ3/2. That structure holds shape, and, having no direction of its own, resists being deformed from any of them.

And the thing worth noticing is what the balance point does not give you: at \(k_{cap} = k_{br}\) the population neither grows nor decays on average, but a branching process held exactly at balance is critical, and a critical process reaches zero with probability one. A real lamellipodium is not autocatalytic: it is fed at the membrane, holding a roughly fixed number of ends open, which is the picture §1.4's belt runs on and why that brush neither explodes nor dies.

1.4 · Actin degradation: taking the network down, and the belt back to the front

In the rear of the cell the network comes down. Cofilin binds the ADP-actin that §1.1's clock has aged — a hundredfold more tightly than the fresh ATP form — and does two things: it severs the filament, making new ends, and it raises the pointed end's off-rate 25-fold (Carlier 1997).

Gelsolin does something different enough to deserve its own paragraph: it severs and then caps the barbed end it has just made. Cofilin makes new ends and lets them run; gelsolin makes new ends and shuts them. Sever-and-release accelerates turnover — more pointed ends shedding subunits, more barbed ends taking them up, the belt running faster. Sever-and-cap stops the network: every new barbed end is dead on arrival, so the filaments can only shorten from their pointed ends and the local mesh dissolves without any of it being rebuilt. A cell reaches for cofilin where it wants to recycle and gelsolin where it wants to demolish — and gelsolin answers to calcium, which is how a wound, or a signal, can take a region of cortex down in a single step. No rate constants are quoted for it here: this module has not read a primary measurement of them, and it will not put a number on the page that it cannot point at.

The freed monomer is ADP-actin, and ADP-actin cannot rejoin a barbed end at any useful rate; it must first exchange its nucleotide, which profilin makes fast, and profilin then hands the recharged monomer to a barbed end. The recharged monomer diffuses; because it was released at the rear, its concentration is highest there, and diffusion carries it toward the front where it is consumed. This is the conveyor belt closing.

The sim runs the whole loop rather than one half of it, because the point of a belt is that it is a loop. Four stations, marked on the cell:

  1. ① Grow. In the outer micron of the leading edge — §1.3's brush — Arp2/3 puts daughters on the sides of mothers at the same 70° you met there, and any ATP-monomer that wanders into the band is taken up by an open barbed end. Capping protein shuts those ends within a few seconds, which is why the filaments stay short and why Arp2/3 has to keep making new ones.
  2. ② Carried back. Every filament is dragged rearward at the retrograde flow speed (slider, 20–100 nm/s). A barbed end holds station at the membrane only while it is being fed fast enough to keep up; starve it and watch the brush fall away from the edge.
  3. ③ Taken down. Past the dashed line the filament has aged into ADP-actin, which is what cofilin binds, and its pointed end starts shedding. How far into the shaded zone it gets before it is gone is set by how long that takes — \(v_{retro}\,n/k_-^P\) — which is why the bare turnover chip leaves a crowd of filaments standing near the nucleus and the cofilin chip leaves almost none.

2.1 · The various types of myosin

There are dozens of myosins in a human cell and they share one motor domain — the head that binds actin and hydrolyses ATP — bolted to different levers and tails for different jobs. Myosin II, the muscle and non-muscle contractor, pairs tail to tail into bipolar minifilaments about 300 nm long with heads at both ends, and those pull actin networks together. Myosin V and VI are two-headed cargo carriers that walk along actin bundles, hand over hand, 37 nm a step. Myosin I links the membrane to the network beneath it; myosin X rides to the tips of filopodia. 2.2 · How a myosin walks: one foot planted, the other searching

Watch the motor first and read afterwards. Two heads — call them feet — hang from one hip. One foot is planted on the filament, and its lever has already swung. The other foot is free, and it is doing nothing but random-walking on its tether: back and forth, a few nanometres either way, hundreds of times a millisecond. Its tether is not centred on the planted foot, though — the planted foot's stroke has carried the hip forward, so the free foot's centre sits about 25 nm ahead of where the planted one stands, which is 11 nm short of the next binding zone and 61 nm past the previous one. So the search is biased: not because the free foot is pushed, but because of where its tether was left. When it wanders into the next zone it binds; the phosphate leaves and it strokes, the hip lurches forward, and only then does the rear foot release its ADP, take up an ATP and lift. The roles swap and the walk goes on, hand over hand, 36 nm per ATP.

The motor that does this is myosin V, the processive myosin — the one that carries a vesicle on its own, where myosin II (§2.1, §2.4) is off the track 95 % of the time and has to work in teams. Every number in that paragraph is measured. Mehta and colleagues showed in 1999 that single myosin V molecules take repeated ≈ 36-nm steps for one encounter with the filament, and Yildiz in 2003 that the centre of mass moves 37 nm per ATP with the two heads alternating — hand over hand, not an inchworm. De La Cruz found that a single myosin V head is strongly bound for more than 70 % of its cycle because ADP release, not binding, is the slow step. And Veigel found the split the sim draws: preferred binding zones every 36 nm along the filament, a working stroke of ≈ 25 nm by the bound head, and the last ≈ 11 nm covered by a biased, thermally driven diffusive movement of the free head, with a mechanical gate coordinating the two heads' cycles so that the rear foot does not let go early. Each head still runs Lymn and Taylor's five-state cycle from 1971 — rigor, ATP binds and it lifts, hydrolysis re-cocks the lever, weak binding, phosphate out and the power stroke, ADP out — the bar under the picture keeps that count.

Now the question the sim is built to answer. The detached head moves by diffusion — a random walk. The filament is polar (every subunit points the same way). Could the polarity alone bias the walk into a drift? It cannot: a random walk biased by a static asymmetry would be a perpetual-motion machine of the second kind — Feynman's ratchet and pawl, which the bath forbids. Something must be spent, and it is the ATP. Hydrolysis breaks the symmetry between binding and unbinding; the track's polarity tells the broken symmetry which way to point. Here that is concrete: the ATP-paid stroke of the planted foot is what parks the free foot's tether 11 nm short of the next zone, and the polarity is what makes every zone's stroke point the same way. 566 · Power stroke or Brownian ratchet? Both, and the thermodynamics that constrains either

Huxley's 1957 model treated the cross-bridge as a thermal spring attaching and detaching with position-dependent rates; the lever-arm picture (Rayment et al. 1993; Geeves & Holmes 1999) makes the stroke a conformational change driven by phosphate release. Astumian (1997) states the constraint any such machine obeys: a periodic, asymmetric potential plus a bath gives no net motion; drive the potential out of equilibrium — by binding and hydrolysing a fuel — and it does. The bias per cycle is at most the free energy spent, ≈ 20–25 kBT per ATP, which is where §2.3's force ceiling comes from. Myosin V is where the two pictures were shown to be one: Veigel et al. 2.3 · Power output of a single motor

Four questions about one motor, in order. How hard can it pull? How far does it move the filament each time? What stops it? And how much ATP does it need to keep going? We go back to myosin II for this — one head, the muscle motor — because its force is the best-measured of any (Finer, Simmons and Spudich held a single head in an optical trap in 1994 and read 3–4 pN and 11 nm), and because §2.4 is about what happens when you put sixty of them together. The geometry is Kron and Spudich's assay again: the head is fixed, the filament slides, and now a load pulls the filament back.

How far, and how hard. A strongly bound head is a spring — its stiffness \(\kappa\) is about 0.3 pN per nanometre — and the power stroke swings its lever by \(d = 11\) nm. With nothing pulling back, the filament moves the full 11 nm. With a load \(F\) pulling back, the head's own spring stretches by \(F/\kappa\) before the filament settles, so the filament gains only \(d - F/\kappa\). Watch the red zigzag in the sim: that is the stretch, and the ruler under the filament shows the three lengths every stroke — the 11 nm the lever swung, the \(F/\kappa\) the load took back, and what was left.

What stops it. When \(F/\kappa\) reaches \(d\) the gain is zero: the head cycles, burns ATP, and the filament does not move. That is the stall force, \(F_{stall} = \kappa d \approx 3.5\) pN — Finer's number, recovered from a spring constant and a step. Push past it and the load wins: the filament is dragged backward through the bound head, and a bond under force lets go sooner (Bell's law), so the head is torn off and the filament slips. Pull, stall, slip — three regimes, one spring.

What it costs. Each cycle needs one ATP, and ATP has to diffuse to the head and bind before the cycle can start again. That is Week 3's enzyme with ATP as the substrate — Michaelis–Menten, eq. 3.2.15 again: Kron and Spudich found the sliding speed half-maximal at 50 µM ATP. A cell runs at millimolar, so the motor is saturated and its speed is set by the cycle, not the fuel. The slider lets you starve it and watch the curve.

  1. Slow the clock until you can see one stroke land. The lever swings 11 nm — the green brace — and the red zigzag stretches as the load takes \(F/\kappa\) of it back; the blue brace is what the filament kept. Read the gain per stroke stat: measured against \(d - F/\kappa\).
  2. Pull at 1.5 pN: the work the filament actually gets is \(F(d - F/\kappa)\). Read that against the ≈ 25 \(k_BT\) an ATP releases — about 9 %, not the ≈ 40 % that \(Fd\) at stall suggests — and read the power in attowatts.

  4. Slip at 6 pN: the filament goes backward. Then drop ATP to 25 µM at 1.5 pN and read the power — a third of the saturated value, exactly as \(K_M = 50\) µM predicts.

A 5-µm actin filament moving through water has \(\zeta \approx 3\times10^{-5}\) pN·s/nm, so at this section's 120 nm/s the drag is \(0.004\) pN, and even at Kron and Spudich's 4 µm/s gliding speed it is \(0.12\) pN — a thirtieth of the head's stall force. That is why the gliding assay runs at the unloaded speed \(v_0\): in water a filament is effectively free, and the load slider here is the trap, not the bath. Inside a cell the filament is threaded through a crosslinked network, and the drag of pulling actin past α-actinin, past other filaments and past the membrane is thousands of times larger — large enough to be the whole load, and to hold retrograde flow at 20–100 nm/s against a team of motors that would slide free actin at microns per second. §2.4 puts exactly that drag into its engine as the thing the team pulls against, and next week's clutch is what sets its value. Hold the distinction: \(F\) below is a steady pull-back, whatever supplies it; in a cell it is drag, and drag is set by the network.

1 · Start from the fuel. Every stroke is paid for by one ATP, and hydrolysing it releases \(\Delta\mu_{ATP} \approx 25\,k_BT \approx 107\) pN·nm. A stroke that moves the filament a distance \(d\) against a steady force \(F\) does work \(F d\) at most — and no machine can do more work than the energy it was handed. So there is a force that no motor with an 11-nm stroke could ever hold against, whatever it is made of:

eq. 4.2.2 — the force ceiling from the fuel $$F_{step}\,d \le \Delta\mu_{ATP} \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{step} \lesssim \frac{\Delta\mu_{ATP}}{d} \approx \frac{25\,k_BT}{11\ \text{nm}} \approx 10\ \text{pN}$$

This is a stall force defined by an energy balance: the load at which one ATP's worth of work is exactly used up moving the filament one step. Hold the number, 10 pN.

2 · Work per stroke, over time per stroke. Power is work divided by the time it takes, and the motor takes one cycle per stroke:

eq. 4.2.3 — the power of one head $$P = \frac{F\,d}{t_{cycle}} = F\,v$$

The second form is the one to remember — force times speed — because it is what the sim's right-hand chart plots, and it says the power is zero at both ends: at no load there is nothing to push against, at stall nothing moves.

3 · What sets the cycle time. The head cannot start a new cycle until an ATP has diffused to it and bound. That is Week 3's enzyme with ATP as the substrate, and its rate saturates exactly as Michaelis–Menten says it must:

eq. 3.2.15 again — the cycle rate's ATP dependence, with ATP as the substrate $$k_{cycle} = k_{max}\,\frac{[\mathrm{ATP}]}{K_M + [\mathrm{ATP}]}, \qquad K_M \approx 50\ \text{µM}$$

Kron and Spudich's half-maximal sliding at 50 µM is where \(K_M\) comes from; a cell's millimolar ATP puts it twenty times past that, saturated.

4 · Now the mechanics — and the second stall. Finer's head held 3–4 pN, not 10. To see why, stop treating the head as a rigid lever and treat it as what it is: a spring. Strongly bound, it has stiffness \(\kappa\), and the power stroke moves the spring's unloaded position — where it would put the filament if nothing pulled back — forward by \(d\). The load \(F\) pulls the filament back until the spring's pull matches it. Write that balance:

Derive: the force balance on a bound head, and where the stall comes from

Let the head bind with the filament at position \(b\) and the spring unstretched. The stroke moves the spring's zero to \(b + d\). With the filament at \(x_f\), the head pulls it forward with \(\kappa(b + d - x_f)\); the load pulls it back with \(F\).

  1. Force balance (the filament settles where the two agree):
    \(\kappa(b + d - x_f) = F \;\Rightarrow\; x_f = b + d - F/\kappa\)
  2. Gain per stroke:
    \(\Delta x = x_f - b = d - F/\kappa\)
    — the lever swung the full \(d\), and the head's own compliance gave \(F/\kappa\) of it back. This is the red zigzag and the blue brace in the sim.
  3. Stall is the load at which the gain is zero:
    \(d - F/\kappa = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{stall} = \kappa d\)
    With \(\kappa \approx 0.32\) pN/nm and \(d = 11\) nm, \(F_{stall} \approx 3.5\) pN.
  4. Speed is gain per stroke times strokes per second:
    \(v = (d - F/\kappa)\,k_{cycle} = d\,k_{cycle}\left(1 - \dfrac{F}{\kappa d}\right) = v_0\left(1 - \dfrac{F}{F_{stall}}\right)\)
  5. Work the filament actually receives per stroke:
    \(W_{delivered} = F\,\Delta x = F\left(d - F/\kappa\right)\)
    zero at stall; the lever's \(F d\) of step 1 was a ceiling, not a delivery.
\(F_{stall} = \kappa d\): a stall force defined by a force balance, not an energy balance — the load at which this particular spring's stroke gives nothing back.
\(\kappa\) is not measured independently here. It is Finer's force divided by Finer's step, \(3.5/11 = 0.32\) pN/nm, so the sim reproducing 3.5 pN is a consistency check, not a prediction. Single-head stiffness has been measured (Veigel 1998, Molloy 1995) and lands within a factor of two or three of this; the module quotes none of those numbers until it has read them.
eq. 4.2.4 — the load line $$v = v_0\left(1 - \frac{F}{F_{stall}}\right), \qquad F_{stall} = \kappa d \approx 3.5\ \text{pN}, \quad v_0 = d\,k_{cycle}$$

Two stalls, one gap. The energy balance said 10 pN; the force balance says 3.5. Both are right, and they are answers to different questions. Ten piconewtons is what the fuel could support if every joule went into the step. Three and a half is what this spring can hold before its stroke gives everything back. The factor of three between them is where the ATP's energy actually goes: at stall the lever still swings, the spring stores \(\tfrac12\kappa d^2 \approx 19\) pN·nm of it, and when the head lets go that energy is released as heat — the filament never saw it. Under a working load the filament gets \(F(d - F/\kappa)\), which peaks at half the stall force and is about 9 % of \(\Delta\mu\) there; the rest is the spring's storage and the cycle's own cost. That 9 % is the sim's efficiency readout, and it is why a muscle holding a weight still burns fuel.

5 · Past stall. For \(F > \kappa d\) the balance point \(b + d - F/\kappa\) sits behind where the head bound: the load drags the filament backward through the bound head, and the bond itself is now carrying \(F\). A bond under force does not fail at a threshold; it fails sooner, exponentially in the force, because the load tilts the energy landscape the bond has to climb out of:

eq. 4.2.5 — a bond under force lets go sooner (Bell) $$k_{off}(f) = k_0\,e^{f/f_\beta}$$

\(f_\beta = k_BT/x_\beta\) is the force that tilts the barrier by one \(k_BT\) over the bond's own length \(x_\beta\); the sim uses 2.5 pN. At 6 pN the off-rate is up elevenfold, the head is torn off before it can finish its cycle, and the filament slips — the third regime. The load line is a straight-line stand-in for a measured curve — Hill's 1938 hyperbola arrives in the muscle weeks — and it is exactly what one elastic head does. The Bell law is minted here because slip is where you first see it; next week's clutch reuses the number for a different bond.

2.4 · Tug of war: force adds, speed does not

A bipolar minifilament has heads at both ends walking toward the barbed ends of two anti-parallel filaments, so it pulls the two filaments toward each other — the sliding-filament geometry of muscle, in miniature, about thirty molecules and sixty heads long. Sixty heads on one filament share the load. At any instant only about \(N r\) of them are attached — three, for sixty heads at 5 % — so the time-averaged force is \(N r F_{step}\), about 10 pN, and it grows linearly with \(N\). The speed does not: once enough heads are present that one is always attached, the filament slides at the speed one attached head lets it, \(d/t_{attached}\). Uyeda, Kron and Spudich measured exactly this in 1990 and 1991 — filaments over a few heads move in bursts at integer multiples of a unit speed; over many heads at a common maximum. F25's lecture opened with this image: polymerization pushing forward, motors pulling back, and clutches deciding who wins. The clutch is next week.

  1. One head: bursts of motion at \(d\) per cycle, ≈ 0.2 µm/s on average, long pauses between. Load it with 4 pN and it stalls.
  2. Twenty heads: one is attached almost all the time; continuous sliding near \(d/t_{attached}\), several µm/s. Read the mean attached count against \(N r\).
  3. Sixty: the same speed, three times the force. Now load them — the spring above the upper filament is drawn slack at zero load and pulls only when you move the second slider. Derive: why the bracket is 1 − (1 − r)^N

The filament moves only while at least one head is attached and pulling; when none is attached it sits still (in this geometry there is nothing else to move it). So its average speed is the speed it has while driven, times the fraction of time it is driven.

  1. One head, at any instant. It is attached with probability \(r\) — that is what the duty ratio means (eq. 4.2.1) — and detached with probability \(1 - r\).
  2. N independent heads. All detached at once has probability
    \(P(\text{none attached}) = (1 - r)^N\)
  3. So the driven fraction is
    \(P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - (1 - r)^N\)
  4. The driven speed. An attached head advances the filament by \(d\) during its attachment, which lasts \(t_{attached} = r\,t_{cycle}\):
    \(v_0 = \dfrac{d}{t_{attached}} = \dfrac{d}{r\,t_{cycle}}\)
  5. Multiply. Note the two limits: at \(N = 1\) the speed is \(v_0 r = d/t_{cycle}\) — one stroke per cycle, the single-head result of §2.2 — and at large \(N\) it saturates at \(v_0\).
\(v = v_0[1 - (1 - r)^N]\) with \(v_0 = d/(r\,t_{cycle}) = 11\ \text{nm}/2.5\ \text{ms} \approx 4\) µm/s: 0.2 µm/s at \(N = 1\), 2.6 at \(N = 20\), 3.8 at \(N = 60\).

2.5 · Reinforcement and rearrangement: a network that gathers itself

Put minifilaments into a layer of actin and let them pull. Each pulls two filaments' barbed ends toward itself; filaments slide, rotate, and pile up; the piles pull in more. Das, Bhat, Sknepnek, Köster, Mayor and Rao (2020) simulated exactly this with coarse-grained Brownian dynamics and reconstituted it on a lipid bilayer, and found a subtlety: if actin and myosin are confined to one plane they block each other, and the clusters that form are elongated and striped — myosin on one side, actin on the other. Let the myosin ride in a layer just above the actin (stratified, as super-resolution microscopy showed it does — the two intensity peaks sit about 400 nm apart) and round asters form with myosin cores and barbed ends pointing in. Longer filaments make connected asters, then bundles. The sim is a reduction of their model: 48 rigid polar filaments, minifilaments that bind, pull and walk, soft steric repulsion, and a chip that turns the stratification on and off.

  1. Stratified, 3-µm filaments: watch asters form. Give it a minute (the clock runs at ×5, so a minute of model time takes twelve seconds) — the white 1-σ ellipses round up to about 1.7 and the fraction of barbed ends pointing at the core reaches 100 %.
  2. Strict 2D: the same 48 rods from the same seed, one steric term switched. Over the first minute the ellipses stay markedly more elongated — about 2.2 at 30 s and 2.6 at 60 s against the stratified run's 1.7 and 1.9 — and fewer barbed ends point at a core. Steric hindrance is a real force. Run both much longer and the difference washes out: with only 48 filaments in a 12 × 9 µm box the clusters coarsen to two either way, which is the reduction talking, not the paper.
  3. Lengthen the filaments to 9 µm: the asters stop being asters. One large cluster replaces several, and the barbed ends stop pointing at its core — the inward fraction collapses from 100 % to about 10 %. Compare where you are with the paper's phase diagram on the right.

The third filament is built the same way and behaves like nothing else in the cell. A single microtubule spans a cell as a straight rod. And its GTP is not a clock but a switch: lose the cap and the whole end falls apart at hundreds of dimers a second, then regrows somewhere else. 3.1 · Why a cell needs microtubules at all — and why they have to be tubes

Start with an experiment that seems to make this tab unnecessary. In 1984 Euteneuer and Schliwa cut fragments off fish keratocytes — pieces of lamellipodium with no nucleus, no centrosome and no microtubules — and the fragments crawled: fast, straight, for as long as they were watched. Everything Tabs 1 and 2 built is in that fragment. Actin polymerizes at the front and pushes; myosin II contracts the rear and pulls; the belt of §1.4 returns the monomer. The engine is complete without a single microtubule. So what is the rest of this tab for?

For the three things an engine cannot do for itself. It cannot remember which way it was going. The actin network turns over in tens of seconds and forgets its own shape; a fragment keeps a heading only as long as nothing perturbs it. A whole cell keeps a heading for minutes, turns deliberately, and holds its rear as the rear — and the thing in it that lasts minutes and spans its whole length is a microtubule. Its ends are wired to the engine's controls: a plus end growing toward the edge activates Rac there and the front protrudes (Waterman-Storer 1999); a shrinking microtubule releases GEF-H1, which activates Rho, and that region contracts (Krendel 2002). Take the array away with nocodazole and a neutrophil does not stop — it becomes more contractile, its pseudopods flatten, and its path turns circuitous (Xu 2005): the engine runs, and nothing is holding the steering. It cannot carry. A vesicle diffusing across a 10-µm cell takes minutes and arrives nowhere in particular; §3.4's Péclet number says when that stops being acceptable, and the answer is a road with a motor on it — kinesin outward, dynein inward, each with a duty ratio near one. A road that wriggled like actin would be no road; the microtubule spans the cell as a straight rod. And it cannot be re-aimed. A compass that pointed forever would be useless the moment the signal moved; §3.2's dynamic instability is what lets the array fall apart and regrow toward the new front.

Each of those jobs sets a mechanical requirement, and every one of them is met by the same choice of architecture: not a thread but a tube. A tube 25 nm across, built from the same kind of subunit as actin by the same door (eq. 4.1.1 with tubulin's rates), has a persistence length of five millimetres — three hundred times actin's — so one of them spans a cell without bending, points, and can be walked. That number is what this section measures, and the measurement is the one Gittes, Mickey, Nettleton and Howard made in 1993 without touching a filament: they filmed the thermal wriggling of microtubules and actin and decomposed the shapes into bending modes. Equipartition — Week 1's Boltzmann, eq. 1.2.7, half a \(k_BT\) per mode — says how much each mode should wriggle for a given flexural rigidity \(EI\), so the wriggle measures \(EI\). They found \(2.2\times10^{-23}\) N·m² for a microtubule and \(7.3\times10^{-26}\) for actin: persistence lengths (eq. 4.1.7) of 5.2 mm and 17.7 µm. 3.2 · Dynamic instability

Actin treadmills; microtubules do something stranger. A growing plus end carries a cap of GTP-tubulin; behind the tip the GTP is hydrolysed, and GDP-tubulin in the lattice wants to peel apart. While growth outruns hydrolysis the cap holds. Lose it and the end switches abruptly to rapid shortening — Walker measured 733 dimers a second, 27 µm/min — until a rescue re-caps it. Mitchison and Kirschner saw growing and shrinking populations coexisting in one bath in 1984 and named it dynamic instability. Four numbers describe it: the growth speed, the shortening speed, and the two switching frequencies, catastrophe and rescue; Dogterom and Leibler showed in 1993 that they decide whether the lengths settle to a steady distribution or grow without bound. Why would a cell build a polymer that keeps falling apart? Because a rod that shrinks and regrows can search — §3.3.

  1. Bounded at 10 µM: watch one tracked microtubule saw-tooth and the length histogram settle. Read the mean against \(v_g v_s/(v_s f_{cat} - v_g f_{res})\).
  2. Unbounded: raise tubulin and tip the MAP balance toward stabilizing — the denominator crosses zero and the array grows until the cortex stops it.
  3. No rescue: every catastrophe runs to zero; short lengths pile up and the mean is \(v_g/f_{cat}\). Then push the MAP slider negative (a stathmin-like destabilizer) and watch the array collapse.

The denominator is a competition: shortening times catastrophe against growth times rescue. Tubulin concentration raises \(v_g\) and lowers \(f_{cat}\) (Walker saw catastrophes grow rarer with concentration); MAPs lower \(f_{cat}\) and raise \(f_{res}\); destabilizers do the reverse. A cell tunes the denominator, and with it the length of every microtubule it owns.

Derive: the mean length, from a balance of what grows and what shrinks

Track a large population. At any moment a fraction \(p_g\) of the ends is growing and \(p_s = 1 - p_g\) is shrinking, and each end contributes its own speed to the population's mean length.

  1. The switching is a two-state balance. In steady state the flux from growing to shrinking equals the flux back:
    \(p_g f_{cat} = p_s f_{res} \quad\Rightarrow\quad p_g = \dfrac{f_{res}}{f_{cat} + f_{res}}\)
  2. The mean rate of change of length is the population average:
    \(\left\langle \dfrac{dL}{dt}\right\rangle = p_g v_g - p_s v_s = \dfrac{v_g f_{res} - v_s f_{cat}}{f_{cat} + f_{res}}\)
  3. Read the sign. If \(v_g f_{res} > v_s f_{cat}\) the mean length grows without bound — the unbounded regime. Otherwise every end drifts toward zero and the lengths reach a steady distribution.
  4. The distribution. In the bounded regime the length distribution is exponential, and solving the two coupled equations for the growing and shrinking populations gives its mean:
    \(\langle L\rangle = \dfrac{v_g v_s}{v_s f_{cat} - v_g f_{res}}\)
  5. Check a limit. With no rescue (\(f_{res} = 0\)): \(\langle L\rangle = v_g/f_{cat}\) — simply how far an end grows before its first catastrophe.
\(\langle L\rangle = v_gv_s/(v_sf_{cat} - v_gf_{res})\), 2.9 µm for the sim's default settings; the boundary between bounded and unbounded is the vanishing of the denominator.

3.3 · Search and capture — where the microtubules point

The minus ends are gathered at the centrosome, so the array is radial: a few hundred rods growing out from one point, each probing toward the edge and shrinking back when it finds nothing. Kirschner and Mitchison called this search and capture in 1986 — a plus end that touches a target (a kinetochore, a cortical site, an adhesion) is stabilized while the rest keep searching. Holy and Leibler (1994) calculated the mean time to find a target and showed that dynamic instability finds it orders of magnitude faster than a polymer that only grows: a rod that never shrinks aims once, and if it misses it is wasted forever. Why growth only fails

3.4 · The motors on the tube — kinesin, dynein, and when a road pays

Microtubules are also roads. Kinesin walks toward the plus end, dynein toward the minus end, each carrying a vesicle or an organelle. Kinesin is the motor that let physicists watch a single stepping machine: Svoboda and colleagues resolved its 8-nm steps in 1993, Schnitzer and Block showed one ATP per step and about a hundred steps before it lets go, and Visscher's force clamp found a stall force near 6 pN that itself depends on ATP, with load raising the apparent Michaelis constant — the enzyme's kinetics coupled to its mechanics. Its duty ratio is near one, so it walks alone where myosin II needed a team (Tab 2 §2.4). Why does a cell bother with motors when diffusion is free? Because over a cell length diffusion is slow for anything bigger than a protein. The Péclet number (eq. 4.1.9) says when the road pays, and this sim races the two.

What is in the vesicle — and what is not. The cargo on these roads is whatever cannot get there on its own. Luby-Phelps and colleagues measured this directly in 1987: inert particles diffuse through cytoplasm more slowly the larger they are, and above a radius of about 26 nm they stop diffusing at all on any useful timescale — "most endosomes, polyribosomes, and the larger multienzyme complexes", in their words. So the vesicles kinesin carries toward the front are membrane for an edge that is growing, the receptors and adhesion proteins that go in it, and secretory cargo; dynein brings endosomes back; mitochondria and RNA granules ride as well. What is not in a vesicle is G-actin. It could not be — a vesicle's contents are inside a lipid bag and actin lives in the cytosol — and, more to the point, it need not be. A 42-kDa monomer diffuses at 3–14 µm²/s in cytoplasm (McGrath 1998; Kiuchi 2011) and crosses a 10-µm cell in about eight seconds; kinesin would take twelve, and would need the monomer loaded, carried and released. The Péclet number for the monomer over the cell is about 1: the road buys nothing, and it costs ATP. That is why §1.4's belt runs on diffusion. It is worth asking, then, what a cell does ship to the front on actin's behalf — and the answer, since Lawrence and Singer found it in 1986, is the message. β-actin mRNA is concentrated up to sixteenfold in the lamellipodia over the region near the nucleus, held there by a 54-nucleotide "zipcode" in its 3′ untranslated region (Kislauskis, Zhu & Singer 1994); a polyribosome is exactly the kind of particle Luby-Phelps found cannot diffuse, and a few thousand copies are far too few to spread by chance. Delocalize the mRNA with an oligonucleotide against the zipcode and the cell's lamellipodia deteriorate and it travels less far (Kislauskis 1997). The monomer diffuses; the recipe rides; the actin is made where it is used.

566 · Duty ratio, processivity, and the two families

3.5 · All together — the compass

Microtubules do not power migration. Euteneuer and Schliwa showed in 1984 that fish keratocytes — and even their nucleus-free fragments — crawl fast and straight with no microtubules at all. But in most cells they bias it. In fibroblasts the array reaches toward the front and a growing plus end touching the edge activates Rac there (Waterman-Storer 1999: protrusion); a depolymerizing microtubule releases GEF-H1, which activates Rho (Krendel 2002: contraction). In neutrophils the array is swept behind the nucleus into the tail (Eddy 2002), and taking it away with nocodazole makes the cell contract more, protrude less, and take circuitous paths (Xu 2005) — microtubules hold the backness down. Actin decides in seconds; microtubules decide in minutes; the slow system is the compass, the fast one the motor. The sim is Tab 1's cell in a uniform bath, self-polarizing from its own flicker, walking a persistent random walk — Week 1's MSD law applied to a whole cell — with the array switchable and its role chosen by chip.

4.2 · From a reading to a compass — the second messengers

Week 3 ended with the receptors at the front a few percent more occupied than at the back, and a floor on how well that difference can be known. The cell turns the shallow external difference into a steep internal one. Receptor occupancy activates a G protein; PI3K makes PIP3 at the front and PTEN removes it at the back; PIP3 activates Rac, and Rac feeds back on PI3K — Xu and colleagues called this assembly frontness. A second branch of the same receptors drives Rho, its kinase and myosin II — backness — and the two assemblies exclude each other. The map is steeper than the gradient (Servant 2000 saw the PIP3 reporter "much steeper than that of the chemoattractant"), it forms even in a uniform bath, and it flips when the gradient flips. But the counting floor still stands. This section pays Week 3's promise.

The sim runs the signalling layer alone — the cell does not move — with the wiring of Levchenko and Iglesias: each patch's excitation follows its own reading quickly, one inhibitor follows the whole cell's mean reading slowly, and the response is their difference. Frontness is a saturating function of the positive response with a little local positive feedback; backness is the mirror.

  1. Gradient: read the two bars at the right of the second canvas — outside ≈ 10 %, inside ≈ 70 %, so the map is tens of times steeper. Then read the pair below them: the reading's signal-to-noise is the larger of the two, and the gain slider moves neither.
  2. Uniform bath: the external ratio is exactly 1 — and a front forms anyway. Its direction is chosen by the flicker; the gradient's only job was to choose it instead.
  3. Source flips at 60 s: the reading changes sign within a receptor relaxation time (watch the orange trace cross zero); the map holds its old front for minutes afterwards. eq. 4.4.1 — local excitation, global inhibition $$\tau_e\,\frac{de_i}{dt} = \hat\theta_i - e_i, \qquad \tau_m\,\frac{dm}{dt} = \bar{\hat\theta} - m, \qquad r_i = e_i - m$$

Each patch \(i\) compares its own reading \(\hat\theta_i\) (fast, \(\tau_e\)) with the cell's running average (slow, \(\tau_m\)). A uniform rise in concentration raises every \(e_i\) and then \(m\), and the response fades: adaptation. The change detector: what the inhibitor does to a step and to a reversal
  1. Write what you know. \(\tau_m = 60\) s; a uniform step raises every reading by 30 % at \(t = 0\).
  2. The inhibitor follows (eq. 4.4.1): \(m(t) = m_0 + 0.3\,m_0(1 - e^{-t/\tau_m})\), so the response
    \(r(t) = 0.3\,m_0\,e^{-t/\tau_m}\)
    is largest at once and gone after a few memory times — every patch flares, then adapts.
  3. A reversal swaps which patches exceed \(m\); the old front's \(r_i\) turns negative within \(\tau_e\), but the new front cannot win until the map's own global inhibitor (the fading old front) releases it — of order \(\tau_a\) to \(\tau_m\).
A step is answered and forgotten in about a minute; a reversal is a U-turn, not a reset, on the same clock. The uniform-bath front is this machinery running on noise.
566 · Why a global inhibitor, and why a little feedback

Levchenko and Iglesias (2002) showed that the pair in eq. 4.4.1 gives perfect adaptation to a uniform stimulus (the steady response to any level is zero) while a persistent gradient gives a persistent, spatially fixed response — the two properties Parent and Devreotes (1999) saw in Dictyostelium and neutrophils. Weiner et al. (2002) added the positive feedback (PIP3 promotes more PIP3 through Rac and PI3K), which is what lets a front form from noise in a uniform bath and lock in; Xu et al. (2003) showed frontness and backness excluding each other, which is what keeps the front a front. The model here is that wiring with one activator and one inhibitor; the real cascade has dozens of species, and this module claims only its logic.

  3. Now notice what the dashboard does not vary. \(\varepsilon\) is held at 5 % in that calculation, but a real 5 % front–back difference exists only because the cell is 15 µm across. The difference a body of size \(2a\) can see in a gradient of decay length \(\lambda\) scales as \(2a/\lambda\), so shrinking the cell fifteen-fold shrinks \(\varepsilon\) fifteen-fold too — and \(\varepsilon^2\) by 225:
    \(53 \times 225 \approx 1.2\times10^4\)
    Seconds become hours. The dashboard cannot show you this term because it has no gradient in it; the cell radius slider changes the counting, not the signal. Hold both effects in your head and the conclusion is not "a bacterium is slower at this" but "a bacterium cannot do this at all" — which is why it swims and compares now with a moment ago instead. Week 3's temporal sensing, arriving as a corollary.
  4. Supply — Da (eq. 4.1.10): the supply path across a half-micron cell is a fraction of a micron, so \(\mathrm{Da}_{edge} = k_{on}\rho_B\ell/D_G\) collapses and the edge is never starved. Diffusion wins trivially at this size.
  5. Stiffness (eq. 4.1.7):
    \(\dfrac{2a}{L_p} = \dfrac{1}{17.7} \approx 0.06\)
    A filament spanning this cell is a rod. It needs no network to hold a shape — which is roughly why a bacterium's cytoskeleton has a different job from yours.
  6. Read the pattern. The two numbers that got easier (supply, stiffness) are the ones that scale with size. The one that got catastrophically harder is the one that scales with counting. Smallness is cheap mechanically and ruinous informationally.
Da collapses, \(2a/L_p\) falls to 0.06 and a filament becomes a beam — while the counting time rises by 53 on the dashboard's own terms, and by four orders of magnitude once you remember that a small body also sees a smaller difference. A bacterium is not a small neutrophil: it is a cell that had to give up spatial sensing and buy direction with time instead. Every scaling argument of Weeks 1–3 is on this one screen, and one of them is deliberately off it.
  • Every filament is Week 3's door repeated: a subunit arrives at \(k_{on}C\), leaves at \(k_{off}\), and builds the next site. Actin, tubulin and the motors' sites obey the same grammar at different rates and cross-sections.
  • Actin's ATP is a clock (it ages subunits so the cell knows what to take down); tubulin's GTP is a switch (it makes an end bistable); myosin's ATP is the bias (thermal motion searches, hydrolysis pays for the direction).
  • Force adds across motors, speed does not; polymerization sets a cell's speed, motors set its forces, microtubules set where it points and how long it keeps pointing there.
  • A steep internal map is no better known than the shallow reading it came from (eq. 4.4.2): the cell decides faster and harder, not better.
  • Five dimensionless numbers — Da, Pe, \(C_f/C_c\), \(Nr\), \(2a/L_p\) — and one ratio of clocks say what a cell of any size can and cannot do.

The ledger as a calculation: what each missing piece would cost
  1. The rule. An honest omission names the number it would change, the direction it would move it, and the size of the move. Anything vaguer is an apology, not a ledger. Take the four that matter most.
  2. Adhesion and the clutch
    \(v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_{retro}\)
    On a stiff, well-adhered floor the clutch engages, \(v_{retro}\) falls toward zero and \(v_{edge}\) rises toward \(v_{pol} \approx 250\) nm/s; on a slippery one the network slips, \(v_{retro}\) rises toward 250 and the edge stalls. The whole 150–200 nm/s answer of §4.3 lives inside that swing, which is why next week is a whole week.
  3. Membrane tension as the load on the tips
    \(v = \delta\!\left(k_{on}C\,e^{-F\delta/k_BT} - k_{off}\right)\)
    At a stall force of 7 pN per tip (Tab 5), a tension that loads each tip with just 2 pN costs a factor \(e^{-2 \times 2.7/4.28} = 0.28\) on the arrival term — the edge speed falls by roughly three quarters. This is the single largest number the module leaves out.
  4. The nucleus as an obstacle
    \(\text{no term at all}\)
    In this model the nucleus is drawn and never pushed. In a 3-µm pore Lämmermann's leukocytes cannot pass without myosin II — so the "no myosin II" chip, which here only fails to retract the rear, would in confinement stop the cell dead.
  5. A real signalling network
    \(\text{one activator, one inhibitor}\)
    Gain and adaptation survive; what is lost is everything the module cannot test — multiple timescales, crosstalk, and the fact that a real cell can be biochemically polarized before it moves at all.
  6. Sum it honestly. Three of the four push the edge speed down and only the clutch can push it up. A model that reproduces the measured 10 µm/min while omitting three retarding forces is getting the right answer partly by luck, and you should say so before you quote it.
The clutch swings the edge speed over its whole range; membrane tension could cost three quarters of it; confinement makes myosin II necessary rather than merely useful; the cascade changes none of the physics. A ledger with numbers in it is a research plan. A ledger without them is a disclaimer.

The provocation: if integrins are dispensable, what are they for?
  1. The experiment. Lämmermann and colleagues (2008) deleted every integrin from mouse leukocytes — all of them, not one family — and the cells still migrated through three-dimensional tissue at full speed. Myosin II mattered only for squeezing the nucleus through narrow gaps.
  2. Take the result seriously. Everything this module built — tips that grow, networks that contract, a rear that follows — needs no adhesion in 3D. The actin network expands, the tissue holds the cell by geometry, and the cell flows forward. Adhesion is not what makes a cell move.
  3. So where does adhesion enter?
    \(v_{edge} = v_{pol} - v_{retro}\)
    Through the second term only. Without a clutch, the network slides backward as fast as it grows forward and the edge goes nowhere on a flat surface, because a flat surface gives nothing to push against. In a tissue, the surroundings do that job for free.
  4. Make the prediction. Then integrin-free migration should fail exactly where confinement fails: on 2D glass. That is what they found — and it is why the clutch, not the motor, is what makes substrate stiffness matter at all.
  5. The question to carry. If a cell can move without adhering, what is a cell buying when it does adhere? (Answer next week: a way to convert its own contraction into traction on the outside world, and with it the ability to read the mechanics of what it is standing on.)
Integrins are not what makes a cell move; they are what lets a surface push back. Every mechanical measurement this course makes next week — traction force microscopy, the motor–clutch, durotaxis — rests on that distinction, and Lämmermann's knockout is the experiment that forces you to make it.
Note from Dr. McGhee. Notice what this equation is not: it is not a motor. The filament does not shove the membrane; it fills the space the bath opens, and the ATP it spends (Tab 1's clock) is what keeps \(C\) above \(C_c\) so that the filling is one-way. Feynman's ratchet and pawl said thermal motion alone cannot do work — and it cannot; the polymerization is the energy source, the flicker is the opportunity.
