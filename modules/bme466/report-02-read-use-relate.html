Three papers, one way of reading

Lecture's numbers — the constants the relations need

  • Pressure1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa. The card's pressures and the paper's are in mmHg; eq. 8.2.1 gives pascals.
  • van 't Hoff\(R\) = 8.314 J/(mol·K); body temperature 310 K; albumin 66 348 g/mol, so 1 g/L is 0.01507 mol/m³. Lecture's check: 40 g/L gives 1 550 Pa = 11.7 mmHg, against a measured 26.9 mmHg for a healthy plasma (Noddeland 1982) — albumin alone falls nearly half short, because the other proteins pull too, albumin's charge holds extra small ions on its side, and plasma is not an ideal dilute solution.
  • The leg's tissue side\(P_t = -0.4\) mmHg, \(\Pi_t = 10.8\) mmHg (Noddeland 1982), \(f_r = 0.9\) for albumin (Rippe & Haraldsson 1986): a healthy leg's, printed on the card for both groups.
  • Bloodviscosity 3–4 mPa·s; yield stress 1.5–5 mPa at a haematocrit of 0.45 (Merrill 1963, as quoted by Fedosov 2011); density 1 060 kg/m³. 1 mPa·s × 1 s⁻¹ = 1 mPa.
  • Straina fraction in every relation: 10 % is 0.10. The stresses in the paper are kPa; the moduli come out in kPa.

When each relation applies, and when it fails

  • Starling (eq. 8.2.2)Any wall at its four pressures; the bracket is the push and \(L_p\) only scales it. The push changes along a capillary with \(P_c\), so say where you took it. Fails when: the pull is taken from the bulk tissue — lecture's ledger overstates steady filtration ten to forty times because the pull that acts is set under the glycocalyx; a difference of two pushes, or a ratio, survives that better than either push alone.
  • van 't Hoff (eq. 8.2.1)Dilute, ideal solutions; only the molecules the wall holds back count. Fails when: at plasma's crowding and charge — use it for the order of magnitude and for the direction, and for what a change in albumin does.
  • The parabola, its mean and the wall shear rate (eqs 7.1.2, 7.1.3, 7.3.6)Steady, developed, laminar flow of one fluid in a straight rigid tube: the mean is half the centreline speed and the wall shear rate is \(8\bar u/D\), with \(D\) the diameter. Fails when: the velocity you were given is not the steady centre speed, or was measured after, not during; the vessel has valves, branches or a compliant wall. Then the numbers are estimates, and a report says what that costs.
  • The viscosity law and the plug (eqs 7.1.1, 7.1.6)The wall stress is the viscosity times the wall shear rate; blood's yield stress sets a core that rides without shearing, \(r_p/R = \tau_y/\tau_w\). Lecture's §1.4: any yield stress makes a formal plug, and "large enough to matter" is the useful question — half the radius is a fair line, reached when the wall stress falls to twice the yield stress. Fails when: the plug is a sizable share of the radius; the parabola no longer holds there, and at half a radius the flow is only 17/48 of the parabola's at the same wall stress (§1.3), so the blood would have to be slower still.
  • Continuity (eq. 7.2.1)Steady flow: the same volume per second passes every cross-section of one vessel, and a vein's flow can only grow toward the heart as tributaries join. Fails when: the three measurements were not simultaneous, or the velocity is a peak — then it tests the paper's consistency, not its exactness.
  • The single-spring surrogate (eq. 6.2.6)\(E_{eff} = \sigma/\varepsilon\) is the stiffness a spring would need to give this stress at this strain, at this moment of this loading. It belongs to the experiment: a faster stretch reads a higher one. Fails when: it is quoted as a property of the tissue without its speed and its strain — this tissue is not linear (Fig. 3 of the paper), so every stiffness here is a secant at 10 %.
  • The standard linear solid under a steady stretch (eq. 6.2.5, §2.7)\(E_{eff}(T) = E_\infty + E_1\,(\tau/T)(1 - e^{-T/\tau})\): a stretch much slower than \(\tau\) reads \(E_\infty\), one much faster reads \(E_0 = E_1 + E_\infty\), and nothing goes faster than that — the damper is in series with its spring, so at high speed it has no time to move and both springs carry the load. Fails when: a tissue has a spread of relaxation times rather than one (§2.6), so its stress need not stop at one ceiling; or \(\tau\) is assumed (the paper held nothing), so \(E_1\) and the sprint stress move with it.
  • Kelvin–Voigt under a steady stretch (eq. 6.2.4, §2.7)\(E_{eff}(T) = E + \eta/T\): the damper sits beside the spring, so the stress grows without limit as the stretch gets faster. Two slow tests fit it as well as they fit the standard linear solid; the two elements part company only far beyond the paper's speeds.
