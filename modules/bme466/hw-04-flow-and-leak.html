Challenge — when the lymph cannot keep up graded for 566 · everyone should attempt

The situation. Problem 3 filled the bitten skin through a leaky venule and left the lymph out. But the lymph does more than run at its resting rate: its flow rises as the tissue's pressure rises, so it should shrink the bump. By how much? Take Problem 3's skin, with your Problem 3 numbers, and add a lymph drain that carries away the extra fluid at a rate proportional to how far the tissue's pressure has risen: per unit volume of tissue, \(G\,(P_t - P_{t0})\), with the lymph's response \(G\) printed below. At rest the lymph already carries off exactly what the wall filters, so only the extra filtration and the extra lymph flow enter.

The question: how much smaller, and how much faster, is the bump when the lymph responds — and what happens to a patient whose lymph cannot?

Work it entirely by hand, in your written solutions: nothing on this tab is recorded, and your exported results file carries no challenge fields. Let \(v(t) = V/V_0 - 1\) be the fractional swelling, with the leak opening at \(t = 0\). Your written solution must do three things. Each step has a 💡 hint under it: try the step first, then open the hint if you are stuck.

  1. Set up the differential equation that \(v\) obeys while the leak is open. A way in: the tissue's volume changes at the rate the wall lets water in, minus the extra rate the lymph takes it away; write the first from the same reasoning as Problem 3, the second with the lymph's response, both in terms of \(v\). It is first order, linear, with constant coefficients. Then decide, and justify from the physics, the value of \(v\) at \(t = 0\).
    💡 Hint
    • The wall’s term is Problem 3’s rate. In Problem 3 the swelling grew at \(\dot v = S_VL_p\,(\text{push} - K_t v)\), with the leaking \(L_p\). Keep that term exactly as it was.
    • The lymph’s term. The lymph removes \(G\,(P_t - P_{t0})\) per unit volume. Problem 3’s spring tells you \(P_t - P_{t0}\) in terms of \(v\); substitute it, and subtract the result from the wall’s term.
    • The starting value. What was the skin’s volume the instant before the leak opened? Can water arrive through a wall fast enough to change the volume in zero time?
  2. Solve it. Give the swelling it settles at and the clock it gets there on. Then switch the lymph's response off (\(G \to 0\)) and show that both become your Problem 3 results, the end swelling and its clock. Say in one sentence what the lymph has bought the skin.
    💡 Hint
    • Collect every term with \(v\) on one side. The equation takes the form \(\dot v + v/\tau' = a\), where \(a\) and \(\tau'\) are constants built from your letters. Write down what \(a\) and \(\tau'\) are.
    • It is the same form as Problem 3’s equation, so its solution has the same shape: \(v(t) = v_{ss}\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau'})\). Check it by substituting it back, or derive it with the integrating factor \(e^{t/\tau'}\).
    • The settling swelling is where the swelling stops changing: set \(\dot v = 0\) and solve for \(v\).
    • To compare with Problem 3, divide the top and the bottom of \(v_{ss}\) and of \(\tau'\) by \(S_VL_p\). The ratio \(G/(S_VL_p)\) should appear in both.
  3. Use it. With your numbers: the settling swelling and the clock, with and without the lymph; the swelling when the leak closes after your Problem 3 time, with and without; the lymph response that would halve the settling swelling, compared with the printed one. Then say what happens in lymphoedema — lymph nodes removed after cancer surgery, or vessels blocked by filarial worms — where that response is lost.
    💡 Hint
    • Units first. \(G\) is printed in % of the tissue volume per minute per mmHg. Divide by 100 (a fraction) and by 60 (seconds) to put it in the same units as \(S_VL_p\), per (s·mmHg).
    • One number does most of the work: \(1 + G/(S_VL_p)\). Your Problem 3 end swelling and clock are each divided by it.
    • Halving the settling swelling means that number equals 2. Solve for the \(G\) that does it, and convert it back to the printed units.
    • Lymphoedema is \(G \to 0\): which of your two results does the swelling now head for?

📓 Notes — equations & when they apply

Notes — every law of Module 7 and Module 8 Tabs 1–2 this homework can use, and when each one applies

This sheet is your field guide. No question in this homework names its equation — you match the situation to the law, and every problem needs two. For each entry: what it says, the assumptions it stands on, and what breaks it. Nothing here is graded and nothing is hidden. Equation numbers are the modules' (module.tab.order), so you can open the derivation any time.

How to search this sheet. Module 7 and the first half of Module 8 are three sentences dressed for different stages: a pressure drives a flow against a resistance (Poiseuille, joined in series and in parallel, read backwards for a viscosity); the crowd and the stress decide how blood flows (the dilute law, the sleeve, single file, the plug); a wall passes things in proportion to a difference (a solute's concentration, water's four pressures), and a spring and a damper set the clock. Ask: what is pushing, what is resisting, what is the geometry or the clock?

Notation (the course's): \(\Delta P\) a pressure drop · \(Q\) a flow (volume per second) · \(\bar u\) a mean speed · \(R\), \(r\) a radius, \(D\) a diameter · \(\eta\) a viscosity, \(\eta_{app}\) an apparent one · \(H\) haematocrit · \(\tau\) a stress or a clock (said each time) · \(J_v\) a water flux · \(\Pi\) an osmotic pressure · the dot means \(d/dt\).

Viscosity is friction, counted — the viscosity law (eq. 7.1.1; 6.2.2 and 6.3.5 again)

$$\tau \equiv \sigma_{xy} = \frac{F}{A} = \eta\,\frac{du}{dy} = \eta\,\dot\gamma$$
  • Symbolsτ = shear stress (Pa) · F/A = the force on a plate per its area · η = viscosity (Pa·s; 1 mPa·s = 10⁻³ Pa·s) · du/dy = γ̇ = the shear rate (s⁻¹; between two plates U/h).
  • What it saysA fluid resists being sheared in proportion to how fast it is sheared. Week 6’s damper, F = ηẋ, spread over an area. An apparent viscosity is whatever η this law reads back from a measurement.
  • When it appliesSteady laminar shear of a Newtonian fluid (water, plasma); blood at high shear in wide tubes, with its apparent viscosity.
  • Fails whenBlood at low shear (rouleaux, a yield stress) and in tubes near a cell’s size, where its apparent viscosity depends on the tube.

The parabolic profile in a pipe (eq. 7.1.2)

$$u(r) = \frac{\Delta P}{4\eta L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right),\qquad \tau(r) = \frac{\Delta P}{2L}\,r$$
  • Symbolsu(r) = speed at distance r from the axis · ΔP = the pressure drop along a length L · R = the radius.
  • What it saysA force balance on a cylinder of fluid gives the stress, zero on the axis and largest at the wall, τ_w = ΔPR/2L; the viscosity law then gives the parabola: zero at the wall (no slip), fastest on the axis.
  • When it appliesSteady, fully developed, laminar flow in a straight rigid tube, no slip, one viscosity. The stress line τ = (ΔP/2L)r holds for ANY fluid; only the profile needs the viscosity law.
  • Fails whenNear the entrance of a tube, in pulsatile or turbulent flow, or when the fluid has a yield stress (the profile changes; the stress line does not).

Poiseuille’s flow — the fourth power, the mean speed, and reading it backwards (eq. 7.1.3)

$$Q = \frac{\pi R^4\,\Delta P}{8\eta L},\qquad \bar u = \frac{Q}{\pi R^2} = \frac{\Delta P\,R^2}{8\eta L} = \frac{u_{max}}{2},\qquad \eta_{app} = \frac{\pi R^4\,\Delta P}{8QL}$$
  • SymbolsQ = volume per second (m³/s; 1 nL/s = 10⁻¹² m³/s) · ū = mean speed · u_max = the centre speed.
  • What it saysThe parabola integrated over the cross-section. Ten per cent narrower is a third less flow. Read backwards, a measured flow gives the apparent viscosity: the viscosity a single fluid would need to pass that flow — a property of the blood in that tube.
  • When it appliesAs eq. 7.1.2; blood through its apparent viscosity for the tube it is in.
  • Fails whenWhere a plug forms (use the Bingham flow), or where ΔP is not one smooth pipe’s friction drop (a level of the tree with branches, valves, inertia).

Hydraulic resistance (eq. 7.1.4)

$$R_h = \frac{\Delta P}{Q} = \frac{8\eta L}{\pi R^4}$$
  • SymbolsR_h in Pa·s/m³; 1 mmHg·s/nL = 1.333 × 10¹⁴ Pa·s/m³.
  • What it saysThe pipe’s resistance is pressure per flow, like an electrical resistor: proportional to length and viscosity, to the inverse fourth power of the radius. It belongs to one vessel.
  • When it appliesEach straight vessel in steady laminar flow.
  • Fails whenWhen the radius is not fixed (a compliant vein, smooth muscle acting) — then recompute it at the new radius.

Series and parallel, for pipes (eq. 6.2.1 again)

$$\text{series: } R = R_1 + R_2,\ \ Q \text{ shared};\qquad \text{parallel: } \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2},\ \ \Delta P \text{ shared};\qquad n \text{ equal in parallel: } R/n$$
  • SymbolsWeek 6’s joining rules with resistance in place of compliance: pipes end to end share one flow and add their drops; pipes side by side share one drop and add their flows.
  • What it saysIn series each drop is the flow times that pipe’s resistance, so the drops follow the resistances; the level with the largest resistance holds the drop. In parallel n equal vessels have one vessel’s resistance over n.
  • When it appliesAny network of rigid pipes in steady flow, with a fixed pressure across it.
  • Fails whenWhen a vessel’s pressure is set by something other than its neighbours (valves, a pump, gravity).

Bingham’s law and the plug (eq. 7.1.5, 7.1.6)

$$\sigma_{xy} = \tau_y + \eta_p\dot\gamma\ \ (\tau > \tau_y);\qquad r_p = \frac{2\tau_y L}{\Delta P} \;\Leftrightarrow\; \frac{r_p}{R} = \frac{\tau_y}{\tau_w};\qquad \frac{Q}{Q_N} = 1 - \frac{4B}{3} + \frac{B^4}{3},\ \ B = \frac{r_p}{R}$$
  • Symbolsτ_y = yield stress (Pa) · η_p = plastic viscosity · r_p = the plug’s radius · Q_N = Poiseuille’s flow with η_p.
  • What it saysBelow its yield stress the fluid does not shear. The stress line is unchanged (eq. 7.1.2), so the core where τ < τ_y rides as a solid plug, and the sheared annulus outside it carries the rest. The plug’s share is the yield stress against the wall’s stress; at B = ½ the flow is 17/48 of Newtonian. Nothing moves below ΔP_crit = 2τ_yL/R. Rouleaux — red cells stacked by plasma proteins at low shear — are the yield stress’s origin.
  • When it appliesBlood at low shear, in an ideal Bingham picture (the yield stress 0.0015–0.005 Pa at H = 0.45, Merrill 1963).
  • Fails whenAt high shear the plug is a negligible correction; a true yield stress of blood is hard to measure (Fedosov 2011); Casson and Herschel–Bulkley fit better.

Continuity — the same litres per minute at every level (eq. 7.2.1)

$$\bar u = \frac{Q}{A_{total}},\qquad t = \frac{L}{\bar u}$$
  • SymbolsQ = the cardiac output through every level (5 L/min at rest) · A_total = the level’s total cross-section · t = the time in one vessel of length L.
  • What it saysEvery litre that leaves the heart passes every level of the tree, so the mean speed at a level is the output over that level’s total cross-section: slowest where the cross-section is largest, the capillaries.
  • When it appliesSteady flow through a closed circuit; any level, or any one vessel with its own Q and A.
  • Fails whenPulsatile instants (the aorta), or a level whose vessels carry very different flows.

The Reynolds number (eq. 7.2.2)

$$\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho\,\bar u\,D}{\eta}$$
  • Symbolsρ = 1 060 kg/m³ for blood · D = the diameter.
  • What it saysInertia against viscosity. Below about 1 the flow forgets inertia altogether (Stokes flow); below about 2 000 a steady pipe flow stays laminar.
  • When it appliesAny level of the tree, to decide whether Poiseuille’s steady laminar picture is fair.
  • Fails whenNear branch points and in the pulsing aorta, where the steady-pipe benchmark is only a guide.

Blood is a crowd — the dilute law and the crowd’s square (eq. 7.3.1, 7.3.2)

$$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = 1 + 2.5H \ \ \text{(dilute)};\qquad \frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = 1 + 2.5H + kH^2,\ \ k \approx 5$$
  • SymbolsH = haematocrit, the volume fraction of cells.
  • What it saysEach cell disturbs the plasma on its own (Einstein’s 2.5H); at blood’s crowding the cells also rub each other, a term that grows as the count of pairs, H².
  • When it appliesA wide tube at high shear: the bulk viscosity of blood (about 3.2 × plasma at H = 0.45).
  • Fails whenIn tubes near a cell’s size (the sleeve and single file take over) and at low shear (rouleaux).

Oxygen per unit pressure, and the best haematocrit (eq. 7.3.3, 7.3.4)

$$\text{O}_2\ \text{per unit pressure} \propto \frac{H}{\eta_{app}/\eta_{plasma}},\qquad H^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}$$
  • SymbolsH* = the haematocrit that delivers the most oxygen for a given push.
  • What it saysMore cells carry more oxygen but cost more push; with the crowd’s square the trade-off peaks at H* = 1/√k, near 0.45.
  • When it appliesThe bulk flow of a wide tube at a fixed pressure.
  • Fails whenWhen the tube is narrow (its apparent viscosity is lower) or the tissue’s demand, not the push, is fixed.

The two-layer tube — a plasma sleeve round a core of cells (eq. 7.3.5)

$$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = \frac{1}{1 - (R_c/R)^4\,(1 - \eta_{plasma}/\eta_c)}$$
  • SymbolsR_c = the core’s radius; the sleeve δ = R − R_c · η_c = the core’s viscosity (about 3.2 × plasma).
  • What it saysCells ride the fast centre and leave a plasma sleeve at the wall that lubricates them. One sleeve of about 1.5 µm draws the Fåhræus–Lindqvist fall: the narrower the tube, the larger the sleeve’s share and the lower the apparent viscosity.
  • When it appliesTubes from a few hundred µm down to about two cells across, many cells abreast.
  • Fails whenBelow the cell’s own size, where the cells pass in single file (next entry) and the apparent viscosity climbs again.

What the wall feels — wall shear rate and stress (eq. 7.3.6, 7.3.7)

$$\dot\gamma_w = \frac{4Q}{\pi R^3} = \frac{4\bar u}{R} = \frac{8\bar u}{D};\qquad \tau_w = \frac{\Delta P\,R}{2L} = \eta_{app}\,\frac{8\bar u}{D}$$
  • Symbolsγ̇_w = the shear rate at the wall for one fluid · τ_w = the stress the wall feels.
  • What it saysThe parabola’s slope at the wall, and the force balance at the wall. For blood the stress is the apparent viscosity times 8ū/D; the plasma sleeve at the wall is sheared (η_app/η_plasma) times harder than one fluid would be.
  • When it appliesSteady flow in a straight vessel; any fluid for the force balance.
  • Fails whenWhere ΔP is not one pipe’s friction drop, and in single file, where the film takes the shear.

Single file — plasma, plus a toll for every cell (eq. 7.3.8)

$$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} \approx 1 + H\,\frac{R}{4\delta}$$
  • Symbolsδ = the thin plasma film between each cell and the wall · R = the tube’s radius.
  • What it saysIn a tube narrower than a cell the cells fold and pass one at a time, each sliding on a thin film; every cell adds a toll R/4δ, so the thinner the film the higher the apparent viscosity — the inverse Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect. (Exact form, Module 7’s Derive box: toll = x/[2(1 − x)], x = (R_c/R)².)
  • When it appliesCapillaries narrower than a red cell (about 3–7 µm), when the film is thin against the radius.
  • Fails whenMany cells abreast (use the two-layer tube); or a thick film, where the thin-film form overstates the toll.

The T cell at the wall — Stokes and the counting floor (eq. 1.3.3, 3.3.12 again)

$$F = 6\pi\eta a v,\ \ v = a\dot\gamma_w;\qquad \frac{\delta C}{C} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D a C\,t}}$$
  • Symbolsa = the cell’s radius · v = the fluid’s speed one radius from the wall · D, C, t = Week 3’s diffusivity, concentration and counting time.
  • What it saysStokes’ drag at the fluid’s speed one radius from the wall gives the push on a cell beside the wall; Week 3’s floor, solved for the time, gives how long a cell must stay beside a patch to read it.
  • When it appliesA cell near a wall in slow, low-Re flow.
  • Fails whenWhen the cell is pinned, rolling or deformed, or the wall’s presence changes the drag (wall factors are left out).

Through a wall — the flux, the wall’s ease, the windows (eq. 2.1.2, 3.3.8 again)

$$J = D\,\frac{C_L - C_R}{L},\qquad k = \frac{D}{\delta},\qquad k_w = f_{open}\,\frac{D}{\delta},\qquad z = k\,\frac{2}{r}\,t$$
  • SymbolsJ = flux per wall area · k = how easily a wall of thickness δ passes a molecule (m/s) · f_open = the share of the wall open to it · 2/r = wall per volume of a capillary · t = time in the capillary.
  • What it saysA wall passes a molecule in proportion to its diffusivity over the wall’s thickness, times the share of the wall it can get through. Oxygen crosses the whole wall; water-soluble solutes only the gaps (continuous walls), more through windows (fenestrated), everything (sinusoids). z compares the wall’s ease and area with the time.
  • When it appliesSteady diffusion across a thin wall.
  • Fails whenWhen the concentrations on either side change on the crossing time, or transport is carried rather than diffused.

Extraction along a capillary (eq. 8.1.1, Renkin–Crone)

$$\frac{C(x) - C_t}{C_{in} - C_t} = e^{-2k_wx/(r\bar u)},\qquad f_{ex} = 1 - e^{-k_wA_w/Q},\qquad \frac{A_w}{Q} = \frac{2L}{r\bar u}$$
  • SymbolsC_t = the tissue’s concentration (held steady) · A_w = the capillary’s wall area 2πrL · Q = its flow πr²ū.
  • What it saysWhat leaks through the wall of each slice is what the blood loses, so the difference falls exponentially along the capillary. k_wA_w/Q decides: small, and only that fraction crosses (wall-limited); large, and the blood empties of it (flow-limited).
  • When it appliesOne capillary with a steady tissue concentration, one permeability along its length.
  • Fails whenWhen the tissue loads up as the solute arrives, or the permeability varies along the vessel.

Osmotic pressure, measured as a height (eq. 8.2.1, van ’t Hoff)

$$\Pi = C\,R\,T = \rho g h$$
  • SymbolsC = concentration of the molecules the wall holds back (mol/m³) · R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) · T = temperature · h = the osmometer’s column height.
  • What it saysWater moves towards the side with molecules it cannot take along; the pressure that stops it is Π. Only what the wall holds back pulls: at a capillary that is the protein (oncotic pressure). Plasma’s measured value is 26.9 mmHg; van ’t Hoff on albumin alone falls nearly half short (charge and crowding).
  • When it appliesDilute, ideal solutions; the order of magnitude and the direction for plasma.
  • Fails whenAt plasma’s crowding and charge — use the measured oncotic pressure.

Starling filtration — four pressures across one wall (eq. 8.2.2)

$$J_v = L_p\Big[\big(P_c - P_t\big) - f_r\big(\Pi_c - \Pi_t\big)\Big]$$
  • SymbolsJ_v = water flux per wall area (positive out of the blood) · L_p = the wall’s hydraulic conductivity · P_c, P_t = blood and tissue pressures · Π_c, Π_t = plasma and tissue oncotic pressures · f_r = the fraction of protein the wall reflects (0 to 1).
  • What it saysThe blood’s pressure (and the tissue’s protein) push water out; the tissue’s pressure (and the plasma’s protein) push it in; the protein terms are scaled by how well the wall holds protein back. Water turns back only where P_c falls below P_t + f_r(Π_c − Π_t) — about 14.1 mmHg in the resting leg.
  • When it appliesAny capillary or venule wall, at the pressures where it is.
  • Fails whenThe bulk-tissue ledger overstates the steady filtration ten- to fortyfold: the pull that acts is set under the glycocalyx (Levick & Michel 2010). Direction and where it changes are right; the size is not.

The tissue is a spring, in pressure and volume (eq. 5.5.1 again)

$$P_t = P_{t0} + K_t\left(\frac{V}{V_0} - 1\right);\qquad \frac{V_{end}}{V_0} - 1 = \frac{(P_c - P_{t0}) - f_r(\Pi_c - \Pi_t)}{K_t}$$
  • SymbolsP_t0 = the tissue’s resting pressure · K_t = its stiffness in pressure per fractional swelling (about 20 mmHg in skin) · V_end = where a swelling heads.
  • What it saysHooke’s law with pressure for force and fractional swelling for stretch. Put it into Starling and filtration slows as the tissue swells, stopping where the tissue’s pressure has risen by the whole net push.
  • When it appliesNear normal hydration, up to about +2 mmHg.
  • Fails whenOverfilled: the pressure stops rising at about +2 mmHg while the volume keeps growing — the spring gives way (Wiig & Reed 1987).

Kelvin–Voigt: the swelling’s clock (eq. 6.2.4 again)

$$F = kx + \eta\dot x \;\longrightarrow\; V(t) = V_{end} + (V_0 - V_{end})\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = \frac{1}{S_V L_p K_t}$$
  • SymbolsS_V = capillary wall area per volume of tissue (about 58 cm⁻¹ in muscle) · the spring k ↔ K_t · the damper η ↔ 1/(S_V L_p).
  • What it saysWater arrives through all the wall in the tissue, dV/dt = S_VV_0J_v; with the spring inside J_v the swelling still to come shrinks at a rate proportional to itself: Week 6’s spring and damper side by side under a step. Filling and draining run on the same clock with the L_p of the wall at the time.
  • When it appliesA step change in the wall’s properties, the spring still straight.
  • Fails whenWhen the leak changes during the filling, the spring gives way, or the lymph’s response is not negligible.

The way back — the lymph

$$\text{resting: lymph} = \text{filtration} \approx 8\ \text{L/day};\qquad \text{lymph flow rises as } P_t \text{ rises}$$
  • SymbolsBlind-ended lymphatic vessels collect the filtrate and its protein and return it to the veins near the heart.
  • What it saysIn most tissues the capillary filters a little along its whole length and the lymph closes the circuit; lymph has about a tenfold reserve and its flow rises with the tissue’s pressure (Breslin 2018), one of the body’s defences against swelling. Uptake back into a capillary is transient, except in the gut and around the kidney’s tubules.
  • When it appliesA tissue at rest, or recovering from a swelling.
  • Fails whenLymphoedema — nodes removed or vessels blocked — where the reserve is lost.

Constants and bridges (cite them)

$$1\ \text{mmHg} = 133.3\ \text{Pa};\quad 1\ \text{mPa·s} = 10^{-3}\ \text{Pa·s};\quad 1\ \text{nL} = 10^{-12}\ \text{m}^3;\quad 1\ \text{cm/s} = 10^{-2}\ \text{m/s}$$
  • SymbolsPlasma 1.2 mPa·s at 37 °C (1.1–1.3, Késmárky 2008) · whole blood 3–4 mPa·s · blood density 1 060 kg/m³ · capillary speed 0.65 ± 0.3 mm/s in human skin (Fagrell 1977) · plasma oncotic 26.9 mmHg, leg tissue −0.4 mmHg and 10.8 mmHg (Noddeland 1982) · resting L_p ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ cm/(s·mmHg) · glucose through a continuous wall 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ cm/s (Alvarez & Yudilevich 1969) · blood’s yield stress 0.0015–0.005 Pa at H = 0.45 (Merrill 1963).

  • Fails whenA printed given always wins.

How to work a problem whose givens are printed

$$\text{letters} \to \text{units and limits} \to \text{numbers}$$
  • What it saysWrite the relation in letters, check its units and its limits (a rigid tube, no yield stress, a closed leak, no cells), and only then put numbers in. Every problem needs two laws from different parts of the modules — name the quantity the first hands to the second. Part (b) uses YOUR part (a).
