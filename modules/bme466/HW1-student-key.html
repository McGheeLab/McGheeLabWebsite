The mistakes that cost the most

These are the errors that cost the class the most credit on this homework, most common first. Each one is worked through under its problem below.

  1. Problem 2: comparing the two dishes at one instant. The most common mistake on the assignment, made by most of the class. Crowding changes the exponent, not just the prefactor, so the dilute dish pulls further ahead the longer you wait. The ratio at t★ carries a factor t★1−α; the plain ratio of coefficients leaves it out.
  2. Problem 3 (b): radius and diameter swapped. About four in ten answers missed it. Most landed at exactly a quarter of the right ratio, R/(2a) instead of 2R/a; a smaller group used R²/D for the crossing time. Write both times symbolically, divide, and let D cancel before any number goes in.
  3. Bench 1: the fit window for the crowded exponent. A window that starts at the shortest lags includes the camera’s localization floor, which flattens the log–log slope and drags α low. Choose the window, and say why.
  4. Problem 4: the nM bridge. 1 nM = 0.6022 molecules per µm³. Leaving it out makes the slope 1.66× too big; dividing by it instead makes it 2.8× too big. Derive it once and you will never cross it backwards.
  5. Problem 1 (b): linear scaling. Mass follows volume for a compact protein, so \(M\propto r^3\). A linear scaling lands at a fifth to a quarter of the right tag count.
  6. Missing citations. The 0.34 nm per base pair (Problem 3) and the FRAP half-time constant (Bench 2) are documented numbers the assignment asks you to find and cite. A right number with no source still loses the citation credit.

Bench 1 — D from tracking, and the crowded α

Given. Their own table of tracking-window fits: each row a window with its log–log slope and its MSD at 1 s, in the dilute and the crowded dish.
1 Choose the relation
The mean-squared displacement of a random walk in TWO dimensions
The camera records a projection onto the image plane, so the walk it sees is two-dimensional and the coefficient is 4, not the 6 of a three-dimensional walk. This is the most important dimensional judgement in the assignment.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Random walks & the mean-squared displacement” · Module 1 §2.3 “Pearson’s drunkard and the law of ⟨x²⟩” (eq. 1.2.6, ⟨r²⟩ = 2dDt) · the crowded exponent: Module 2 §2.4 “When the law expires: the crowded cell”.
2 Set it up
$$\langle r^2\rangle = 4Dt \quad\Rightarrow\quad D = \frac{\mathrm{MSD}(\tau = 1\ \mathrm{s})}{4}$$
dilute dish
$$\langle r^2\rangle = 4\Gamma\,t^{\alpha} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha = \text{slope of } \log\langle r^2\rangle \text{ against } \log t$$
crowded dish

Choose windows above the localization floor at short lags (the 4σ² floor flattens the slope) and below the scatter at long lags (few independent intervals).

3 Put the numbers in

Median MSD at 1 s over the dilute windows: 80.43 µm² (the noise-free value for this dataset; real windows scatter around it).

$$D = \frac{80.43\ \mathrm{µm^2}}{4\times 1\ \mathrm{s}} = 20.11\ \mathrm{µm^2/s}$$

Median log–log slope over the crowded windows: α = 0.75.

Answer. D = 20.11 µm²/s and α = 0.75
A complete write-up also shows
  • Why your fit windows are trustworthy: above the noise floor at short lags, below the long-lag scatter. A sketch of the MSD with the window marked is the clearest way.
  • Why the coefficient is 4 and not 6.
  • An uncertainty on D from the spread across your own windows.
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 0.67× the right answer (13.41 µm²/s on this dataset). Divided by 6 — a three-dimensional walk. The camera records a 2-D projection, so the coefficient is 4.
  • ≈ 4× the right answer (80.43 µm²/s on this dataset). Reported the MSD at 1 s itself as D (no division at all). MSD at τ = 1 s is 2dD numerically, so divide by 4.
  • α noticeably below the slope your later windows settle on. A window that starts at the shortest lags includes the camera's localization floor (4σ²), which props up the short-lag MSD and flattens the fitted slope. Start the window above the floor, and say in the PDF why you chose it.

Bench 2 — a second, independent D from bleaching

Given. Their own table of bleach runs: a spot radius w and its half-recovery time τ½, for at least four spot sizes.
1 Choose the relation
Diffusive refilling of a bleached spot — the FRAP half-time
A uniform circular bleach spot refills by pure diffusion; its half-recovery time scales as w², and 0.224 is the documented constant for that geometry. The relation is a citable classic, and the citation carries marks.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Filling a bleached hole (FRAP)” (Soumpasis 1983) · Module 2 §2.3 “Filling the hole — Fick’s second law with a stopwatch” (eq. 2.2.3).
2 Set it up
$$\tau_{1/2} = 0.224\,\frac{w^2}{D} \quad\Rightarrow\quad D = 0.224\,\frac{w^2}{\tau_{1/2}}$$

First check the exponent on your own data: log τ½ against log w should have slope 2. Then convert each spot size and average the D values (not the τ½ values).

3 Put the numbers in

At w = 1.0 µm the stopwatch reads τ½ = 0.01114 s.

$$D = 0.224\times\frac{(1.0\ \mathrm{µm})^2}{0.01114\ \mathrm{s}} = 20.11\ \mathrm{µm^2/s}$$
Answer. D = 20.11 µm²/s — the same particle, measured a completely different way
A complete write-up also shows
  • The exponent your own τ½-vs-w data shows, stated before you convert anything.
  • The conversion written symbolically first, then with numbers, averaged over spot sizes.
  • A citation for the relation (Soumpasis 1983, or the Notes entry that cites it).
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 4.5× the right answer (89.77 µm²/s on this dataset). Dropped the 0.224 (D = w²/τ½). The 0.224 belongs to the uniform circular spot; it is part of the relation, and it needs a citation.
  • ≈ 0.25× the right answer (5.03 µm²/s on this dataset). Used the spot DIAMETER where the relation wants its radius. w is the spot radius.
  • Averaging τ½ across spot sizes, then converting once. τ½ depends on w, so an average over different spots means nothing. Convert each spot to a D, then average the D values.

Problem 1 — Size it, count its tags, get it through a film [15]

The chain at a glance — the equations used, and how they combine
(a) Stokes–Einstein, eq. 1.3.9, run backwards: D in, size out
$$\begin{aligned}\eta &= 2.24\times\eta_{\rm water}(310\ \mathrm{K}) = 2.24\times 6.924\times 10^{-4} = 1.551\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s} \\ r &= \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta D} = \frac{(1.381\times10^{-23})(310)}{6\pi(1.551\times 10^{-3})(20.11\times10^{-12})} = \mathbf{7.28\ \mathrm{nm}}\end{aligned}$$
(b) \(M\propto r^3\) anchored on the printed pair (150 kDa ↔ 5.6 nm); then count the tags
$$\begin{aligned}M &= 150\ \mathrm{kDa}\times\Big(\frac{7.28}{5.6}\Big)^3 = 329.6\ \mathrm{kDa},\qquad N = \frac{329.6 - 150}{30} = \mathbf{5.99}\end{aligned}$$
about 6 tags per antibody
(c) Fick’s first law across the film at steady state, eq. 1.3.5 with the linear profile of eq. 2.1.2
$$\begin{aligned}J &= D\,\frac{\Delta C}{L} = 20.11\times\frac{100}{5.0} = \mathbf{402.2\ \mathrm{µm^{-2}\,s^{-1}}}\end{aligned}$$

Problem 1 (a) — How big is the conjugate?

Given. Their measured D, the datasheet viscosity (2.24× water) and the temperature, 310 K.
1 Choose the relation
Stokes–Einstein, rearranged for the radius
It is the one relation linking a measured diffusion coefficient to a size through the viscosity and the temperature, and all three are in hand. Run backwards it is an instrument: D in, size out.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Moving through fluid — drag, and Einstein’s bargain” · Module 1 §3.2 “Mobility has a law” and §3.3 “Why the bargain is forced: D = μkBT” (eq. 1.3.9, D = kBT/6πηa).
2 Set it up
$$D = \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad r = \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta D}$$

Units: D in m²/s and η in Pa·s; r then comes out in metres.

3 Put the numbers in
$$\eta = 2.24\times\eta_{\rm water}(310\ \mathrm{K}) = 2.24\times 6.924\times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s} = 1.551\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}$$
= 1.551 mPa·s
$$D = 20.11\ \mathrm{µm^2/s} = 20.11\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$$
$$r = \frac{(1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K})(310\ \mathrm{K})}{6\pi\,(1.551\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s})(20.11\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m^2/s})} = 7.28\times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m}$$
Answer. r = 7.28 nm (the bare antibody is 5.6 nm: the tags have made it bigger)
A complete write-up also shows
  • A visible unit track: µm²/s to m²/s, cP to Pa·s, metres to nm.
  • Your radius set beside the bare antibody’s 5.6 nm.
  • The assumptions: a sphere, no-slip, low Reynolds number, dilute.
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 2.2× the right answer (16.31 nm on this dataset). Used water’s viscosity; the datasheet’s ×2.24 was left out. The conjugate sits in the crowding medium: η = η_rel × η_water at your T.
  • ≈ 1.5× the right answer (10.92 nm on this dataset). Stokes drag written 4πηr instead of 6πηr. No-slip drag on a sphere is 6πηr.
  • ≈ 0.001× the right answer (0.00728 nm on this dataset). η left in centipoise (or a nm/µm slip): a factor of 1000. Work in SI throughout (Pa·s, m²/s) and convert to nm only at the end.

Problem 1 (b) — How many tags does it carry?

The one step you had to work out for yourself. The question prints the mass-to-count accounting but deliberately not the radius-to-mass scaling.
Given. The radius from (a), and the QC sheet: a bare antibody is 150 kDa with radius 5.6 nm; each tag adds 30 kDa.
1 Choose the relation
For a compact protein, mass scales as the CUBE of the hydrodynamic radius
A compact globular protein is roughly a constant-density sphere, so mass follows volume, and volume grows as r³. Anchoring on the printed pair (150 kDa ↔ 5.6 nm) cancels the density out, so none has to be looked up or invented.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Molecular weight ↔ hydrodynamic radius” (M2/M1 = (R2/R1)³, anchored to a known pair) · practised in the module’s ★ Practice tab (DLS on IgG).
2 Set it up
$$M_{\rm conj} = 150\ \mathrm{kDa}\times\Big(\frac{r_p}{5.6\ \mathrm{nm}}\Big)^3$$
$$N = \frac{M_{\rm conj} - 150\ \mathrm{kDa}}{30\ \mathrm{kDa}}$$
subtract the bare antibody first
3 Put the numbers in
$$M_{\rm conj} = 150\times\Big(\frac{7.28}{5.6}\Big)^3 = 150\times 2.197 = 329.6\ \mathrm{kDa}$$
$$N = \frac{329.6 - 150}{30} = 5.99$$
Answer. About 6 tags per antibody
A complete write-up also shows
  • Why constant density gives \(M\propto r^3\).
  • The anchor on the printed 150 kDa ↔ 5.6 nm pair, rather than a density you looked up.
  • A sanity check: N should land close to a whole number.
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 0.25× the right answer (1.50 on this dataset). Linear scaling, \(M\propto r\). Mass follows volume for a compact protein, and volume grows as r³.
  • ≈ 1.8× the right answer (10.99 on this dataset). Did not subtract the bare 150 kDa antibody before dividing by 30. N counts tags: (conjugate mass − 150 kDa) ÷ 30 kDa.
  • ≈ 0.58× the right answer (3.45 on this dataset). Area scaling, \(M\propto r^2\). Mass fills a volume, not a surface.

Problem 1 (c) — How fast does it cross the film?

Given. Their measured D, the film thickness L = 5.0 µm and the held difference ΔC = 100 particles/µm³.
1 Choose the relation
Fick’s first law across a slab at steady state
At steady state, with a fixed difference held across a uniform slab, the concentration profile is a straight line, so the gradient is simply ΔC over the thickness.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Steady gradients — flux through a film” · Module 1 §3.3 (eq. 1.3.5, Fick’s first law) · Module 2 §1.2 “Counting makes a current” and §1.3 “Steady state: nature’s straight line” (eq. 2.1.2).
2 Set it up
$$J = D\,\frac{\Delta C}{L}$$
steady state; a linear profile inside the film
3 Put the numbers in
$$J = 20.11\ \mathrm{µm^2/s}\times\frac{100\ \mathrm{µm^{-3}}}{5.0\ \mathrm{µm}} = 402.2\ \mathrm{µm^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$$
Answer. J = 402 particles per µm² per second
A complete write-up also shows
  • What must be steady (∂C/∂t = 0) and what must be dilute (D independent of C).
  • Why the gradient is ΔC/L: the steady profile through a uniform slab is a straight line.
  • The units carried through to µm⁻²s⁻¹.
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 5× the right answer (2010.9 /µm²·s on this dataset). Left out the thickness: J = DΔC. The gradient is ΔC/L.
  • ≈ 0.2× the right answer (80.4 /µm²·s on this dataset). Divided by L². Check the units: D·ΔC/L is (µm²/s)(µm⁻³)/µm = µm⁻²s⁻¹.

Problem 2 — The crowding tax [10]

The hardest problem on the assignment, and the most common mistake in the class: comparing the two dishes at one instant instead of at t★.
The chain at a glance — the equations used, and how they combine
eq. 1.2.6 for the dilute dish, the subdiffusive law \(\mathrm{MSD}\propto t^{\alpha}\) for the crowded one; divide at t★
$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\langle r^2\rangle_d(t^\star)}{\langle r^2\rangle_c(t^\star)} &= \frac{\mathrm{MSD}_d(1\ \mathrm{s})\;t^\star}{\mathrm{MSD}_c(1\ \mathrm{s})\;t^{\star\alpha}} = \frac{\mathrm{MSD}_d(1\ \mathrm{s})}{\mathrm{MSD}_c(1\ \mathrm{s})}\;t^{\star\,1-\alpha} \\ &= \frac{80.43}{48.26}\times 60^{\,1-0.75} = 1.667\times 2.783 = \mathbf{4.64}\end{aligned}$$

Problem 2 — How many times further has the dilute dish spread after t★?

Given. Their own MSD-at-1 s from both dishes, their α, and the observation time t★ = 60 s.
1 Choose the relation
Compare the two MSD curves AT t★, not the two diffusion coefficients
The crowded dish is subdiffusive: its MSD grows like tα with α < 1, while the dilute dish grows like t. The gap between them WIDENS with time, and the question asks for the gap at one specific time. Comparing prefactors alone throws away exactly the physics being tested.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Random walks & the mean-squared displacement”, its second line (\(\mathrm{MSD}\propto\tau^{\alpha}\), α < 1 when crowded) · Module 2 §2.4 “When the law expires: the crowded cell”.
2 Set it up
$$\langle r^2\rangle_d(t) = \mathrm{MSD}_d(1\ \mathrm{s})\cdot t \qquad \langle r^2\rangle_c(t) = \mathrm{MSD}_c(1\ \mathrm{s})\cdot t^{\alpha}$$
$$\text{ratio}(t^\star) = \frac{\langle r^2\rangle_d(t^\star)}{\langle r^2\rangle_c(t^\star)} = \frac{\mathrm{MSD}_d(1\ \mathrm{s})}{\mathrm{MSD}_c(1\ \mathrm{s})}\times t^{\star\,1-\alpha}$$

The crowded prefactor has units µm²/sα, so the ratio of prefactors carries sα−1. The t★1−α factor is exactly what cancels it and leaves a pure number.

3 Put the numbers in
$$\frac{\mathrm{MSD}_d}{\mathrm{MSD}_c} = \frac{80.43}{48.26} = 1.667$$
from the window fits, dilute and crowded
$$t^{\star\,1-\alpha} = 60^{0.25} = 2.783$$
$$\text{ratio} = 1.667\times 2.783 = 4.64$$
Answer. 4.64× — after 60 s the dilute dish has spread 4.6 times further
A complete write-up also shows
  • The ratio derived symbolically before any numbers.
  • Why the odd units of the crowded prefactor (µm²/sα) are harmless once t★1−α is carried along.
  • Both prefactors and α taken from your own window fits.
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 0.36× the right answer (1.667 on this dataset). Stopped at the ratio of the two prefactors, with no time factor. The crowded MSD grows like t^α, the dilute one like t, so the gap widens with time; carry both to t★.
  • ≈ 7.7× the right answer (35.9 on this dataset). Used t★^α where t★^(1−α) belongs. Write both MSDs at t★ and divide; the exponent that survives is 1 − α.
  • ≈ 0.046× the right answer (0.216 on this dataset). Inverted the ratio (crowded over dilute). The question asks how many times LARGER the dilute MSD is, so the answer exceeds 1.

Problem 3 — One address in the whole nucleus [15]

The chain at a glance — the equations used, and how they combine
(a) the confined search for a small absorbing target (Notes, FRAP entry, second box); \(a_{\rm site}\) = 10 bp × 0.34 nm

Problem 3 (a) — How long to find one site?

Given. Their bleaching D, a nucleus of radius R = 5.5 µm, and a single 10-base-pair target.
1 Choose the relation
Mean first-passage time to a small absorbing target inside a reflecting sphere
The searcher is trapped inside the nucleus (the envelope reflects it back) and the site is a tiny absorbing target: touch it and the search ends. That is the classical confined-search result, valid when the target is far smaller than the container.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Filling a bleached hole (FRAP)”, its second box (ttraverse and τsearch, with asite ≈ 3.4 nm) · Module 2 §4.2 “One molecule, one chance” and §4.3 “Why it is a/r — and why D is nowhere in it”.
2 Set it up
$$a_{\rm site} = 10\ \mathrm{bp}\times 0.34\ \mathrm{nm/bp} = 3.4\ \mathrm{nm} = 3.4\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{µm}$$
$$\tau_{\rm search} = \frac{R^3}{3D\,a_{\rm site}}$$
3 Put the numbers in
$$\tau_{\rm search} = \frac{(5.5\ \mathrm{µm})^3}{3\,(20.11\ \mathrm{µm^2/s})(0.0034\ \mathrm{µm})} = \frac{166.4}{0.2051}\ \mathrm{s} = 811\ \mathrm{s}$$
Answer. 811 seconds — about 13.5 minutes to find one address
A complete write-up also shows
  • A citation for 0.34 nm per base pair.
  • The nm → µm conversion of asite, done visibly.
  • The assumptions: a target much smaller than the nucleus, a reflecting envelope, a uniform start, no sliding along the DNA.
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 0.001× the right answer (0.811 s on this dataset). Put a_site in nm (3.4) into a formula in µm. Convert first: 3.4 nm = 3.4×10⁻³ µm.
  • ≈ 10× the right answer (8112 s on this dataset). Took the site as one base pair (0.34 nm) instead of ten. The target is a 10-bp site: 10 × 0.34 nm = 3.4 nm.

Problem 3 (b) — How much worse is searching than crossing?

Given. Their search time from (a), the same nucleus and the same D.
1 Choose the relation
Divide by the plain three-dimensional crossing time
The point of this part is that D, the thing two benches were spent measuring, cancels completely, leaving pure geometry. The crossing time is the ordinary 3-D diffusion time over a distance R, which carries the 6.
Where it is taught. Notes › the same FRAP entry, its Watch-out (the structural check τsearch ÷ ttraverse = 2R/asite) · Module 1 §2.5 (eq. 1.2.8, t ≈ L²/2dD with d = 3) · Module 2 §4.3 “why D is nowhere in it”.
2 Set it up
$$t_{\rm cross} = \frac{R^2}{6D}$$
$$\frac{\tau_{\rm search}}{t_{\rm cross}} = \frac{R^3/(3D\,a_{\rm site})}{R^2/(6D)} = \frac{6D\,R^3}{3D\,a_{\rm site}\,R^2} = \frac{2R}{a_{\rm site}}$$
D cancels, and so does everything measured
3 Put the numbers in
$$t_{\rm cross} = \frac{(5.5\ \mathrm{µm})^2}{6\,(20.11\ \mathrm{µm^2/s})} = 0.251\ \mathrm{s}$$
$$\frac{811\ \mathrm{s}}{0.251\ \mathrm{s}} = 3235\qquad\text{check: }\frac{2(5.5)}{0.0034} = 3235\ \checkmark$$
Answer. 3235 — the nucleus DIAMETER divided by the target RADIUS
A complete write-up also shows
  • The cancellation of D shown symbolically, before any numbers.
  • A physical reading: finding one specific site takes thousands of crossings' worth of time, which is why real transcription factors slide along DNA (facilitated diffusion).
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 0.25× the right answer (809 on this dataset). Radius and diameter swapped at both ends: R/(2a) instead of 2R/a. Write both times symbolically and divide; the 2 lands on R.
  • ≈ 0.17× the right answer (539 on this dataset). Used R²/D for the crossing time (the 6 dropped). The three-dimensional crossing time is R²/(2dD) = R²/6D.

Problem 4 — Delivery or chemistry? [10]

The chain at a glance — the equations used, and how they combine
eq. 1.5.1 (Smoluchowski’s ceiling), then the nM bridge, then the arrival rate at c★
$$\begin{aligned}k &= 4\pi Da = 4\pi(20.11)(3.0) = 758.1\ \mathrm{µm^3/s} \\ k_{\rm slope} &= k\times 0.6022 = \mathbf{456.5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}\ per\ nM}},\qquad \text{arrivals} = k_{\rm slope}\,c^\star = 456.5\times 0.5 = 228\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\end{aligned}$$

Problem 4 — The capture-rate slope, and the verdict

Given. Their measured D, a cell of radius a = 3.0 µm, and a bath at c★ = 0.5 nM.
1 Choose the relation
Smoluchowski’s diffusion-limited capture rate, then the nM → per-µm³ bridge
A perfectly absorbing sphere sets the ceiling on how fast diffusion alone can deliver molecules to a cell, proportional to its radius, not its area. Converting the bath concentration from nM to molecules per µm³ is part of the problem, and the question says so.
Where it is taught. Notes › “Encounters — the diffusion-limited ceiling, and the unit bridge” (1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³) · Module 1 §5.1 “The hungry receptor” (eq. 1.5.1), derived line by line in Module 2 §3.3 (eq. 2.3.1 → eq. 1.5.1).
2 Set it up
$$k = 4\pi D a\qquad[\mathrm{µm^3/s}]$$
$$1\ \mathrm{nM} = 0.6022\ \text{molecules}/\mathrm{µm^3}\quad\Rightarrow\quad k_{\rm slope} = 4\pi D a\times 0.6022\qquad[\mathrm{s^{-1}\ per\ nM}]$$
$$\text{arrivals per second} = k_{\rm slope}\,c^\star$$
3 Put the numbers in
$$k = 4\pi\,(20.11\ \mathrm{µm^2/s})(3.0\ \mathrm{µm}) = 758.1\ \mathrm{µm^3/s}$$
$$k_{\rm slope} = 758.1\times 0.6022 = 456.5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}\ per\ nM}$$
$$\text{arrivals} = 456.5\times 0.5 = 228\ \text{per second}$$
Answer. k_slope = 457 s⁻¹ per nM — 228 arrivals/s against the ~1/s needed
A complete write-up also shows
  • The nM → per-µm³ bridge derived, not just asserted.
  • The verdict argued from your own arrival rate against the ~1 committed event per second.
  • That 4πDa is a ceiling: a real cell, covered in finite receptors, captures less.
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 1.7× the right answer (758.1 s⁻¹ per nM on this dataset). Dropped the nM bridge: reported 4πDa (µm³/s) as the slope. 1 nM = 0.6022 molecules/µm³; multiply by it.
  • ≈ 2.8× the right answer (1258.8 s⁻¹ per nM on this dataset). Crossed the bridge backwards (divided by 0.6022). Derive the bridge: 10⁻⁹ mol/L × 6.022×10²³ ÷ 10¹⁵ µm³/L = 0.6022 /µm³.
  • ≈ 0.5× the right answer (228.3 s⁻¹ on this dataset). Reported the arrival rate k·c★ instead of the slope per nM. The field asks for the slope; the arrival rate is what the verdict uses.
The verdict is B — chemistry. Arrivals at c★ are plentiful: the diffusion ceiling delivers 228 molecules a second against the ~1 committed event a second the response needs. Every dataset in the class lands far above 1/s, so delivery is never the bottleneck; binding and processing must be.

The Challenge, worked

Graded for 566 students; attempted by everyone. Solved entirely by hand: the module records nothing for it.

The question. A single cell of radius a is sealed in a droplet of radius R holding one nutrient at concentration C0. The cell’s surface consumes the nutrient on contact. How long until 90 % of the nutrient is gone, and was the model even legitimate?

Required: solve the governing differential equation; get the time constant two independent ways and reconcile them; rule on the well-mixed assumption with a symbolic ratio of timescales.

The chain at a glance
Smoluchowski’s rate (eq. 1.5.1) drains a well-mixed droplet: first-order loss
$$\begin{aligned}V\frac{dC}{dt} &= -4\pi D_{\rm nut}a\,C \;\Rightarrow\; C = C_0e^{-t/\tau},\quad \tau = \frac{V}{4\pi D_{\rm nut}a},\quad t_{90} = \tau\ln 10\end{aligned}$$
\(D_{\rm nut}\) two ways: scale the measured D by \(r_p/r_{\rm nut}\), or Stokes–Einstein (eq. 1.3.9) directly
$$\begin{aligned}D_{\rm nut} &= 20.11\times\frac{7.28}{0.3} = 488.0\ \mathrm{µm^2/s} = \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta r_{\rm nut}}\ \checkmark \\ \tau &= \frac{2.681\times 10^{5}}{4\pi(488.0)(3.0)} = 14.57\ \mathrm{s},\qquad t_{90} = \mathbf{33.6\ \mathrm{s}}\end{aligned}$$
well-mixed? the mixing time (eq. 1.2.8, d = 3) over τ; D cancels
$$\begin{aligned}\frac{t_{\rm mix}}{\tau} &= \frac{R^2/6D}{V/4\pi Da} = \frac{a}{2R} = \frac{3.0}{80} = \mathbf{0.0375}\ \ll 1\ \Rightarrow\ \text{well mixed}\end{aligned}$$
Where it is taught. Notes › “First-order loss — when removal is proportional to what remains” (X = X0e−t/τ, t90 = τ ln 10, and “well-mixed is an assumption you can test”) · “Encounters” for k = 4πDa (Module 1 eq. 1.5.1) · “Moving through fluid” for D of a smaller molecule (Module 1 eq. 1.3.9) · the mixing time is Module 1 eq. 1.2.8 · Module 2 §3.1–3.4.

Step 1 — Set up and solve the equation

The cell is a perfect absorber, so it removes nutrient at the Smoluchowski rate. If the droplet stays well mixed the concentration is uniform, and the problem collapses to one ordinary differential equation:

$$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \qquad k = 4\pi D_{\rm nut}\,a$$
droplet volume; capture rate constant
$$V\frac{dC}{dt} = -kC \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dC}{dt} = -\frac{C}{\tau} \quad\text{with}\quad \tau = \frac{V}{4\pi D_{\rm nut}\,a}$$
$$\int_{C_0}^{C}\frac{dC'}{C'} = -\int_0^t\frac{dt'}{\tau} \quad\Rightarrow\quad C(t) = C_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
separate, integrate, apply C(0) = C0
$$\frac{C}{C_0} = 0.1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t_{90} = \tau\ln 10 = 2.303\,\tau$$
90 % gone
C0 cancels. Because the decay is exponential, the time to lose 90 % does not depend on how much nutrient was there to start with. Saying so shows you understood what you solved.

Step 2 — Get τ two independent ways

Both routes need the NUTRIENT’s diffusion coefficient. The nutrient is a far smaller molecule than the conjugate, which is why rnut is given.

Route A — uses the measurementRoute B — uses none of it
Find Dnut\(D_{\rm nut} = D_{\rm conj}\,\dfrac{r_p}{r_{\rm nut}}\)
Scale the measured conjugate D by the radius ratio: \(D\propto 1/r\) at fixed η and T.		\(D_{\rm nut} = \dfrac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta\,r_{\rm nut}}\)
Straight from the datasheet viscosity and the temperature. Touches nothing measured.
Then τ\(\tau = \dfrac{V}{4\pi D_{\rm nut}\,a}\)\(\tau = \dfrac{3\eta\,r_{\rm nut}\,V}{2a\,k_BT}\)
the same thing, in closed form
Reconciling the routes. They are algebraically identical: put Dconj = kBT/(6πηrp) into Route A and you get Route B. So they agree exactly when your measurement is good, and any gap between them IS the error in your measured D. A 5 % gap, correctly blamed on the tracking measurement, is a complete reconciliation.

Step 3 — Put the numbers in

$$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi(40\ \mathrm{µm})^3 = 2.681\times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{µm^3}$$
$$\text{Route A: } D_{\rm nut} = 20.11\times\frac{7.28}{0.3} = 488.0\ \mathrm{µm^2/s}$$
$$\text{Route B: } D_{\rm nut} = \frac{(1.381\times10^{-23})(310)}{6\pi(1.551\times 10^{-3})(0.3\times10^{-9})} = 488.0\ \mathrm{µm^2/s}$$
✓ they agree
$$\tau = \frac{2.681\times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{µm^3}}{4\pi\,(488.0\ \mathrm{µm^2/s})(3.0\ \mathrm{µm})} = 14.57\ \mathrm{s}$$
$$t_{90} = 14.57\times\ln 10 = 33.6\ \mathrm{s}$$
Answer. 90 % of the nutrient is gone in about 34 seconds

Step 4 — Was the well-mixed assumption legitimate?

The model assumed a uniform concentration. That only holds if the droplet re-mixes by diffusion much faster than the cell eats through it. Compare the two timescales:

$$t_{\rm mix} = \frac{R^2}{6D_{\rm nut}} \qquad \tau = \frac{V}{4\pi D_{\rm nut}\,a}$$
diffuse across the droplet; deplete it
$$\frac{t_{\rm mix}}{\tau} = \frac{R^2}{6D}\times\frac{4\pi D a}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3} = \frac{a}{2R}$$
D cancels: pure geometry
$$\frac{a}{2R} = \frac{3.0}{80} = 0.0375$$
mixing is ~27× faster than depletion → well mixed holds
The ratio is the cell radius over the droplet diameter, and nothing else. Mixing wins whenever the cell is small compared with its droplet, which is true for every dataset in the class. Showing that D drops out symbolically, and then stating the verdict, is the complete answer; two numbers compared side by side are not.
Common mistakes
  • ≈ 13× the right t90 (422 s on this dataset). Dropped the 4π. The capture ceiling is 4πDa.
  • ≈ 2× the right t90 (67 s on this dataset). Diameter used where a radius belongs. Both a and R are radii.
  • ≈ 24× the right t90 (814 s on this dataset). Used the CONJUGATE’s D for the nutrient. The nutrient is a far smaller molecule; scale D by r_p/r_nut, or compute it from Stokes–Einstein.
  • ≈ 0.3× the right t90 (10 s on this dataset). τ ln 2 instead of τ ln 10. 90 % gone means C/C0 = 0.1, so t = τ ln 10.
  • ≈ 0.75× the right t90 (25 s on this dataset). Volume as πR³. A sphere’s volume is (4/3)πR³.
  • No ruling on the well-mixed assumption. The question asks whether the model was legitimate; the a/2R ratio answers it in one line.
