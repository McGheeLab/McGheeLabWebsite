1.1 · A concentration is a census

Week 1 ended on a distinction worth repeating, because this entire week is built on it: one particle has no findable position — many particles have a countable distribution. Section 2.4's probability ring told you where a single walker might be. This week we stop apologizing for that and cash it in: put enough walkers in the box and the maybes become a measurement.

Divide space into bins of width \(\Delta x\) and simply count. The count per bin, per unit length, is the concentration \(C(x)\) — the constants box has carried it since Week 1 (\(C\), number per volume) without ever saying what it really is: a census of random walkers. No walker knows it is part of a field. The field is our bookkeeping, and it is only smooth when the counts are large: counting statistics puts \(\pm\sqrt{N}\) noise on every bin, so a bin holding 25 walkers flickers by ±20%, while a bin holding a million flickers by ±0.1%. A drop of water holds ~10²² molecules — which is why the "concentration field" of chemistry feels like a continuous substance rather than the head-count it actually is.

The two views, one last time. Single particle → probability cloud (Week 1). Many particles → concentration field (this week). They are the same mathematical object: the census of a billion walkers traces exactly the probability cloud of one. Every equation this week is therefore secretly a statement about probabilities — which is why it will apply to a single transcription factor just as well as to a mole of oxygen.

Watch it happen below. Same walkers as Week 1, same blind staggering — the only thing that changes across the three buttons is how many there are, and therefore whether the census is noise or a field.

  1. Start at a handful: the histogram is garbage — bins flicker between 0, 1, 2. No field to speak of.
  2. Switch to a crowd: a lumpy but recognizable hill appears, jittering by roughly ±√N per bin. Check the "bin flicker" stat against the √N prediction.
  3. Switch to a field: the same census drawn as a smooth curve — this is what every "concentration profile" in every textbook actually is. Watch it spread and flatten: that evolution is Tab 2's law.

1.2 · Counting makes a current — Fick's first law, measured

Now stand a tollbooth in the middle of the box and count traffic. Every walker that crosses left-to-right is +1; right-to-left is −1. If the census is flat, enormous traffic crosses in both directions — and the net is zero, because the two directions are statistically identical. Nothing about a random walk prefers a side.

Now tilt the census: keep the left end crowded and the right end sparse. Each individual walker is exactly as blind as before — nobody smells the gradient, nobody is pushed. But there are simply more walkers on the left available to stumble rightward than walkers on the right available to stumble leftward. The two huge one-way currents no longer cancel, and their small difference is a steady net flux \(J\) — molecules per area per second, flowing down the gradient. Run the sim first: the tollbooth measures gross traffic in the thousands per second and a net of barely a tenth of that. Diffusive flux is a rounding error between two torrents — that is why it is so easy to bias (Week 9's flow) and so impossible to stop.

How big is the net? Walkers within one step \(\delta\) of the booth cross within one step-time \(\tau\), half of them toward it. Left side sends \(\tfrac12 C_L\,\delta\) per \(\tau\); right side sends back \(\tfrac12 C_R\,\delta\). The difference, per unit time:

eq. 2.1.1 $$J \;=\; \frac{\delta}{2\tau}\left(C_L - C_R\right) \;=\; \frac{\delta^2}{2\tau}\,\frac{C_L - C_R}{\delta} \;\;\xrightarrow{\ \delta\to 0\ }\;\; -\frac{\delta^2}{2\tau}\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}$$

And there is the payoff Week 1 promised. Recall §2.3's exact words when we defined \(D \equiv \delta^2/2\tau\): "we split off a 2 so that this D will match, with no stray factors, the D that appears in Fick's flux law next week." It is next week. The factor drops in perfectly:

eq. 1.3.5 — Fick's first law, from Week 1 $$J \;=\; -D\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}$$

This is Fick's first law (Adolf Fick, 1855 — a physiologist, measuring salt in tubes, thirty years before anyone believed in molecules). And you have already used it: it sat inside Einstein's derivation in Week-1 §3.3 as the diffusive half of the flux standoff, wearing this same equation number. Fick found it empirically; the random walk explains it; last week's Einstein argument shows it carries the same \(D\) as \(\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt\). One coefficient, three jobs.

Nothing flows. Everything hops. A gradient is not a force. No molecule feels a pull toward low concentration (compare Week-1 §3.3, where a real force built a real Boltzmann pile-up). Fick's law is pure statistics: more walkers here than there, so more hop from here to there. If you internalize one sentence this week, make it this one — it is what makes diffusion free, unstoppable, and utterly indifferent to what the cell "wants."
Deeper: why the minus sign, and what J's units hide

The minus sign encodes "downhill": where \(C\) falls with \(x\), \(\partial C/\partial x < 0\) and \(J > 0\) — flux runs toward sparse territory. In 3D the law reads \(\vec J = -D\nabla C\) with \(J\) in #/(m²·s): molecules crossing a unit area per second. Our 1D cartoon counts crossings of a point, so its \(J\) is #/s — the same physics, one integration over the booth's area short of the full units. Sources use both without comment; HW1 will not be so kind about it.

  1. Start on flat: gross traffic is huge (hundreds/s each way), net hugs zero. Flux is bookkeeping, not force.
  2. Switch to gradient: same blind walkers, but now the booth's net settles onto \(-D\,\partial C/\partial x\). Check the measured-vs-predicted ratio — it should ride near 1.
  3. Double D. The net flux doubles: steeper price per gradient. The gross traffic barely changes character — only the imbalance scales.

1.3 · Steady state: nature's straight line

Fick's first law becomes a design tool the moment the situation stops changing. Pin the concentration at two ends — a reservoir of \(C_L\) on the left, \(C_R\) on the right, like oxygen-rich tissue fluid on one side of a membrane and an oxygen-consuming cell on the other — and wait. Whatever profile you start with, the channel relaxes to the only shape that can hold still: since no molecules pile up anywhere in steady state, the flux must be the same at every station, and by eq. 1.3.5 a constant \(J\) demands a constant slope. The steady profile is a straight line:

eq. 2.1.2 $$C(x) = C_L + \left(C_R - C_L\right)\frac{x}{L}, \qquad J = D\,\frac{C_L - C_R}{L} \quad\text{(steady state)}$$

Read the flux formula as a recipe: to move material faster across a gap, raise the concentration difference, raise \(D\), or — the lever biology pulls hardest — shrink \(L\). This one formula is why capillaries sit within ~20 µm of nearly every cell you own (Week 11), why the synaptic cleft is 20 nanometers (Q6 below), and why the alveolar wall is thinner than a red blood cell (Week 13). Evolution cannot change \(D\); it relentlessly shortens \(L\).

Oxygen through a membrane: put eq. 2.1.2 to work
  1. Write what you know. A cell membrane region of thickness \(L = 5\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) (a generous tissue gap) separates fluid at \(C_L = 60\ /\mu\mathrm{m}^3\) of dissolved O₂ from a consuming interior held near \(C_R \approx 0\). Dissolved-gas \(D \approx 2000\ \mu\mathrm{m^2/s}\) (constants box, the dissolved rung).
  2. Assemble the equation symbolically first. Steady state + two pinned ends is exactly eq. 2.1.2's setup:
    \(J = D\,\dfrac{C_L - C_R}{L}\)
  3. Plug in, watching units.
    \(J = 2000\ \tfrac{\mu\mathrm{m^2}}{\mathrm{s}} \times \dfrac{60 - 0\ /\mu\mathrm{m^3}}{5\ \mu\mathrm{m}} = 24{,}000\ \tfrac{\#}{\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s}}\)
  4. Sanity-check the scale. A mitochondrion's surface is a few µm² — this gap can feed it tens of thousands of O₂ molecules per second, no pump required. Steady-state diffusion across short gaps is not the bottleneck of life; it is the free infrastructure everything else is built on.
\(J = 2.4\times10^{4}\) molecules per µm² per second — set entirely by \(D\), \(\Delta C\), and \(L\). Halve the gap, double the delivery.
  1. Start empty and watch the channel fill: the profile bows, straightens, and locks onto the line. The three station fluxes disagree during filling — and agree in steady state. That agreement is the definition of steady.
  2. Restart full: a totally different beginning, the same final line. Steady state has no memory — that is why eq. 2.1.2 needs no initial condition.
  3. Now double D and confirm the profile does not change — only the flux does. The shape is geometry; the rate is physics.

2.1 · Watch a cloud obey

Tab 1 pinned concentrations and got steady answers. Now pin nothing: release a puff of walkers at one point — histamine leaving a mast cell, dye touching water, neurotransmitter dumped into a cleft — and just watch the census. Run the sim below first, before any equation. You will see a spike soften into a hill, the hill into a dome, the dome into a plain, while the σ² readout climbs in a dead-straight line. The cloud is obeying something. This section catches the law red-handed.

No new physics is needed — only Tab 1's two ideas run together. Watch one census bin. Fick's first law (eq. 1.3.5) sets the traffic across its left wall and its right wall; the bin's count changes by whatever the difference fails to cancel: \(\partial C/\partial t = -\,\partial J/\partial x\) — molecules are conserved, so a bin only gains what its neighbors lose. Substitute eq. 1.3.5 for \(J\) and the flux vanishes from the story:

eq. 2.2.1 $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} \;=\; -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x} \;=\; D\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2} \qquad\text{(in 3D: } \partial C/\partial t = D\nabla^2 C\text{)}$$

This is Fick's second law — the diffusion equation, arguably the most reused equation in all of science (heat, ink, neutrons, house prices…). Read it as a sentence: concentration grows where the profile is curved upward, shrinks where it bulges. \(\partial^2 C/\partial x^2\) is curvature; diffusion is nature's relentless smoothing filter, filling dips and shaving peaks until nothing curves. That is why the spike must become a plain — and why the straight lines of §1.3 were the only shapes that could stand still (zero curvature = zero change).

2.2 · The Gaussian is the atom of diffusion

The red curve that rode your histogram is the diffusion equation's most important solution — the response to a point release of \(N\) molecules, called the Green's function or fundamental solution:

eq. 2.2.2 $$C(x,t) = \frac{N}{\sqrt{4\pi D t}}\,e^{-x^2/4Dt} \qquad\text{(3D: } C(r,t) = \frac{N}{(4\pi D t)^{3/2}}\,e^{-r^2/4Dt}\text{)}$$

Look at what Week 1 becomes inside it. The exponent's characteristic width is \(\sigma = \sqrt{2Dt}\) — exactly §2.4's probability radius, and the histogram your drunkard built in §2.3 was this function all along. What is new is the prefactor: as the cloud widens like \(\sqrt t\), its peak must fall like \(1/\sqrt t\) (in 3D, like \(t^{-3/2}\) — brutally fast) because the same \(N\) molecules are being spread over more territory. Spreading is dilution. The capstone turns that observation into the reason your whole leg doesn't itch.

And the "two pulses" button demonstrated the property that makes eq. 2.2.2 an atom: the diffusion equation is linear, so solutions add. Any initial condition whatsoever can be built as a sum of point releases, and its future is the sum of their Gaussians. Every canonical solution you will ever use — the spreading pulse, the eroding step, the recovering hole below — is this one function, summed. One equation to learn, an entire catalogue for free.

Deeper (566): check eq. 2.2.2 actually solves eq. 2.2.1

Differentiate: \(\partial C/\partial t\) brings down \(\left(\frac{x^2}{4Dt^2}-\frac{1}{2t}\right)C\), while \(D\,\partial^2 C/\partial x^2\) gives \(D\left(\frac{x^2}{4D^2t^2}-\frac{1}{2Dt}\right)C\) — identical, term for term. Also confirm \(\int C\,dx = N\) at every instant (the Gaussian integral): diffusion moves molecules, never creates them. Ten minutes of calculus that converts eq. 2.2.2 from folklore to property.

2.3 · Filling the hole — Fick's second law with a stopwatch

Here is the same law run in reverse — and it happens to be one of cell biology's workhorse measurements. Fill a dish with fluorescent molecules, then flash a laser: every molecule inside a disk of radius \(w\) is photobleached — chemically blinded, still present, still diffusing, just dark. You have carved a hole in the visible census. Now watch: dark molecules wander out, bright ones wander in, and the hole fills. The technique is called FRAP — fluorescence recovery after photobleaching — and the recovery is pure eq. 2.2.1, running on the bleach hole as its initial condition.

What should the recovery time be? No new derivation needed — dimensional analysis owns this one, the Week-1 way: the only time you can build from a length \(w\) and a diffusion coefficient \(D\) is \(w^2/D\). The exact solution for a sharp circular bleach spot (Soumpasis 1983) merely supplies the prefactor for the half-recovery time:

eq. 2.2.3 $$\tau_{1/2} \;=\; 0.224\,\frac{w^2}{D}$$

The \(w^2\) is the fingerprint. Double the bleach spot and recovery takes 4× longer — if it does, diffusion is doing the filling; if recovery scales like \(w^1\) or doesn't depend on \(w\), something else (binding, flow, active transport) is running the show, and eq. 2.2.3's \(D\) would be a lie. This is why HW1's FRAP tab makes you bleach at several sizes before it lets you extract \(D\): the exponent test comes first, the number second. Practice the two-step discipline here, where nothing is graded.

From a stopwatch to D — the HW1 move, rehearsed
  1. Write what you measure. In the sim: bleach at \(w = 3\ \mu\mathrm{m}\), read the half-recovery stopwatch — say it shows \(\tau_{1/2} = 0.20\) s.
  2. Test the exponent before trusting the law. Re-bleach at \(w = 6\ \mu\mathrm{m}\):
    \(\dfrac{\tau_{1/2}(2w)}{\tau_{1/2}(w)} = \dfrac{0.224\,(2w)^2/D}{0.224\,w^2/D} = 4\)
    The stopwatch should read ≈ 0.80 s. If it does, the \(w^2\) law is earned — only now proceed.
  3. Rearrange the law symbolically for the unknown.
    \(\tau_{1/2} = 0.224\,\dfrac{w^2}{D} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; D = 0.224\,\dfrac{w^2}{\tau_{1/2}}\)
  4. Plug in each spot size separately, then average the D's.
    \(D = 0.224 \times \dfrac{(3\ \mu\mathrm{m})^2}{0.20\ \mathrm{s}} \approx 10.1\ \mu\mathrm{m^2/s}; \qquad D = 0.224 \times \dfrac{(6)^2}{0.80} \approx 10.1\ \mu\mathrm{m^2/s}\)
    Average the raw quantity at each size first — never average the sizes and then apply the law: \(\langle f(x)\rangle \neq f(\langle x\rangle)\). HW1 grades this habit.
\(D \approx 10\ \mu\mathrm{m^2/s}\), certified by a passed \(w^2\) test. Exponent first, number second — always.
  1. Bleach at w = 3 µm. Watch the hole fill and the recovery curve cross ½ — the stopwatch stops itself and drops a point on the τ½-vs-w chart.
  2. Bleach at 1.5, 3, and 6 µm. On the log–log chart the points fall on a slope-2 line: the exponent test, passed visually.
  3. Now change D and re-run one size: the point slides along, the slope stays 2. The exponent is geometry's; only the height is yours to move.

2.4 · When the law expires: the crowded cell

Everything above assumed the walker roams free water. Week-1 §5.2 already warned you, and left a debt we now pay: cytoplasm is 20–40% occupied by volume — ribosomes, filaments, organelles — and a real cell is closer to a pachinko machine than a swimming pool. Two distinct things go wrong, and they are worth separating because HW1 measures them separately:

  • D drops. Detours cost time. The long-time slope of the MSD is still linear, but with an effective \(D_{\rm eff} < D_0\) — this is the ÷4 "tissue crowding" factor the Week-1 bite ledger used, now with a mechanism. The dilute estimate \(D_{\rm eff}/D_0 \approx (1-\phi)/(1+\phi)\) you tested last week remains a decent first guess.
  • Worse: the exponent itself can fail. At high crowding, over the timescales a microscope actually watches, the MSD grows as \(\langle r^2\rangle \propto t^{\alpha}\) with \(\alpha < 1\) — anomalous subdiffusion (Höfling & Franosch 2013). Trapped in cages, released, trapped again: the walk remembers, and the memory bends the log–log slope below 1. When \(\alpha \neq 1\) there is no single D to report — eq. 2.2.1, eq. 2.2.2, and eq. 2.2.3 all quietly presuppose \(\alpha = 1\).

So the honest workflow — the one HW1's "crowded α" problem grades — is the FRAP discipline again, one level up: check the exponent before you fit the coefficient. Plot the MSD on log–log axes; read the slope; only if it is ≈ 1 are you allowed to divide by \(2dt\) and call the result \(D\).

Where this leaves the week's toolkit. Not broken — bounded. In dilute extracellular fluid, in the synaptic cleft, across a capillary wall: \(\alpha = 1\), use everything above with confidence. Deep in the cytoskeletal mesh, inside the nucleus, in membranes: measure \(\alpha\) first. Fick's laws are not wrong in a cell — they are local, valid exactly as far as the dilute assumption walks.
  1. Run φ = 0%: the log–log MSD is a clean slope-1 line and the measured α reads ≈ 1.0. Free diffusion, certified.
  2. Slide φ to 35–40%: the tracer's trail starts to show cages, Deff is cut in half, and α slips visibly below 1. The exponent — not just the coefficient — is giving way (real cytoplasm, which also binds and cages, pushes it much further).
  3. Read Deff/D₀ at φ = 30% and compare it to the ledger's ÷4. The capstone's crowding slider now has a mechanism behind it.

3.1 · The hungry sphere, revisited — measure first

Chemistry class taught you that reaction rates come from activation energies and rate constants in a table. Inside a cell there is a step before any of that: the two molecules have to find each other — by diffusion, because nothing else is on offer. Enzyme and substrate, transcription factor and its DNA site, histamine and its receptor: every one of those reactions runs at most as fast as diffusion can deliver partners. This tab computes that ceiling — the week's headline result.

  • The rate is proportional to \(a\) — not \(a^2\). Doubling the target's radius doubles the catch, despite quadrupling the surface. Whatever the mechanism is, it is not "more parking spaces."
  • A depletion halo forms: the census near the sphere is permanently hollowed out, relaxing to \(C(r) = C_\infty(1 - a/r)\). The absorber eats its neighborhood and lives off the supply line.

That last point deserves a slow look, because it is the mechanism behind the \(a\)-not-\(a^2\) mystery: the bottleneck is not the sphere's surface — arrivals at the surface are instant business — it is the diffusive supply through the spherical shells around it. A bigger sphere reaches deeper into the supply; area never enters. Biology exploits this shamelessly: a receptor does not need to be big to be fed, it needs the cell to be big — which is where Week 3 picks up.

  1. Write what you know. A sphere of radius \(a\) that eats every molecule that touches it. Diffusers with coefficient \(D\) at concentration \(C_\infty\) far away. Two facts the sim handed you: the halo stops changing (steady state, \(\partial C/\partial t = 0\)) and it looks the same in every direction (spherical symmetry, so \(C\) depends on \(r\) alone). Unknown: the function \(C(r)\) for \(r \ge a\).
  2. Count molecules through a shell. Draw the thin shell between \(r\) and \(r + dr\). In steady state nothing accumulates inside it, so whatever crosses the inner sphere per second must also cross the outer one: the total inward current \(I\) (molecules/s) is the same through every shell. The current is flux times area:
    \(I = 4\pi r^2\,J(r) = \text{const} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2 J\right) = 0\)
    That sentence is Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates — steady spherical supply is the whole content of eq. 2.3.1. Area grows as \(r^2\), so to carry the same current the flux must shrink as \(1/r^2\).
  3. Bring in Fick I. Flux is driven by the gradient, \(J = -D\,dC/dr\) (eq. 1.3.5). Substitute and drop the constants:
    \(\dfrac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\,\dfrac{dC}{dr}\right) = 0\)
  4. Integrate twice. The bracket is a constant — call it \(K\). Then \(dC/dr = K/r^2\), and one more integration gives
    \(C(r) = c_1 - \dfrac{K}{r}\)
    Two constants, \(c_1\) and \(K\), because we integrated twice. This is §3.3's general solution (eq. 2.3.2, with \(c_2 = -K\)) — the same \(1/r\) that gives gravity and Coulomb their potentials, for the same reason: something conserved is spreading through shells whose area grows as \(r^2\).
  5. Spend the constants on the boundaries. Far away the sea is undisturbed, \(C(r\to\infty) = C_\infty\), and the \(K/r\) term dies there, so \(c_1 = C_\infty\). At the surface every arrival is consumed on contact, so nothing is ever waiting there: \(C(a) = 0\). Then \(0 = C_\infty - K/a\), so \(K = C_\infty a\).
    \(C(r) = C_\infty - \dfrac{C_\infty a}{r} = C_\infty\left(1 - \dfrac{a}{r}\right)\)
    Notice where \(a\) entered: through the surface condition, as one power, into the integration constant. The sphere's area was never consulted. That is the \(a\)-not-\(a^2\) mystery, already solved.
  6. Read the curve back against the chart. \(C(a) = 0\) ✓ and \(C(\infty) = C_\infty\) ✓ by construction. In between: half-depleted at \(r = 2a\), 90% recovered only at \(r = 10a\). And the current that flows through every shell is
    \(I = 4\pi r^2 \cdot D\,\dfrac{C_\infty a}{r^2} = 4\pi D a\,C_\infty\)
    — independent of \(r\), proportional to \(D\) and to \(a\). That is the number the stats panel calls "prediction". Thursday stamps it eq. 2.3.6 and reads it as a rate constant, \(k_s = 4\pi D a\).
\(C(r) = C_\infty\,(1 - a/r)\) for \(r \ge a\) — a steady \(1/r\) depletion halo whose only length scale is the absorber's radius. §3.3 will file it as eq. 2.3.3; the current it carries, \(4\pi D a\,C_\infty\), is the week's headline in disguise.

3.2 · The two riddles: why D's add, why radii add

Week 1 closed with two riddles about the full encounter formula \(k_s = 4\pi(D_1+D_2)(a_1+a_2)\) and promised one-sentence answers. Here they are — then the sim proves them.

Riddle 1 — why do the diffusion coefficients add? Because what decides an encounter is not where either particle is, but the separation between them — and when two independent random walks are subtracted, their variances add (randomness never cancels; two staggering drunks close distance faster than one staggering toward a lamppost). The separation therefore performs a perfectly ordinary random walk with \(D_{rel} = D_1 + D_2\).

Riddle 2 — why do the radii add? Because "touching" happens when the centers are one radius-sum apart: two spheres of radii \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) make contact when their center-to-center distance is \(a_{rel} = a_1 + a_2\). In separation coordinates, the pair problem is §3.1's problem: one effective walker of diffusivity \(D_1+D_2\), one fixed absorbing sphere of radius \(a_1+a_2\).

The cleanest way to see both answers is a change of camera, and the sim below is that camera: "imagine we have a camera fixed to one of the particles… to our perspective this particle is not moving, however other particles seem to be moving relatively faster." Ride the camera and watch both riddles resolve: the pinned particle sits at center wearing the combined radius \(a_1+a_2\) as a contact zone, while its partner staggers with the combined \(D_1+D_2\) — measured live on the chart from an ensemble of hidden pairs.

One change of coordinates buys the whole problem. This move — trade two diffusing bodies for one diffusing separation — is why §3.1's single-sphere result is not a toy but the general answer. It also tells you when to be careful: pin the DNA (it barely moves), and \(D_{rel} \approx D_{\rm TF}\); but for two small molecules meeting in solution, forgetting to add the D's undercounts encounters by up to 2×.

3.3 · The derivation, line by line: eq. 2.3.1 → eq. 1.5.1

Now we earn §3.1's measurement, one numbered line at a time. The camera trick has already reduced everything to one question: a fixed absorbing sphere of radius \(a_{rel}\) sits in a sea of diffusers (\(D_{rel}\)) held at \(C_\infty\) far away — what is the steady arrival rate? Marian Smoluchowski asked exactly this in 1916, wanting to know how fast colloid particles coagulate; his answer now sets the speed limit for every reaction in your cells.

Step 1 — steady state. §3.1 showed the halo stops changing: around the sphere the census is time-independent and spherically symmetric, so Fick's second law (eq. 2.2.1, in spherical coordinates) collapses to

eq. 2.3.1 $$\nabla^2 C(r) = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\,\frac{dC}{dr}\right) = 0$$

Step 2 — the general solution. Integrate twice (the \(1/r\) is the same harmonic profile that gives you gravity and Coulomb potentials — steady spherical supply problems all share it):

eq. 2.3.2 $$C(r) = c_1 + \frac{c_2}{r}$$

Step 3 — boundary conditions buy the constants. Far away the sea is undisturbed: \(C(r\to\infty) = C_\infty\), so \(c_1 = C_\infty\). And at the surface the condition is exact: the concentration must be 0, because they react on contact — every walker that touches is consumed, so \(C(a_{rel}) = 0\), giving \(c_2 = -C_\infty a_{rel}\). Flag that surface condition — "reacts on contact, never saturates, never lets go" — it is an idealization with an expiration date, and Tab 5 breaks it.

eq. 2.3.3 $$C(r) = C_\infty\left(1 - \frac{a_{rel}}{r}\right)$$

Pause: this is exactly the halo you measured in §3.1's profile chart. The derivation has already earned its first check-mark.

Step 4 — Fick's first law turns the profile into traffic. Eq. 15, the same law that ran the tollbooth, evaluated on the gradient of eq. 2.3.3:

eq. 2.3.4 $$J(r) = -D_{rel}\,\frac{dC}{dr} = -D_{rel}\,C_\infty\,\frac{a_{rel}}{r^2}$$

Step 5 — evaluate at the surface. At \(r = a_{rel}\):

eq. 2.3.5 $$J(a_{rel}) = -\,\frac{D_{rel}\,C_\infty}{a_{rel}}$$

Step 6 — from flux to a rate constant. Define the interaction rate as the total flow over the target's surface per unit ambient concentration, \(k_s = JA/C_\infty\) with \(A = 4\pi a_{rel}^2\):

eq. 2.3.6 $$k_s = \frac{J\,A}{C_\infty} = \frac{J}{C_\infty}\,4\pi a_{rel}^2 = -\,4\pi D_{rel}\,a_{rel}$$

(The minus sign is bookkeeping: radially inward flux is negative in our convention. The encounter rate is its magnitude — the notes drop the sign in the final line, and so do we.) Substitute the camera trick's \(D_{rel} = D_1+D_2\), \(a_{rel} = a_1+a_2\):

eq. 1.5.1 again — previewed in Week 1, earned here $$\boxed{\;k_s = 4\pi\,(D_1+D_2)\,(a_1+a_2)\;}\qquad \left[\mathrm{m^3/s}\right]$$

The Smoluchowski encounter rate — the week's summit. Multiply by a concentration and you get events per second: \(\text{rate} = k_s\,c\). Look back at what the derivation used: one steady-state solution (§1.3's logic in spherical dress), one application of Fick I (§1.2), one change of frame (§3.2). Every ingredient was measured in a sim before it was spent here. And notice what the result reproduces from §3.1: rate \(\propto D\) ✓, \(\propto a\) not \(a^2\) ✓ (the \(a^2\) of the area was eaten by the \(1/a\) of eq. 2.3.5 — supply, not surface), halo \((1-a/r)\) ✓.

Deeper (566): the Berg–Purcell clock — how long must a receptor count?

3.4 · Expiration dates: what reactions and flow do to a cloud

The week's toolkit is nearly complete: gradients (eq. 1.3.5), clouds (eq. 2.2.1–2.2.2), encounters (eq. 1.5.1). Two real-world ingredients remain, and each contributes one number that decides who wins. Both were promised in Week 1's "assumptions" table; both are graded in HW1's map problem; and together they answer the capstone's question — why the bite stays local.

Ingredient 1: the molecule doesn't live forever. Histamine is destroyed by enzymes; ATP is consumed; a signal is cleared — say with mean lifetime \(\tau\). A molecule diffuses while it lasts, so the farthest it typically gets is Week 1's probability radius evaluated at its own funeral, \(\sqrt{2D\tau}\) per axis. For a continuously releasing source, the steady census becomes eq. 2.3.3's supply profile with an exponential guillotine — and the guillotine's length scale is the week's fourth famous number:

eq. 2.3.7 $$\lambda = \sqrt{D\,\tau} \qquad\Big(\text{steady point source with clearance: } C(r) \propto \tfrac{1}{r}\,e^{-r/\lambda}\Big)$$

Without clearance, a steady source's \(1/r\) tail reaches absurdly far (it is gravity's shape — it never gives up). With clearance, everything beyond a few \(\lambda\) is exponentially erased. Reaction plus diffusion = automatic locality, and \(\lambda\) is the radius of "here": it is why a synapse's neurotransmitter stays in its own cleft, why morphogen gradients in embryos have crisp ranges, and why your knee will never hear about your ankle's histamine.

Ingredient 2: sometimes the medium moves. Blood flows, cytoplasm streams, interstitial fluid seeps. Advection carries a molecule a distance \(vt\) while diffusion covers \(\sqrt{2Dt}\); over a distance \(L\), the ratio of the two transport rates is the Péclet number:

eq. 2.3.8 $$\mathrm{Pe} = \frac{L\,v}{D} \qquad \mathrm{Pe}\ll1:\ \text{diffusion wins} \quad\big|\quad \mathrm{Pe}\gg1:\ \text{flow wins}$$

Pe grows with \(L\) — that is the punchline. At the micron scale of a cell (\(L\sim10\ \mu\mathrm{m}\), \(v\sim\) µm/s if anything), Pe ≪ 1: flow is irrelevant, diffusion is free and instant, and nothing needs plumbing. At the centimeter scale of your body, diffusion would take months (Week 1's derby) — so you run a pump: in a capillary (\(L\sim500\ \mu\mathrm{m}\), \(v\sim500\ \mu\mathrm{m/s}\), \(D\sim500\ \mu\mathrm{m^2/s}\)), Pe ≈ 500. Your circulatory system exists because Pe grows with L — evolution's answer to the √t catastrophe. (Where transport hands off to binding at a surface, a sibling dimensionless number — the Damköhler number — takes over as referee; that is Week 3's opening move.)

4.2 · One molecule, one chance

Before any formula: put one molecule in the bite pocket and follow it. It has no destination and no sense of direction — it is the same drunkard from Week 1, now in three dimensions, and somewhere 30 µm away there is a cell that will consume it on contact. Two things can happen. It finds the cell. Or it doesn't, ever.

That second option is the one worth staring at. There is no wall out there, nothing pushing the molecule away — and still, most releases end with the molecule simply gone, wandering outward without ever touching anything. Note that the panel never declares a molecule "escaped": it cannot, and neither can you. Molecules that leave the frame are still in the simulation and still in the statistics, because "it is not coming back" is a claim about three-dimensional random walks, not an event you can watch happen.

4.3 · Why it is \(a/r\) — and why \(D\) is nowhere in it

The tally has settled on about 0.167 at \(a = 5\) µm, \(r = 30\) µm. That is \(5/30\). Before deriving it, deal with the thing that looks like a misprint: the diffusion coefficient is not in the answer.

What would it even mean for \(D\) to cancel? Slow the molecule down and every single trial takes longer — the sim shows you that directly. But slowing it down does not change where it goes. A random walk with a small step and a random walk with a large step trace out the same set of paths; one just takes more steps to do it. Capture-or-escape is a question about the path, and \(D\) only sets the clock the path is traversed on. So: \(D\) decides when. Geometry decides whether. Hold on to that sentence — it is the difference between §4.3 and §4.4.

Step 1 — ask the question as a field. Define \(u(r)\) = the probability that a molecule released at distance \(r\) from the cell's centre is eventually captured. Here is the trick that makes it solvable: a molecule at \(r\) takes one unbiased step, so its chance of capture must be the average of the chances at all the places one step could land it. A function equal to its own spherical average has zero Laplacian. That is all Laplace's equation ever says:

eq. 2.3.1 again — same operator, new unknown $$\nabla^2 u(r) = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\,\frac{du}{dr}\right) = 0$$

Look carefully at what just happened. In §3.3 we solved this exact equation for a concentration. Now we are solving it for a probability. Same operator, same general solution (eq. 2.3.2), completely different physical object — and that is why the answers look like each other.

Step 2 — the boundary conditions, which are now about fates, not fluxes. A molecule that starts already touching the cell is captured with certainty, so \(u(a) = 1\). A molecule released infinitely far away has no chance at all, so \(u(\infty) = 0\). Feed those into \(u = c_1 + c_2/r\): the second condition kills \(c_1\), the first gives \(c_2 = a\).

eq. 2.4.1 $$\boxed{\;P_\infty = \frac{a}{r}\;}\qquad\text{(probability of ever being captured; exact, unbounded 3D)}$$

At \(a = 5\) µm and \(r = 30\) µm that is \(1/6\) — the number the tally converged on, with no fitting and no fudge factor. Five molecules in six never touch the cell at all.

Why "never" is a theorem, not sloppy language (566)

It is tempting to think a molecule with infinite time must eventually hit anything. In two dimensions that is true: a 2D random walk is recurrent, and returns to any neighbourhood with probability 1. In three dimensions it is false. Pólya's theorem: the 3D lattice walk returns to its origin with probability only ≈ 0.34, and escapes to infinity otherwise. Space is simply too roomy — the surface area a walker must find grows as \(r^2\) while the walker's reach grows as \(\sqrt{t}\), and the walker loses.

This is why \(P_\infty < 1\) is not a modelling artefact you could remove with more patience. The 83% that wander off are gone in the strong sense. Every diffusive signalling system in your body is built on top of this fact, and pays for it by sending far more molecules than it needs.

Step 3 — put a clock on it. Eq. 2.4.1 is the score at the end of time, which no cell can wait for. The same problem solved with the time-dependent equation instead of the steady one gives the probability of having been captured by time \(t\) — and it is eq. 2.4.1 with a fading factor in front:

eq. 2.4.2 $$P(t) = \frac{a}{r}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{r-a}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\right)$$

Every symbol earns its place. The prefactor is eq. 2.4.1: the fraction that ever arrive. The \(\mathrm{erfc}\) is the fraction of those that have arrived yet, and its argument is the gap to be crossed, \(r-a\), measured in units of the diffusion length \(\sqrt{4Dt}\) — the same ratio that has organised every transient in this module. As \(t \to \infty\) the argument goes to zero, \(\mathrm{erfc}(0) = 1\), and we recover \(a/r\). Here at last is where \(D\) lives: in when, never in whether.

Step 4 — differentiate to get the thing we actually want. An encounter rate is arrivals per second, so differentiate \(P\) and multiply by the number of molecules in the bolus:

eq. 2.4.3 $$\dot n(t) = N_0\,\frac{dP}{dt} = N_0\,\frac{a}{r}\cdot\frac{r-a}{\sqrt{4\pi D t^{3}}}\; e^{-(r-a)^{2}/4Dt}$$

This is the capstone's answer to "what encounter rate do we expect for a given time." It starts at nothing (nobody has crossed the gap yet), rises, peaks at \(t = (r-a)^2/6D\) — set the derivative of \(t^{-3/2}e^{-(r-a)^2/4Dt}\) to zero and that falls straight out — and then decays as \(t^{-3/2}\), a long tail of stragglers.

Step 5 — and nothing waits forever. Real allergen is cleared: washed into lymph, chewed by proteases. Give each molecule a lifetime \(\tau\) and the steady problem picks up a sink term, \(\nabla^2 u = u/\lambda^2\) with \(\lambda = \sqrt{D\tau}\) — §3.4's reaction–diffusion length, arriving here as a probability screen:

eq. 2.4.4 $$P_\infty = \frac{a}{r}\,e^{-(r-a)/\lambda}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{D\tau} \quad \text{(eq. 2.3.7)}$$

Note what clearance does to the earlier claim: \(\lambda\) contains \(D\), so with clearance the odds finally do depend on how fast the molecule moves. A faster molecule covers more ground before it is destroyed. The \(D\)-independence of eq. 2.4.1 was a statement about an immortal walker, and immortality has an expiration date like every other assumption in this module.

Two pictures of the same arrival, and a coincidence that isn't one (566)

There is a second, lazier way to get the arrival rate: treat the bolus as the Gaussian cloud of eq. 2.2.2, evaluate it at the cell's position, and multiply by Smoluchowski's rate constant from eq. 1.5.1 — \(\dot n \approx 4\pi D a\,C(r,t)\). It gives a curve that peaks at \(r^2/6D\) = 15 s, while the exact first-passage result of eq. 2.4.3 peaks at \((r-a)^2/6D\) = 10.4 s. Different shapes.

And yet their time integrals are identical, both exactly \(N_0 a/r\): the cloud picture gives \(4\pi D a \times N_0/(4\pi D r)\), and the \(D\)'s cancel. So the two pictures disagree about the schedule and agree perfectly about the total. The reason is that the quasi-static picture ignores the depletion halo the cell digs around itself (eq. 2.3.3) — an error in the timing that integrates away. When you want a total, be lazy; when you want a clock, don't.

4.4 · Does it fire, and when?

One molecule has a 1-in-6 chance, which tells us nothing about whether a cell notices a bite. The mosquito did not send one molecule. Multiply:

eq. 2.4.5 $$\mu(t) = N_0\,P(t) = N_0\,\frac{a}{r}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{r-a}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\right)$$

\(\mu\) is the expected number of arrivals by time \(t\) — a mean, not a count. The actual count fluctuates, and because each molecule wanders independently of the others, the fluctuation has a known shape: \(N\) is binomial over \(N_0\) trials, which for large \(N_0\) and small \(P\) is Poisson with mean \(\mu\). So the cell fires when the count clears \(N_{th}\), and the probability of that is one minus the chance of falling short:

eq. 2.4.6 $$P(\text{fire by } t) = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{N_{th}-1} \frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^{k}}{k!}$$

That is the answer to "what is the probability the mast cell senses it," and it is worth noticing how much structure had to be built to even ask it: a geometry (eq. 2.4.1), a clock (eq. 2.4.2), a dose (eq. 2.4.5), and a decision rule (eq. 2.4.6). Tabs 1–3 could compute concentrations. Only now can we compute a verdict.

One loose end from §4.2 first. That sim let the whole cell swallow anything that touched it — a perfect absorber. Real cells are mostly inert membrane with tiny receptors scattered over them, and it seems obvious that a surface which is 99.7% deaf should catch far less. It doesn't:

eq. 2.4.7 $$k_s = 4\pi D a\,\cdot\,\frac{N_{\rm rec}\,a_s}{N_{\rm rec}\,a_s + \pi a}$$

with \(N_{\rm rec}\) receptors of radius \(a_s\) on a cell of radius \(a\). The correction factor is not \(N_{\rm rec}a_s^2/4a^2\) — not the area fraction — but a ratio of lengths, for the same reason §3.1's rate went as \(a\) and not \(a^2\): a molecule that arrives at the cell and finds inert membrane does not vanish. It rattles around in the depleted layer and tries again, dozens of times. Receptors do not need to cover the surface; they only need to be findable. 5.1 · Next week's headline, measured today — the receptor gets full

Every capture calculation this week leaned on one boundary condition — §3.3 flagged it in red: "the concentration must be 0 since they react on contact." §4.2's mast cell leaned on it hardest of all: we let it swallow ten billion arrivals without once running out of doors. The absorber never hesitates, never fills, never lets go. That bought us eq. 1.5.1, and for a hungry receptor in dilute ligand it is honest. But raise the concentration and watch what the sim below measures: the capture rate stops climbing. The receptor has a finite number of binding sites, each one busy for a while after it binds — and a busy site is not a boundary condition of zero, it is a closed door.

Measure it now, name it next week: the fraction of sites occupied, θ, traces a curve that rises linearly at low concentration (Smoluchowski's regime — delivery-limited) and saturates toward 1 at high concentration (chemistry's regime — the door, not the delivery, is the bottleneck). That curve is the Langmuir isotherm, next week's central equation, and it is how a cell reads a concentration: not by measuring arrivals, but by letting occupancy report it. Week 3 — "Binding: how a cell reads a concentration" — derives everything you deposit on this chart, from two rate constants, \(k_{on}\) and \(k_{off}\).

  1. At low c: capture rate ∝ c (this week's law holds) and θ stays small. The receptor is hungry and honest.
  2. Slide c up: the θ-vs-c point you deposit climbs and bends. Saturation — this week's boundary condition failing in real time, and next week's isotherm being born.
  3. Raise koff (impatient sites): saturation retreats to higher c. The curve's midpoint is set by koff/kon — memorize the shape, Week 3 gives it its name (Keq).
