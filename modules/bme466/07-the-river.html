1.1 · Viscosity is friction, counted

Where we are in the story. The bite happened a minute ago. The neutrophil of Weeks 3–6 is already in the dermis, gripping collagen. Somewhere upstream a T cell that has met mosquito saliva before is riding the blood. It leaves the heart at a few tenths of a metre per second and reaches the venule beside the bite at about a millimetre per second, near the wall. Who slowed it, and who moved it to the side? This module is the plumbing that did both.

Start with friction you can feel

Slide a book across a table. Your hand pulls forward; the table exerts a friction force backward. At a steady speed those forces balance. Your hand still does work, and that mechanical energy becomes heat at the interface. Push the book harder into the table and it becomes harder to slide.

The familiar sliding-friction law is \(F_f=\mu_k N\): a friction coefficient times the normal load. It gives us a starting picture: two things move relative to one another, their interaction resists that motion, and maintaining the motion costs work.

Now carry that picture into a fluid. Molecules, suspended particles and nearby walls interact at many different relative velocities. Instead of following one rubbing interface, we collect the resistance of many dynamic interactions into an average shear stress. Viscosity describes how that averaged resistance responds to the rate of shearing. The fluid law comes from this collective response; we do not apply \(F_f=\mu_k N\) to every molecule or cell.

Begin with view 1 below. Identify the relative motion and the force opposing it. In view 2, follow water’s molecular interactions; in view 3, follow honey’s sugar clusters; in view 4, add cell–fluid, cell–cell and cell–wall interactions. The plate reports their combined, averaged effect.

From one frictional contact to many dynamic interactions

In view 2 the top plate moves and the bottom plate stays still. Fluid next to each plate follows that plate (the no-slip condition), so fluid near the top moves faster than fluid near the bottom. Neighboring layers exchange momentum through molecular motion and intermolecular forces. The faster layer pulls the slower one forward; the slower layer pulls back. Maintaining that velocity difference costs work, which is dissipated as heat. This distributed internal friction is viscosity. Water already has it: suspended particles are not required.

Now add particles. Each particle moves relative to the surrounding fluid and experiences viscous drag. Particles in faster and slower regions move past one another, shearing fluid in the gaps between them. Near a wall, the particle and wall can also have different velocities, and the intervening fluid resists their relative motion. In a dense suspension, direct contacts and particle deformation can contribute too. These are the fluid’s dynamic frictional interactions: local resistance associated with relative motion. An interaction can transmit this resistance through fluid without the two solid surfaces touching.

What are we averaging? Imagine a plane separating a faster layer from a slower one. Many molecular and particle interactions transmit tangential force across that plane. Average that transmitted force over a representative area and time, then divide by area: \(\sigma_{xy}=\langle F_{x,\mathrm{transmitted}}\rangle/A\). This is the macroscopic shear stress. We average the transmitted response, rather than assigning every encounter a dry-friction coefficient and averaging those coefficients.

Blood carries deformable red cells in plasma. The plate feels the collective response of the plasma’s molecular interactions and the cells’ dynamic interactions with fluid, other cells and nearby walls. The viscous friction counted here is dynamic and depends on relative motion; it is not a collection of static holding forces. We describe its average with a viscosity. The bench illustrates the interactions and computes a continuum reference response; it does not resolve and sum individual microscopic encounters. §1.3 adds the extra physics of a cell network that can hold stress at rest.

Why does force per area equal viscosity times a velocity gradient?

Let \(x\) point along the plates and \(y\) across the gap. Write the fluid’s mean velocity along the plates as \(u(y)\). Here \(u\) is a velocity; call the plate’s accumulated displacement \(\Delta x\). The usual symbol for shear stress is \(\tau\), the same quantity this course writes as \(\sigma_{xy}\). We will use both names in the equation below.

  1. 1 · Why divide the force by area? Imagine doubling the plate’s area by placing two identical experiments side by side. Each patch of fluid has the same local motion and transmits the same average resistance. The total force doubles because twice as many patches contribute. Thus \(F\propto A\), and \(\tau=F/A\) measures the local, averaged resistance independently of how much plate we use. Here \(F\) is the magnitude of the tangential pull needed to maintain steady motion; the fluid exerts an equal opposing force on the plate.
  2. 2 · Why a difference in velocity? A faster layer transfers forward momentum to a slower layer and is pulled back in return. This resistance comes from their relative motion. If the entire fluid and both plates translate together at one velocity, neighboring layers have no mean velocity difference and there is no viscous shear stress. Absolute speed cannot set the shear resistance.
  3. 3 · Why divide that difference by distance? Local interactions couple nearby regions. If the mean velocity changes by \(\Delta u\) across a separation \(\Delta y\), the speed difference experienced over a small interaction distance \(\ell\) is approximately \((\Delta u/\Delta y)\ell\). Packing the same velocity change into a smaller gap makes nearby regions move past one another faster. The local measure is the velocity gradient, \(du/dy=\lim_{\Delta y\to0}\Delta u/\Delta y\). The derivative describes a smooth mean velocity field, averaged over many molecules.
  4. 4 · Why multiply the gradient by η? In the Newtonian regime, the averaged viscous resistance is proportional to the local relative velocity and therefore to this gradient: \(\tau\propto du/dy\). The proportionality coefficient is \(\eta\). It collects how strongly molecular motion, intermolecular forces and particle interactions transfer momentum and dissipate mechanical energy. Doubling the gradient doubles the stress in this regime. This linear response is a material law supported by experiment; it does not follow from the definition of stress alone.

Read the bench physically. In its steady, uniform fluid film, the bottom moves at zero and the top at \(U\), so \(u(y)=Uy/h\) and \(du/dy=U/h\). Halving \(h\) at fixed \(U\) doubles the relative velocity over the same small interaction distance, and therefore doubles the local viscous stress. Doubling \(A\) doubles how many regions transmit force, and therefore doubles \(F\), while leaving that local stress unchanged.

Week 6’s shear strain provides the same measure of relative motion. Using \(\Delta x\) for displacement,

eq. 6.3.5 again — shear strain, shear rate and the velocity gradient$$\gamma=\frac{\Delta x}{h}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\dot\gamma=\frac{U}{h}=\frac{du}{dy}$$

The two equalities now have different jobs: force per area defines stress; the Newtonian law connects that stress to the velocity gradient. For this positive-gradient plate experiment, written in magnitudes,

eq. 7.1.1 — the viscosity law$$\tau=\frac{F}{A}=\eta\frac{du}{dy}=\eta\dot\gamma$$

Viscosity is the link between the two sides. The plate measures the averaged force transmitted by many dynamic interactions. The gradient tells us how rapidly neighboring regions move past one another. The stress required per unit gradient is the viscosity. This is the same stress–gradient relation described in NASA Glenn’s viscosity explanation.

Reverse the shear and the signed stress reverses; the fluid’s traction on the driven plate opposes its motion. At zero velocity gradient this Newtonian viscous contribution vanishes, even though molecules continue moving and interacting. Blood’s cell network can also support stress at rest; that additional response enters in §1.3.

Week 6’s damper is this entire plate apparatus viewed from outside:

eq. 6.2.2 again — the damper$$F=\eta_d U,\qquad \eta_d=\frac{\eta A}{h}$$

The fluid property is \(\eta\); the apparatus’s damping coefficient \(\eta_d\) also includes its area and gap. Changing geometry changes the measured force without changing the Newtonian fluid’s viscosity.

Why pascal-seconds?

View 3 keeps the units attached to every measured quantity. Stress is force per area, in N/m² or Pa. Shear rate is speed per gap: (m/s)/m = s⁻¹. Dividing stress by shear rate therefore gives Pa/s⁻¹ = Pa·s, equivalently N·s/m² or kg/(m·s). These are the SI units of dynamic viscosity (BIPM, SI Brochure, Table 6).

Read it as “stress required per unit shear rate.” A Newtonian fluid of viscosity 1 Pa·s requires 1 Pa to shear at 1 s⁻¹, and 10 Pa at 10 s⁻¹. The second is a consequence of dividing by a rate; it is not a waiting time. A millipascal-second is 0.001 Pa·s. At 37 °C water is 0.69 mPa·s and plasma about 1.2 mPa·s. Set the bench to 1 s⁻¹: plasma requires 0.0012 Pa, or 0.12 µN on a 1 cm² plate.

For blood, \(\sigma_{xy}/\dot\gamma\) is called the apparent viscosity: the value a single Newtonian \(\eta\) would need to explain the measured force under those conditions. The bench’s high-shear, large-tube reference at haematocrit 0.45 is about 3.8 mPa·s, using the existing Pries fit; Rosenson’s healthy-adult study measured about 3.3 mPa·s at 100 s⁻¹. A single reading at rest cannot determine a viscosity: zero stress divided by zero rate cannot be evaluated, even though the fluid still has a viscosity.

Read the models at their stated scale. Water’s reference is 0.69 mPa·s at 37 °C. Derive eq. 7.1.1 from many local dynamic resistances

A physical model for the form of the law. Consider a representative area \(A\) across the layers. Model its averaged, dissipative coupling by an effective drag coefficient \(B\), acting across a representative interaction distance \(\ell\). This is a simplified Newtonian coupling model, not a calculation of individual molecular contacts.

  1. Local dynamic friction responds to relative velocity. In this linear model, the magnitude of the transmitted force is \(F=B\Delta u\), where \(\Delta u\) is the mean speed difference between the coupled regions. More area provides proportionally more interacting regions, so \(B\propto A\).
  2. Nearby regions sample the velocity gradient. Over the interaction distance \(\ell\), a smooth velocity field gives \(\Delta u\simeq\ell\,du/dy\). The same small region experiences a larger speed difference when the gradient is larger.
  3. Average per area and collect the interaction physics.
    $$\frac{F}{A}\simeq\underbrace{\frac{B\ell}{A}}_{\displaystyle\eta}\frac{du}{dy}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\tau=\eta\frac{du}{dy}$$
    The combination \(B\ell/A\) has units \((\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m})\,\text{m}/\text{m}^2=\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}\). Area cancels because the summed coupling \(B\) grows with area.
  4. Recover the plate measurement. For a steady, homogeneous Newtonian film with no pressure gradient, the transmitted shear stress is constant across the gap. Constant \(\eta\) then makes \(du/dy\) constant. With no slip, \(u(0)=0\) and \(u(h)=U\), giving \(u(y)=Uy/h\), \(du/dy=U/h\), and \(F=\eta A U/h\).
Force grows with the number of interacting regions (area) and with their local relative motion (velocity gradient). Viscosity summarizes the strength of their averaged dynamic coupling.

For positive viscosity, the mechanical power dissipated per fluid volume is \(\tau\,du/dy=\eta(du/dy)^2\ge0\). This is how the averaged dynamic resistance converts the plate’s work into heat.

The physical model explains why the Newtonian law has this form. It does not predict a liquid’s numerical viscosity from first principles. Those values require measurement or a microscopic theory; aggregation and nonlinear responses require a richer constitutive law.

Check yourself · the units tell a physical story

1. Two Newtonian liquids are sheared at 10 s⁻¹. One needs 0.01 Pa; the other needs 0.03 Pa. Which is more viscous, and by how much?

Answer

The viscosities are 1 and 3 mPa·s. The second liquid needs three times the stress at the same rate, so its viscosity is three times larger.

2. You double the plate area and measure twice the force. Has the viscosity changed?

Answer

No. Force per area is unchanged, as is shear rate. The damper coefficient doubles; the fluid viscosity stays the same.

3. To find viscosity, do we give every encounter a force μN and average the friction coefficients?

Answer

We average the tangential force transmitted across layers by their dynamic interactions, including molecular momentum transfer and viscous drag. That gives shear stress. Viscosity describes its dependence on shear rate; the dry-friction law μN does not supply the fluid law.

The bridge from friction to viscosity. One sliding interface introduces friction. A fluid contains many dynamic interactions at relative velocities. Their averaged tangential force per area is shear stress; viscosity relates that stress to shear rate. The next sections ask how this collective response changes in a tube, in a crowd, and near a wall.

Start with the velocity plot

The bench plots \(u(r)\), the axial velocity at distance \(r\) from the tube’s axis. The curve is highest and flat on the axis, then falls to zero at the wall. This is a parabolic profile. The moving dye and arrows use that same field: the wall stays still, nearby fluid moves slowly, and fluid near the centre moves fastest. The next steps explain the curve before using it to calculate a volume flow.

1 · Use a cylinder of fluid to find the velocity gradient

Consider an imaginary cylinder inside the fluid, radius \(r\), length \(L\). Pressure acts on its two circular ends. The inlet is \(\Delta P\) above the outlet, so the net downstream pressure force is \(F_P=\Delta P\,\pi r^2\). The slower fluid outside the cylinder pulls back on its curved surface, of area \(2\pi rL\). In steady, fully developed flow this fluid cylinder has no axial acceleration, so the forces balance:

$$\underbrace{\Delta P\,\pi r^2}_{\text{pressure push}}=\underbrace{\tau(r)\,2\pi rL}_{\text{viscous drag}}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\tau(r)=\frac{\Delta P}{2L}\,r$$

Here \(\tau(r)\) is the positive magnitude of the retarding shear stress. Its force on the inner fluid points upstream. The stress is zero on the axis and increases toward the wall: a larger core has more pressure-pushed end area relative to its sheared side area.

Now use §1.1’s Newtonian viscosity law locally. Velocity decreases as we move outward, so \(du/dr<0\). The positive drag magnitude is \(\tau(r)=-\eta\,du/dr\). Substitution gives

$$-\eta\frac{du}{dr}=\frac{\Delta P}{2L}\,r\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{du}{dr}=-\frac{\Delta P}{2\eta L}\,r$$

This explains the shape. The slope is zero at the centre and grows more negative in direct proportion to radius. Integrating a slope proportional to \(-r\) produces a parabola.

2 · Integrate from the stationary wall to obtain u(r)

No slip fixes the integration constant: \(u(R)=0\). Integrate from the wall \(R\) to the point \(r\), using \(s\) as the integration variable:

$$u(r)-u(R)=-\frac{\Delta P}{2\eta L}\int_R^r s\,ds=-\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta L}(r^2-R^2)$$
eq. 7.1.2 — the velocity field in a circular pipe$$u(r)=\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta L}\left(R^2-r^2\right)$$

At the wall the bracket is zero. On the axis it is \(R^2\), so \(u_{max}=\Delta P R^2/(4\eta L)\). Raising pressure makes the whole profile taller; increasing length or viscosity makes it shorter. At fixed pressure drop, length and viscosity, a wider pipe also has a higher central speed.

3 · Turn the velocity field into volume per second

One velocity does not determine the total flow. In a short time \(dt\), fluid crossing a tiny area \(dA\) advances a distance \(u(r)dt\), transporting a volume \(dV=u(r)dt\,dA\). Thus its contribution to volume flow is \(dQ=dV/dt=u(r)dA\).

Group points with the same velocity into a thin circular annulus. Its area is circumference times thickness, \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\). Therefore

$$dQ=u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr,\qquad Q=\int_0^R u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr$$

Read view 2 of the bench. The axis has the largest speed but vanishing annular area. At the wall the circumference is largest but velocity is zero. Intermediate annuli contribute most strongly. The flow-density plot includes the area weighting \(2\pi r\); the cyan band has a finite thickness and its reported flow is integrated across that band.

4 · Substitute the parabola and perform the integral

$$\begin{aligned}Q&=\int_0^R\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta L}(R^2-r^2)\,2\pi r\,dr\\[4pt]&=\frac{\pi\Delta P}{2\eta L}\int_0^R(R^2r-r^3)\,dr\\[4pt]&=\frac{\pi\Delta P}{2\eta L}\left[\frac{R^2r^2}{2}-\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^R\\[4pt]&=\frac{\pi\Delta P}{2\eta L}\left(\frac{R^4}{2}-\frac{R^4}{4}\right).\end{aligned}$$
eq. 7.1.3 — Hagen–Poiseuille’s flow law$$Q=\frac{\pi R^4\,\Delta P}{8\eta L}$$

Now divide the volume flow by the entire cross-sectional area to obtain the area-averaged velocity: \(\bar u=Q/(\pi R^2)=\Delta P R^2/(8\eta L)=u_{max}/2\). An equally spaced average of the values on the radial plot would miss the annular area weights.

Where the fourth power comes from. A larger radius provides an area proportional to \(R^2\), and its pressure-driven velocity scale also grows as \(R^2\). Together they give \(Q\propto R^2R^2=R^4\). Doubling radius gives four times the area and four times the mean speed: sixteen times the flow.

5 · Read the same result as hydraulic resistance

The resistance \(R_h\) is pressure drop per volume flow. The subscript distinguishes it from the tube radius \(R\):

eq. 7.1.4 — hydraulic resistance$$R_h=\frac{\Delta P}{Q}=\frac{8\eta L}{\pi R^4}$$

Its units are Pa·s/m³. Length and viscosity add resistance in direct proportion; radius reduces it through the fourth power. A 10% narrowing leaves \(0.9^4=0.6561\) of the original flow, a loss of about 34%, at the same \(\Delta P\), \(L\) and \(\eta\). This is why small changes in arteriole radius can strongly regulate flow.

What was assumed? Steady, fully developed, laminar flow of an incompressible Newtonian fluid in a straight, rigid, circular tube; constant viscosity and no slip at the wall. The derivation excludes entrance and pulsatile effects. Blood’s aggregation and yield stress need §1.3; cell-scale confinement needs §3.2. A plotted reference parabola does not establish those assumptions for every vessel.

Derive why a wider pipe gives two factors of R²

Rewrite the field with \(x=r/R\): \(u=u_{max}(1-x^2)\), where \(u_{max}\propto R^2\). An annulus has \(dA=2\pi R^2x\,dx\), so \(Q=u_{max}\,2\pi R^2\int_0^1(1-x^2)x\,dx\). The dimensionless integral equals \(1/4\), leaving \(Q\propto R^2R^2\). The first factor belongs to speed; the second belongs to area.

At fixed ΔP, L and η, radius changes both the velocity field and the area it flows through. Keeping only the area change would miss half of the radius dependence.

1.3 · Keep the force balance; change the fluid’s stress law

Use the same pipe and the same pressure force, \(F_P=\Delta P\,A\). In §1.2 we closed the force balance with Newtonian viscosity, \(\tau=\eta\dot\gamma\). Now give the fluid a yield stress: it can support a small shear stress without shearing. The pressure still sets the stress inside the pipe; the new relation between stress and shear rate changes the velocity field. We will derive that field again.

Blood motivates this change. At low shear, red cells can form reversible stacks called rouleaux, and aggregation increases the low-shear resistance. An ideal Bingham fluid captures a yield threshold and a flowing slope. It is a teaching model for this effect; actual blood also shear-thins, depends on its flow history, and becomes a cell-scale suspension in small vessels.

1 · Replace the constitutive relation

Keep \(\tau\ge0\) as a shear-stress magnitude and \(\dot\gamma\ge0\) as a shear-rate magnitude. A Newtonian fluid shears at every nonzero stress. The ideal Bingham fluid has two branches:

eq. 7.1.5 — Bingham’s law$$\begin{cases}\dot\gamma=0,&0\le\tau\le\tau_y,\\ \tau=\tau_y+\eta_p\dot\gamma,&\tau>\tau_y.\end{cases}$$

Here \(\tau_y\) is the yield stress and \(\eta_p\) is the plastic viscosity, the slope of the flowing branch. Its units remain Pa·s. For nonzero shear, the apparent viscosity is \(\tau/\dot\gamma=\eta_p+\tau_y/\dot\gamma\): the extra term matters most at small rates. At rest the stress can lie anywhere below yield; the intercept is the limit as flow begins, not a unique resting stress. Reversing the flow requires the corresponding signed branch; this section uses positive pressure drops.

The reference \(\tau_y=0.005\) Pa is the upper end of the 0.0015–0.005 Pa range at normal haematocrit quoted from Merrill’s measurements by Fedosov et al. (2011). It is an order-of-magnitude teaching reference, not a universal threshold for circulating blood. Real blood’s response is smoother and depends on aggregation and measurement history.

2 · The pressure force still gives exactly the same stress field

Choose the same imaginary fluid cylinder, radius \(r\) and length \(L\). Its end area is \(A=\pi r^2\); its sheared side area is \(2\pi rL\). Fully developed steady flow has no axial acceleration, so pressure force and retarding shear force balance:

$$\underbrace{\Delta P\,\pi r^2}_{\Delta P\,A}=\underbrace{\tau(r)\,2\pi rL}_{\text{side traction}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\tau(r)=\frac{\Delta P}{2L}r.$$

This equation did not use viscosity. Pressure drop and geometry determine the stress. What changes is how much velocity gradient a given stress produces. In the pipe, speed decreases outward, so \(\dot\gamma=-du/dr\). Invert the new law:

$$-\frac{du}{dr}=\begin{cases}0,&\dfrac{\Delta P\,r}{2L}\le\tau_y,\\[5pt]\dfrac{1}{\eta_p}\left(\dfrac{\Delta P\,r}{2L}-\tau_y\right),&\dfrac{\Delta P\,r}{2L}>\tau_y.\end{cases}$$

Compare this directly with §1.2’s \(-du/dr=\Delta P\,r/(2\eta L)\). Above yield, a stress \(\tau_y\) is needed before the rest drives shear. Below yield, the velocity gradient is zero.

3 · Locate the boundary between plug and sheared fluid

The stress is zero on the axis and increases toward the wall. Where it first reaches \(\tau_y\), the fluid starts to shear. For a flowing tube, \(\tau(r_p)=\tau_y\) gives

eq. 7.1.6 — the plug boundary, when the wall yields$$r_p=\frac{2\tau_y L}{\Delta P},\qquad \frac{r_p}{R}=\frac{\tau_y}{\tau_w},\qquad \tau_w=\frac{\Delta P\,R}{2L}.$$

If \(\tau_w\le\tau_y\), no part of the tube yields. No slip at the fixed wall then makes the entire velocity field zero. The threshold is

$$\Delta P_{crit}=\frac{2\tau_y L}{R};\qquad \Delta P\le\Delta P_{crit}\ \Longrightarrow\ u(r)=0.$$

At zero pressure, use this stopped branch rather than dividing by \(\Delta P\) to find a plug radius. A formal \(r_p\ge R\) means the whole tube is unyielded.

4 · Integrate inward from the wall again

For \(\Delta P>\Delta P_{crit}\), the annulus \(r_p\le r\le R\) shears. Use the same boundary condition as before, \(u(R)=0\), and integrate the new gradient. With \(s\) as a dummy radius,

$$\begin{aligned}u(r)-u(R)&=-\frac{1}{\eta_p}\int_R^r\left(\frac{\Delta P}{2L}s-\tau_y\right)ds\\[4pt]&=\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R^2-r^2)-\frac{\tau_y}{\eta_p}(R-r).\end{aligned}$$

The first term is the old parabola with \(\eta=\eta_p\). The second reduces the speed because some of the stress is needed to overcome the yield threshold. This expression applies only in the yielded annulus. Continuing it toward the axis would incorrectly predict a nonflat core.

5 · Join to the flat core

Inside \(r_p\), \(du/dr=0\), so velocity is constant. Continuity fixes it to the annulus velocity at \(r_p\). Substituting \(\tau_y=\Delta P\,r_p/(2L)\) simplifies the plug speed to

$$u_p=u(r_p)=\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R-r_p)^2.$$

The complete field for a flowing tube is therefore

$$u(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R-r_p)^2,&0\le r\le r_p,\\[8pt]\dfrac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R^2-r^2)-\dfrac{\tau_y}{\eta_p}(R-r),&r_p<r\le R.\end{cases}$$

No shear means no difference in neighboring velocities. It does not require velocity itself to vanish. The outer fluid carries the unyielded core along. Velocity and slope join continuously at \(r_p\), while the wall remains stationary. In the bench’s profile view, the same pressure drop gives the same stress field for both fluids, but a lower, flat-topped Bingham velocity field.

Recover §1.2. Set \(\tau_y=0\): then \(r_p=0\), the yield correction disappears, and \(u(r)=\Delta P(R^2-r^2)/(4\eta_p L)\). The Newtonian result is the zero-yield limit of the new field.
Derive the flow by adding the moving plug and the sheared rings

The volume weighting is unchanged: \(dQ=u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr\). Split the integral at the plug boundary:

$$\begin{aligned}Q&=\underbrace{\pi r_p^2 u_p}_{\text{moving plug}}+2\pi\int_{r_p}^{R}\left[\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R^2-r^2)-\frac{\tau_y}{\eta_p}(R-r)\right]r\,dr.\end{aligned}$$

Use \(x=r/R\), \(B=r_p/R\), \(\tau_y=\Delta P RB/(2L)\), and the Newtonian axis scale \(U_0=\Delta P R^2/(4\eta_p L)\). The dimensionless annulus speed is \(1-x^2-2B(1-x)\), while the plug speed is \((1-B)^2\). Thus

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{Q}{2\pi R^2U_0}&=\frac{B^2(1-B)^2}{2}+\int_B^1\left[(1-2B)x+2Bx^2-x^3\right]dx\\[3pt]&=\frac{B^2(1-B)^2}{2}+\left[\frac{1-2B}{2}x^2+\frac{2B}{3}x^3-\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_B^1\\[3pt]&=\frac14-\frac{B}{3}+\frac{B^4}{12}.\end{aligned}$$
$$Q=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\pi R^4\Delta P}{8\eta_pL}\left(1-\dfrac43B+\dfrac13B^4\right),&0\le B<1,\\[7pt]0,&\Delta P\le\Delta P_{crit}.\end{cases}$$

This is the Buckingham–Reiner result. The bracket is 1 at zero yield and 0 when the plug fills the tube. Because \(B\) itself depends on pressure, Q is no longer proportional to ΔP. View 3 plots that curve with the matched Newtonian line. The force balance has stayed the same throughout.

This reversible structure helps explain why blood has much greater apparent resistance at low shear and can show a small, solid-like yield response.

Rouleaux supply structure, not a new pressure force

Zero relative mean velocity does not mean zero microscopic motion. Inside the ideal plug, neighboring fluid layers share the same mean speed. This view follows that translation, leaving cell wandering and rotation visible. Diffusive fluctuations bring nearby cells into encounters; short-range attraction can hold them together, while contact forces rotate and slide their broad faces into compact stacks. As contacts accumulate, neighboring cells constrain one another’s motion. The animation accelerates this process; it is not a calibrated measurement of RBC diffusion or aggregation time.

Each red cell remains a separate deformable cell. Plasma macromolecules promote weak attractions between cells; low shear allows stacks and larger associations to persist. Relative motion can disrupt them. The suspension can therefore behave like a weak, reversible solid below a small yield stress: the network resists rearrangement even though its individual cells and surrounding plasma are not solidifying into one fused object.

Fedosov et al. (2011) reproduced reversible aggregation and a nonzero extrapolated yield stress in cell simulations with attraction, while the nonaggregating suspension had no extrapolated yield stress. Their comparison also shows why the existence and value of a true blood yield stress are difficult to measure. Here, “solid-like” refers to the ideal model’s ability to support stress without sustained shear; actual blood’s response depends on its history.

Primary simulation and experimental comparison: Fedosov et al., 2011, especially Reversible Rouleaux Formation and Yield Stress and Aggregation. The bench’s network law and clock are illustrative, not a reproduction of those simulations.

When is a yield-induced plug substantial?

Bring the structure into §1.3’s force balance. The pressure force is still ΔP·A, the wall shear stress is still \(\tau_w=\Delta P R/(2L)\), and the dimensionless comparison is

$$B=\frac{\tau_y}{\tau_w}=\frac{2\tau_yL}{\Delta P\,R},\qquad \frac{r_p}{R}=B\quad(0\le B<1).$$
  • \(B\ll1\): pressure-driven shear overwhelms the small yield term through most of the tube. The formal central core is tiny, so a yield-induced flat region may be negligible.
  • \(0<B<1\) and appreciable: a substantial unyielded core translates inside a sheared annulus. It is solid-like in shear, but can still move as a plug.
  • \(B\ge1\): even the wall stress is at or below yield. In the ideal rigid no-slip model, the whole tube is unyielded and stationary.

Thus, a large yield-induced plug needs an effective pressure gradient small enough relative to yield stress and vessel radius. Any nonzero yield stress produces a formal core in the ideal Bingham solution; “large enough to matter” is the useful distinction. A blunt measured blood profile can also reflect cell migration, shear thinning or pulsatility, so a flat profile alone does not prove rouleaux-induced yielding.

Hold the right quantity fixed. At fixed \(\Delta P/L\), increasing R raises \(\tau_w\) and reduces B. At fixed R, lowering ΔP or increasing L raises B. At fixed flow, pressure must readjust, so that is a different experiment. Small vessels also require cell-scale physics (§3.2).

For example, the declared fully structured state has \(\tau_y=5\) mPa. A 20 cm segment of radius 2 mm has \(\Delta P_{crit}=2\tau_yL/R=1\) Pa = 0.00750 mmHg. At twice this drive B = 1/2. These numbers show the scale of the ideal threshold; they do not establish that ordinary seated leg veins reach complete yield arrest.

Why a seated leg can develop venous stasis

Leg veins return blood toward the heart. Calf contraction compresses veins, and competent valves help direct the displaced blood toward the heart. During prolonged sitting, repeated calf-pump action is reduced; cramped positioning or compression can also impede venous return. Flow can slow and blood can pool, particularly in poorly washed-out regions around venous valves. A vein’s main lumen can still carry blood while a valve pocket has much slower exchange.

Low shear favors aggregation, and aggregation increases low-shear resistance: this is a useful mechanical feedback to connect with the bench. It is one contributor to the behavior of slow blood, not a requirement that all blood first become a stationary Bingham solid. The straight-tube solution does not resolve venous valves, compliant walls or muscle-driven transients.

Prolonged sitting and travel: CDC. Work/computer sitting association: Healy et al., 2010 (case-control study). Valve-pocket hypoxia under undisturbed flow: Hamer et al., 1981 (small human/dog physiology study; abstract verified).

Where DVT adds biology

A rouleaux stack is reversible cell aggregation; a venous thrombus involves coagulation and a fibrin-rich structure. Fibrinogen in plasma and polymerized fibrin in a clot have different roles. Slow flow and poor washout interact with the vessel lining, inflammatory cells, platelets and coagulation. This is the connection to the familiar Virchow framework: altered flow/stasis, vessel-wall changes and increased coagulability.

In a mouse model, restricting venous flow recruited innate immune cells and platelets and promoted fibrin formation; complete cessation of flow was not required. The clinical point is that prolonged leg immobility can create conditions favorable to DVT, with other risk factors influencing whether a thrombus forms. Reversing rouleaux does not establish that an existing thrombus has dissolved.

Primary mechanistic evidence: von Brühl et al., 2012, mouse flow-restriction model. This supports the distinction between rheological aggregation and coagulation; it is not a patient risk calculator.

Why height and seat fit belong in the story

Taller stature has been associated with higher VTE incidence in a large Swedish registry/co-sibling study. In the MEGA travel study, very tall travelers also had higher thrombosis risk; cramped legroom and venous compression were proposed explanations. The height subgroups were small, and short air travelers could also be affected when seat fit left their feet unsupported. Height matters in context, rather than through a universal rule that tall blood aggregates more.

A longer vertical column also has a larger hydrostatic head, \(\Delta P_h=\rho gh\). The height slider shows this effect. Hydrostatic pressure is not the same as the pressure head available to overcome friction. For an upward segment with rise h, the effective drive in this simplified balance is \(\Delta P_{eff}=P_{lower}-P_{upper}-\rho gh\). A stationary column can have a large pressure difference while gravity balances it and flow remains zero. Muscle pumping, valves, vessel compliance and posture determine actual venous return; body height is not substituted into the Bingham yield law.

Height association: Zöller et al., 2017 (registry/co-sibling study; abstract verified). Travel and seat-fit hypotheses: Cannegieter et al., 2006 (MEGA, full text). These studies establish associations, not a height-to-rouleaux causal law.
