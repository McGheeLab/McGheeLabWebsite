1.1 · Viscosity is friction, counted

Where we are in the story. The bite happened a minute ago. The neutrophil of Weeks 3–6 is already in the dermis, gripping collagen. Somewhere upstream a T cell that has met mosquito saliva before is riding the blood. It leaves the heart at a few tenths of a metre per second and reaches the venule beside the bite at about a millimetre per second, near the wall. Who slowed it, and who moved it to the side? This module is the plumbing that did both.

Start with friction you can feel

Slide a book across a table. Your hand pulls forward; the table exerts a friction force backward. At a steady speed those forces balance. Your hand still does work, and that mechanical energy becomes heat at the interface. Push the book harder into the table and it becomes harder to slide.

The familiar sliding-friction law is \(F_f=\mu_k N\): a friction coefficient times the normal load. It gives us a starting picture: two things move relative to one another, their interaction resists that motion, and maintaining the motion costs work.

Now carry that picture into a fluid. Molecules, suspended particles and nearby walls interact at many different relative velocities. Instead of following one rubbing interface, we collect the resistance of many dynamic interactions into an average shear stress. Viscosity describes how that averaged resistance responds to the rate of shearing. The fluid law comes from this collective response; we do not apply \(F_f=\mu_k N\) to every molecule or cell.

From one frictional contact to many dynamic interactions

In view 2 the top plate moves and the bottom plate stays still. Fluid next to each plate follows that plate (the no-slip condition), so fluid near the top moves faster than fluid near the bottom. Neighboring layers exchange momentum through molecular motion and intermolecular forces. The faster layer pulls the slower one forward; the slower layer pulls back. Maintaining that velocity difference costs work, which is dissipated as heat. This distributed internal friction is viscosity. Water already has it: suspended particles are not required.

Now add particles. Each particle moves relative to the surrounding fluid and experiences viscous drag. Particles in faster and slower regions move past one another, shearing fluid in the gaps between them. Near a wall, the particle and wall can also have different velocities, and the intervening fluid resists their relative motion. In a dense suspension, direct contacts and particle deformation can contribute too. These are the fluid’s dynamic frictional interactions: local resistance associated with relative motion. An interaction can transmit this resistance through fluid without the two solid surfaces touching.

What are we averaging? Imagine a plane separating a faster layer from a slower one. Many molecular and particle interactions transmit tangential force across that plane. Average that transmitted force over a representative area and time, then divide by area: \(\sigma_{xy}=\langle F_{x,\mathrm{transmitted}}\rangle/A\). This is the macroscopic shear stress. We average the transmitted response, rather than assigning every encounter a dry-friction coefficient and averaging those coefficients.

Blood carries deformable red cells in plasma. The plate feels the collective response of the plasma’s molecular interactions and the cells’ dynamic interactions with fluid, other cells and nearby walls. The viscous friction counted here is dynamic and depends on relative motion; it is not a collection of static holding forces. We describe its average with a viscosity. The bench illustrates the interactions and computes a continuum reference response; it does not resolve and sum individual microscopic encounters. §1.3 adds the extra physics of a cell network that can hold stress at rest.

Why does force per area equal viscosity times a velocity gradient?

Let \(x\) point along the plates and \(y\) across the gap. Write the fluid’s mean velocity along the plates as \(u(y)\). Here \(u\) is a velocity; call the plate’s accumulated displacement \(\Delta x\). The usual symbol for shear stress is \(\tau\), the same quantity this course writes as \(\sigma_{xy}\). We will use both names in the equation below.

  1. 1 · Why divide the force by area? Imagine doubling the plate’s area by placing two identical experiments side by side. Each patch of fluid has the same local motion and transmits the same average resistance. The total force doubles because twice as many patches contribute. Thus \(F\propto A\), and \(\tau=F/A\) measures the local, averaged resistance independently of how much plate we use. Here \(F\) is the magnitude of the tangential pull needed to maintain steady motion; the fluid exerts an equal opposing force on the plate.
  2. 2 · Why a difference in velocity? A faster layer transfers forward momentum to a slower layer and is pulled back in return. This resistance comes from their relative motion. If the entire fluid and both plates translate together at one velocity, neighboring layers have no mean velocity difference and there is no viscous shear stress. Absolute speed cannot set the shear resistance.
  3. 3 · Why divide that difference by distance? Local interactions couple nearby regions. If the mean velocity changes by \(\Delta u\) across a separation \(\Delta y\), the speed difference experienced over a small interaction distance \(\ell\) is approximately \((\Delta u/\Delta y)\ell\). Packing the same velocity change into a smaller gap makes nearby regions move past one another faster. The local measure is the velocity gradient, \(du/dy=\lim_{\Delta y\to0}\Delta u/\Delta y\). The derivative describes a smooth mean velocity field, averaged over many molecules.
  4. 4 · Why multiply the gradient by η? In the Newtonian regime, the averaged viscous resistance is proportional to the local relative velocity and therefore to this gradient: \(\tau\propto du/dy\). The proportionality coefficient is \(\eta\). It collects how strongly molecular motion, intermolecular forces and particle interactions transfer momentum and dissipate mechanical energy. Doubling the gradient doubles the stress in this regime. This linear response is a material law supported by experiment; it does not follow from the definition of stress alone.

Read the bench physically. In its steady, uniform fluid film, the bottom moves at zero and the top at \(U\), so \(u(y)=Uy/h\) and \(du/dy=U/h\). Halving \(h\) at fixed \(U\) doubles the relative velocity over the same small interaction distance, and therefore doubles the local viscous stress. Doubling \(A\) doubles how many regions transmit force, and therefore doubles \(F\), while leaving that local stress unchanged.

Week 6’s shear strain provides the same measure of relative motion. Using \(\Delta x\) for displacement,

eq. 6.3.5 again — shear strain, shear rate and the velocity gradient$$\gamma=\frac{\Delta x}{h}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\dot\gamma=\frac{U}{h}=\frac{du}{dy}$$

The two equalities now have different jobs: force per area defines stress; the Newtonian law connects that stress to the velocity gradient. For this positive-gradient plate experiment, written in magnitudes,

eq. 7.1.1 — the viscosity law$$\tau=\frac{F}{A}=\eta\frac{du}{dy}=\eta\dot\gamma$$

Viscosity is the link between the two sides. The plate measures the averaged force transmitted by many dynamic interactions. The gradient tells us how rapidly neighboring regions move past one another. The stress required per unit gradient is the viscosity. This is the same stress–gradient relation described in NASA Glenn’s viscosity explanation.

Reverse the shear and the signed stress reverses; the fluid’s traction on the driven plate opposes its motion. At zero velocity gradient this Newtonian viscous contribution vanishes, even though molecules continue moving and interacting. Blood’s cell network can also support stress at rest; that additional response enters in §1.3.

The fluid property is \(\eta\); the apparatus’s damping coefficient \(\eta_d\) also includes its area and gap. Changing geometry changes the measured force without changing the Newtonian fluid’s viscosity.

Why pascal-seconds?

View 3 keeps the units attached to every measured quantity. Stress is force per area, in N/m² or Pa. Shear rate is speed per gap: (m/s)/m = s⁻¹. Dividing stress by shear rate therefore gives Pa/s⁻¹ = Pa·s, equivalently N·s/m² or kg/(m·s). These are the SI units of dynamic viscosity (BIPM, SI Brochure, Table 6).

Read it as “stress required per unit shear rate.” A Newtonian fluid of viscosity 1 Pa·s requires 1 Pa to shear at 1 s⁻¹, and 10 Pa at 10 s⁻¹. The second is a consequence of dividing by a rate; it is not a waiting time. A millipascal-second is 0.001 Pa·s. At 37 °C water is 0.69 mPa·s and plasma about 1.2 mPa·s. Set the bench to 1 s⁻¹: plasma requires 0.0012 Pa, or 0.12 µN on a 1 cm² plate.

For blood, \(\sigma_{xy}/\dot\gamma\) is called the apparent viscosity: the value a single Newtonian \(\eta\) would need to explain the measured force under those conditions. The bench’s high-shear, large-tube reference at haematocrit 0.45 is about 3.8 mPa·s, using the existing Pries fit; Rosenson’s healthy-adult study measured about 3.3 mPa·s at 100 s⁻¹. A single reading at rest cannot determine a viscosity: zero stress divided by zero rate cannot be evaluated, even though the fluid still has a viscosity.

Derive eq. 7.1.1 from many local dynamic resistances

A physical model for the form of the law. Consider a representative area \(A\) across the layers. Model its averaged, dissipative coupling by an effective drag coefficient \(B\), acting across a representative interaction distance \(\ell\). This is a simplified Newtonian coupling model, not a calculation of individual molecular contacts.

  1. Local dynamic friction responds to relative velocity. In this linear model, the magnitude of the transmitted force is \(F=B\Delta u\), where \(\Delta u\) is the mean speed difference between the coupled regions. More area provides proportionally more interacting regions, so \(B\propto A\).
  2. Nearby regions sample the velocity gradient. Over the interaction distance \(\ell\), a smooth velocity field gives \(\Delta u\simeq\ell\,du/dy\). The same small region experiences a larger speed difference when the gradient is larger.
  3. Average per area and collect the interaction physics.
    $$\frac{F}{A}\simeq\underbrace{\frac{B\ell}{A}}_{\displaystyle\eta}\frac{du}{dy}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\tau=\eta\frac{du}{dy}$$
    The combination \(B\ell/A\) has units \((\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m})\,\text{m}/\text{m}^2=\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}\). Area cancels because the summed coupling \(B\) grows with area.
  4. Recover the plate measurement. For a steady, homogeneous Newtonian film with no pressure gradient, the transmitted shear stress is constant across the gap. Constant \(\eta\) then makes \(du/dy\) constant. With no slip, \(u(0)=0\) and \(u(h)=U\), giving \(u(y)=Uy/h\), \(du/dy=U/h\), and \(F=\eta A U/h\).
Force grows with the number of interacting regions (area) and with their local relative motion (velocity gradient). Viscosity summarizes the strength of their averaged dynamic coupling.

For positive viscosity, the mechanical power dissipated per fluid volume is \(\tau\,du/dy=\eta(du/dy)^2\ge0\). This is how the averaged dynamic resistance converts the plate’s work into heat.

The physical model explains why the Newtonian law has this form. It does not predict a liquid’s numerical viscosity from first principles. Those values require measurement or a microscopic theory; aggregation and nonlinear responses require a richer constitutive law.

Check yourself · the units tell a physical story

1. Two Newtonian liquids are sheared at 10 s⁻¹. One needs 0.01 Pa; the other needs 0.03 Pa. Which is more viscous, and by how much?

Answer

The viscosities are 1 and 3 mPa·s. The second liquid needs three times the stress at the same rate, so its viscosity is three times larger.

2. You double the plate area and measure twice the force. Has the viscosity changed?

Answer

No. Force per area is unchanged, as is shear rate. The damper coefficient doubles; the fluid viscosity stays the same.

3. To find viscosity, do we give every encounter a force μN and average the friction coefficients?

Answer

We average the tangential force transmitted across layers by their dynamic interactions, including molecular momentum transfer and viscous drag. That gives shear stress. Viscosity describes its dependence on shear rate; the dry-friction law μN does not supply the fluid law.

The bridge from friction to viscosity. One sliding interface introduces friction. A fluid contains many dynamic interactions at relative velocities. Their averaged tangential force per area is shear stress; viscosity relates that stress to shear rate. Start with the velocity plot

The bench plots \(u(r)\), the axial velocity at distance \(r\) from the tube’s axis. The curve is highest and flat on the axis, then falls to zero at the wall. This is a parabolic profile. The moving dye and arrows use that same field: the wall stays still, nearby fluid moves slowly, and fluid near the centre moves fastest. The next steps explain the curve before using it to calculate a volume flow.

1 · Use a cylinder of fluid to find the velocity gradient

Consider an imaginary cylinder inside the fluid, radius \(r\), length \(L\). Pressure acts on its two circular ends. The inlet is \(\Delta P\) above the outlet, so the net downstream pressure force is \(F_P=\Delta P\,\pi r^2\). The slower fluid outside the cylinder pulls back on its curved surface, of area \(2\pi rL\). In steady, fully developed flow this fluid cylinder has no axial acceleration, so the forces balance:

$$\underbrace{\Delta P\,\pi r^2}_{\text{pressure push}}=\underbrace{\tau(r)\,2\pi rL}_{\text{viscous drag}}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\tau(r)=\frac{\Delta P}{2L}\,r$$

Here \(\tau(r)\) is the positive magnitude of the retarding shear stress. Its force on the inner fluid points upstream. The stress is zero on the axis and increases toward the wall: a larger core has more pressure-pushed end area relative to its sheared side area.

Now use §1.1’s Newtonian viscosity law locally. Velocity decreases as we move outward, so \(du/dr<0\). The positive drag magnitude is \(\tau(r)=-\eta\,du/dr\). Substitution gives

$$-\eta\frac{du}{dr}=\frac{\Delta P}{2L}\,r\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{du}{dr}=-\frac{\Delta P}{2\eta L}\,r$$

This explains the shape. The slope is zero at the centre and grows more negative in direct proportion to radius. Integrating a slope proportional to \(-r\) produces a parabola.

2 · Integrate from the stationary wall to obtain u(r)

No slip fixes the integration constant: \(u(R)=0\). Integrate from the wall \(R\) to the point \(r\), using \(s\) as the integration variable:

$$u(r)-u(R)=-\frac{\Delta P}{2\eta L}\int_R^r s\,ds=-\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta L}(r^2-R^2)$$
eq. 7.1.2 — the velocity field in a circular pipe$$u(r)=\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta L}\left(R^2-r^2\right)$$

At the wall the bracket is zero. On the axis it is \(R^2\), so \(u_{max}=\Delta P R^2/(4\eta L)\). Raising pressure makes the whole profile taller; increasing length or viscosity makes it shorter. At fixed pressure drop, length and viscosity, a wider pipe also has a higher central speed.

3 · Turn the velocity field into volume per second

One velocity does not determine the total flow. In a short time \(dt\), fluid crossing a tiny area \(dA\) advances a distance \(u(r)dt\), transporting a volume \(dV=u(r)dt\,dA\). Thus its contribution to volume flow is \(dQ=dV/dt=u(r)dA\).

Group points with the same velocity into a thin circular annulus. Its area is circumference times thickness, \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\). Therefore

$$dQ=u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr,\qquad Q=\int_0^R u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr$$

Read view 2 of the bench. The axis has the largest speed but vanishing annular area. At the wall the circumference is largest but velocity is zero. Intermediate annuli contribute most strongly. The flow-density plot includes the area weighting \(2\pi r\); the cyan band has a finite thickness and its reported flow is integrated across that band.

4 · Substitute the parabola and perform the integral

$$\begin{aligned}Q&=\int_0^R\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta L}(R^2-r^2)\,2\pi r\,dr\\[4pt]&=\frac{\pi\Delta P}{2\eta L}\int_0^R(R^2r-r^3)\,dr\\[4pt]&=\frac{\pi\Delta P}{2\eta L}\left[\frac{R^2r^2}{2}-\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^R\\[4pt]&=\frac{\pi\Delta P}{2\eta L}\left(\frac{R^4}{2}-\frac{R^4}{4}\right).\end{aligned}$$
eq. 7.1.3 — Hagen–Poiseuille’s flow law$$Q=\frac{\pi R^4\,\Delta P}{8\eta L}$$

Now divide the volume flow by the entire cross-sectional area to obtain the area-averaged velocity: \(\bar u=Q/(\pi R^2)=\Delta P R^2/(8\eta L)=u_{max}/2\). An equally spaced average of the values on the radial plot would miss the annular area weights.

Where the fourth power comes from. A larger radius provides an area proportional to \(R^2\), and its pressure-driven velocity scale also grows as \(R^2\). Together they give \(Q\propto R^2R^2=R^4\). Doubling radius gives four times the area and four times the mean speed: sixteen times the flow.

5 · Read the same result as hydraulic resistance

The resistance \(R_h\) is pressure drop per volume flow. The subscript distinguishes it from the tube radius \(R\):

eq. 7.1.4 — hydraulic resistance$$R_h=\frac{\Delta P}{Q}=\frac{8\eta L}{\pi R^4}$$

Its units are Pa·s/m³. Length and viscosity add resistance in direct proportion; radius reduces it through the fourth power. A 10% narrowing leaves \(0.9^4=0.6561\) of the original flow, a loss of about 34%, at the same \(\Delta P\), \(L\) and \(\eta\). This is why small changes in arteriole radius can strongly regulate flow.

What was assumed? Steady, fully developed, laminar flow of an incompressible Newtonian fluid in a straight, rigid, circular tube; constant viscosity and no slip at the wall. The derivation excludes entrance and pulsatile effects. Blood’s aggregation and yield stress need §1.3; cell-scale confinement needs §3.2. A plotted reference parabola does not establish those assumptions for every vessel.

Derive why a wider pipe gives two factors of R²

Rewrite the field with \(x=r/R\): \(u=u_{max}(1-x^2)\), where \(u_{max}\propto R^2\). An annulus has \(dA=2\pi R^2x\,dx\), so \(Q=u_{max}\,2\pi R^2\int_0^1(1-x^2)x\,dx\). The dimensionless integral equals \(1/4\), leaving \(Q\propto R^2R^2\). The first factor belongs to speed; the second belongs to area.

At fixed ΔP, L and η, radius changes both the velocity field and the area it flows through. Keeping only the area change would miss half of the radius dependence.

1.3 · Keep the force balance; change the fluid’s stress law

Use the same pipe and the same pressure force, \(F_P=\Delta P\,A\). In §1.2 we closed the force balance with Newtonian viscosity, \(\tau=\eta\dot\gamma\). Now give the fluid a yield stress: it can support a small shear stress without shearing. The pressure still sets the stress inside the pipe; the new relation between stress and shear rate changes the velocity field. We will derive that field again.

Blood motivates this change. At low shear, red cells can form reversible stacks called rouleaux, and aggregation increases the low-shear resistance. An ideal Bingham fluid captures a yield threshold and a flowing slope. It is a teaching model for this effect; actual blood also shear-thins, depends on its flow history, and becomes a cell-scale suspension in small vessels.

1 · Replace the constitutive relation

Keep \(\tau\ge0\) as a shear-stress magnitude and \(\dot\gamma\ge0\) as a shear-rate magnitude. A Newtonian fluid shears at every nonzero stress. The ideal Bingham fluid has two branches:

eq. 7.1.5 — Bingham’s law$$\begin{cases}\dot\gamma=0,&0\le\tau\le\tau_y,\\ \tau=\tau_y+\eta_p\dot\gamma,&\tau>\tau_y.\end{cases}$$

Here \(\tau_y\) is the yield stress and \(\eta_p\) is the plastic viscosity, the slope of the flowing branch. Its units remain Pa·s. For nonzero shear, the apparent viscosity is \(\tau/\dot\gamma=\eta_p+\tau_y/\dot\gamma\): the extra term matters most at small rates. At rest the stress can lie anywhere below yield; the intercept is the limit as flow begins, not a unique resting stress. Reversing the flow requires the corresponding signed branch; this section uses positive pressure drops.

The reference \(\tau_y=0.005\) Pa is the upper end of the 0.0015–0.005 Pa range at normal haematocrit quoted from Merrill’s measurements by Fedosov et al. (2011). It is an order-of-magnitude teaching reference, not a universal threshold for circulating blood. Real blood’s response is smoother and depends on aggregation and measurement history.

2 · The pressure force still gives exactly the same stress field

Choose the same imaginary fluid cylinder, radius \(r\) and length \(L\). Its end area is \(A=\pi r^2\); its sheared side area is \(2\pi rL\). Fully developed steady flow has no axial acceleration, so pressure force and retarding shear force balance:

$$\underbrace{\Delta P\,\pi r^2}_{\Delta P\,A}=\underbrace{\tau(r)\,2\pi rL}_{\text{side traction}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\tau(r)=\frac{\Delta P}{2L}r.$$

This equation did not use viscosity. Pressure drop and geometry determine the stress. What changes is how much velocity gradient a given stress produces. In the pipe, speed decreases outward, so \(\dot\gamma=-du/dr\). Invert the new law:

$$-\frac{du}{dr}=\begin{cases}0,&\dfrac{\Delta P\,r}{2L}\le\tau_y,\\[5pt]\dfrac{1}{\eta_p}\left(\dfrac{\Delta P\,r}{2L}-\tau_y\right),&\dfrac{\Delta P\,r}{2L}>\tau_y.\end{cases}$$

Compare this directly with §1.2’s \(-du/dr=\Delta P\,r/(2\eta L)\). Above yield, a stress \(\tau_y\) is needed before the rest drives shear. Below yield, the velocity gradient is zero.

3 · Locate the boundary between plug and sheared fluid

The stress is zero on the axis and increases toward the wall. Where it first reaches \(\tau_y\), the fluid starts to shear. For a flowing tube, \(\tau(r_p)=\tau_y\) gives

eq. 7.1.6 — the plug boundary, when the wall yields$$r_p=\frac{2\tau_y L}{\Delta P},\qquad \frac{r_p}{R}=\frac{\tau_y}{\tau_w},\qquad \tau_w=\frac{\Delta P\,R}{2L}.$$

If \(\tau_w\le\tau_y\), no part of the tube yields. No slip at the fixed wall then makes the entire velocity field zero. The threshold is

$$\Delta P_{crit}=\frac{2\tau_y L}{R};\qquad \Delta P\le\Delta P_{crit}\ \Longrightarrow\ u(r)=0.$$

At zero pressure, use this stopped branch rather than dividing by \(\Delta P\) to find a plug radius. A formal \(r_p\ge R\) means the whole tube is unyielded.

4 · Integrate inward from the wall again

For \(\Delta P>\Delta P_{crit}\), the annulus \(r_p\le r\le R\) shears. Use the same boundary condition as before, \(u(R)=0\), and integrate the new gradient. With \(s\) as a dummy radius,

$$\begin{aligned}u(r)-u(R)&=-\frac{1}{\eta_p}\int_R^r\left(\frac{\Delta P}{2L}s-\tau_y\right)ds\\[4pt]&=\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R^2-r^2)-\frac{\tau_y}{\eta_p}(R-r).\end{aligned}$$

The first term is the old parabola with \(\eta=\eta_p\). The second reduces the speed because some of the stress is needed to overcome the yield threshold. This expression applies only in the yielded annulus. Continuing it toward the axis would incorrectly predict a nonflat core.

5 · Join to the flat core

Inside \(r_p\), \(du/dr=0\), so velocity is constant. Continuity fixes it to the annulus velocity at \(r_p\). Substituting \(\tau_y=\Delta P\,r_p/(2L)\) simplifies the plug speed to

$$u_p=u(r_p)=\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R-r_p)^2.$$

The complete field for a flowing tube is therefore

$$u(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R-r_p)^2,&0\le r\le r_p,\\[8pt]\dfrac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R^2-r^2)-\dfrac{\tau_y}{\eta_p}(R-r),&r_p<r\le R.\end{cases}$$

No shear means no difference in neighboring velocities. It does not require velocity itself to vanish. The outer fluid carries the unyielded core along. Velocity and slope join continuously at \(r_p\), while the wall remains stationary. In the bench’s profile view, the same pressure drop gives the same stress field for both fluids, but a lower, flat-topped Bingham velocity field.

Recover §1.2. Set \(\tau_y=0\): then \(r_p=0\), the yield correction disappears, and \(u(r)=\Delta P(R^2-r^2)/(4\eta_p L)\). The Newtonian result is the zero-yield limit of the new field.
Derive the flow by adding the moving plug and the sheared rings

The volume weighting is unchanged: \(dQ=u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr\). Split the integral at the plug boundary:

$$\begin{aligned}Q&=\underbrace{\pi r_p^2 u_p}_{\text{moving plug}}+2\pi\int_{r_p}^{R}\left[\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta_p L}(R^2-r^2)-\frac{\tau_y}{\eta_p}(R-r)\right]r\,dr.\end{aligned}$$

Use \(x=r/R\), \(B=r_p/R\), \(\tau_y=\Delta P RB/(2L)\), and the Newtonian axis scale \(U_0=\Delta P R^2/(4\eta_p L)\). The dimensionless annulus speed is \(1-x^2-2B(1-x)\), while the plug speed is \((1-B)^2\). Thus

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{Q}{2\pi R^2U_0}&=\frac{B^2(1-B)^2}{2}+\int_B^1\left[(1-2B)x+2Bx^2-x^3\right]dx\\[3pt]&=\frac{B^2(1-B)^2}{2}+\left[\frac{1-2B}{2}x^2+\frac{2B}{3}x^3-\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_B^1\\[3pt]&=\frac14-\frac{B}{3}+\frac{B^4}{12}.\end{aligned}$$
$$Q=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\pi R^4\Delta P}{8\eta_pL}\left(1-\dfrac43B+\dfrac13B^4\right),&0\le B<1,\\[7pt]0,&\Delta P\le\Delta P_{crit}.\end{cases}$$

This is the Buckingham–Reiner result. The bracket is 1 at zero yield and 0 when the plug fills the tube. Because \(B\) itself depends on pressure, Q is no longer proportional to ΔP. View 3 plots that curve with the matched Newtonian line. The force balance has stayed the same throughout.

Rouleaux supply structure, not a new pressure force

Zero relative mean velocity does not mean zero microscopic motion. Inside the ideal plug, neighboring fluid layers share the same mean speed. This view follows that translation, leaving cell wandering and rotation visible. Diffusive fluctuations bring nearby cells into encounters; short-range attraction can hold them together, while contact forces rotate and slide their broad faces into compact stacks. As contacts accumulate, neighboring cells constrain one another’s motion. The animation accelerates this process; it is not a calibrated measurement of RBC diffusion or aggregation time.

Each red cell remains a separate deformable cell. Plasma macromolecules promote weak attractions between cells; low shear allows stacks and larger associations to persist. Relative motion can disrupt them. The suspension can therefore behave like a weak, reversible solid below a small yield stress: the network resists rearrangement even though its individual cells and surrounding plasma are not solidifying into one fused object.

Fedosov et al. (2011) reproduced reversible aggregation and a nonzero extrapolated yield stress in cell simulations with attraction, while the nonaggregating suspension had no extrapolated yield stress. Their comparison also shows why the existence and value of a true blood yield stress are difficult to measure. Here, “solid-like” refers to the ideal model’s ability to support stress without sustained shear; actual blood’s response depends on its history.

When is a yield-induced plug substantial?

Bring the structure into §1.3’s force balance. The pressure force is still ΔP·A, the wall shear stress is still \(\tau_w=\Delta P R/(2L)\), and the dimensionless comparison is

$$B=\frac{\tau_y}{\tau_w}=\frac{2\tau_yL}{\Delta P\,R},\qquad \frac{r_p}{R}=B\quad(0\le B<1).$$
  • \(B\ll1\): pressure-driven shear overwhelms the small yield term through most of the tube. The formal central core is tiny, so a yield-induced flat region may be negligible.
  • \(0<B<1\) and appreciable: a substantial unyielded core translates inside a sheared annulus. It is solid-like in shear, but can still move as a plug.
  • \(B\ge1\): even the wall stress is at or below yield. In the ideal rigid no-slip model, the whole tube is unyielded and stationary.

Thus, a large yield-induced plug needs an effective pressure gradient small enough relative to yield stress and vessel radius. Any nonzero yield stress produces a formal core in the ideal Bingham solution; “large enough to matter” is the useful distinction. A blunt measured blood profile can also reflect cell migration, shear thinning or pulsatility, so a flat profile alone does not prove rouleaux-induced yielding.

Hold the right quantity fixed. At fixed \(\Delta P/L\), increasing R raises \(\tau_w\) and reduces B. At fixed R, lowering ΔP or increasing L raises B. At fixed flow, pressure must readjust, so that is a different experiment. Small vessels also require cell-scale physics (§3.2).

For example, the declared fully structured state has \(\tau_y=5\) mPa. A 20 cm segment of radius 2 mm has \(\Delta P_{crit}=2\tau_yL/R=1\) Pa = 0.00750 mmHg. At twice this drive B = 1/2. These numbers show the scale of the ideal threshold; they do not establish that ordinary seated leg veins reach complete yield arrest.

Why a seated leg can develop venous stasis

Leg veins return blood toward the heart. Calf contraction compresses veins, and competent valves help direct the displaced blood toward the heart. During prolonged sitting, repeated calf-pump action is reduced; cramped positioning or compression can also impede venous return. Flow can slow and blood can pool, particularly in poorly washed-out regions around venous valves. A vein’s main lumen can still carry blood while a valve pocket has much slower exchange.

Low shear favors aggregation, and aggregation increases low-shear resistance: this is a useful mechanical feedback to connect with the bench. It is one contributor to the behavior of slow blood, not a requirement that all blood first become a stationary Bingham solid. The straight-tube solution does not resolve venous valves, compliant walls or muscle-driven transients.

Prolonged sitting and travel: CDC. Work/computer sitting association: Healy et al., 2010 (case-control study). Valve-pocket hypoxia under undisturbed flow: Hamer et al., 1981 (small human/dog physiology study; abstract verified).

Where DVT adds biology

A rouleaux stack is reversible cell aggregation; a venous thrombus involves coagulation and a fibrin-rich structure. Fibrinogen in plasma and polymerized fibrin in a clot have different roles. Slow flow and poor washout interact with the vessel lining, inflammatory cells, platelets and coagulation. This is the connection to the familiar Virchow framework: altered flow/stasis, vessel-wall changes and increased coagulability.

In a mouse model, restricting venous flow recruited innate immune cells and platelets and promoted fibrin formation; complete cessation of flow was not required. The clinical point is that prolonged leg immobility can create conditions favorable to DVT, with other risk factors influencing whether a thrombus forms. Reversing rouleaux does not establish that an existing thrombus has dissolved.

Primary mechanistic evidence: von Brühl et al., 2012, mouse flow-restriction model. This supports the distinction between rheological aggregation and coagulation; it is not a patient risk calculator.

Why height and seat fit belong in the story

Taller stature has been associated with higher VTE incidence in a large Swedish registry/co-sibling study. In the MEGA travel study, very tall travelers also had higher thrombosis risk; cramped legroom and venous compression were proposed explanations. The height subgroups were small, and short air travelers could also be affected when seat fit left their feet unsupported. Height matters in context, rather than through a universal rule that tall blood aggregates more.

A longer vertical column also has a larger hydrostatic head, \(\Delta P_h=\rho gh\). The height slider shows this effect. Hydrostatic pressure is not the same as the pressure head available to overcome friction. For an upward segment with rise h, the effective drive in this simplified balance is \(\Delta P_{eff}=P_{lower}-P_{upper}-\rho gh\). A stationary column can have a large pressure difference while gravity balances it and flow remains zero. Muscle pumping, valves, vessel compliance and posture determine actual venous return; body height is not substituted into the Bingham yield law.

  • Reversible rouleaux can provide a weak yield-like response. Prolonged sitting can reduce venous return, while DVT adds vessel-wall and coagulation biology; height and seat fit affect the clinical context (§1.4).

2.1 · The same litres per minute at every level, and whether it is Stokes flow there

The heart pumps about five litres a minute, 83 millilitres every second, into one aorta. That blood passes through arteries, then arterioles, then something like ten billion capillaries, then venules and veins, and comes back through two venae cavae. Nothing is added or lost on the way, so whatever level you cut across, the same 83 mL/s is passing through it. The speed at a level is therefore set by nothing but the total cross-section of all its vessels taken together:

eq. 7.2.1 — continuity $$\bar u = \frac{Q}{A_{total}}$$

The aorta's cross-section is about 4.5 cm²; the capillaries', all of them side by side, is of the order of several thousand square centimetres. So the blood that left the heart at about two tenths of a metre per second is moving at a few tenths of a millimetre per second by the time it is in a capillary: some six hundred times slower by the count below, and slower still than the 0.9 m/s peak the aorta reaches at the top of each beat. It spends about a second and a half in a half-millimetre capillary. That second is the number Module 8 is built on, because it is the time the blood has to trade with the tissue.

Is it Stokes flow there? Week 1 said the Reynolds number of a protein is "tiny" and never wrote it down. Here is the number, minted at last: the ratio of the inertia a fluid carries to the viscous friction that stops it,

eq. 7.2.2 — the Reynolds number $$\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho\,\bar u\,D}{\eta}$$

with \(\rho\) the density, \(\bar u\) the mean speed, \(D\) the diameter and \(\eta\) the viscosity; it has no units. Small means viscosity wins: the flow follows the pressure instantly, the profile is Poiseuille's parabola, nothing coasts and nothing sloshes. Large means inertia matters: the fluid keeps going when the push stops, flows separate at bends, and a fast enough flow becomes turbulent. In the aorta Re is about 1 400 at the mean speed and about 7 000 at the 0.9 m/s peak of each beat, which is why a pulse exists at all: the blood the heart throws forward keeps going between beats, and the arterial wall stores and returns the pressure. By the arterioles Re is a hundredth; in the capillary and the venule it is a thousandth or less. There the flow has no memory, and that is why Tab 4 can treat the T cell's drag with Week 1's Stokes law and nothing more.

Laminar or turbulent. Osborne Reynolds asked the question with a thread of dye in water running through a glass pipe (Reynolds 1883). At low speed the thread stays a straight, thin line all the way down the pipe: the water moves in layers, laminae, each sliding past its neighbours and keeping its own distance from the axis. That is laminar flow. Push faster and, past a critical Reynolds number, about 2 000 for a steady flow in a long straight pipe, the thread wavers, breaks and smears across the whole pipe within a short distance. Eddies are now carrying fluid sideways, from the fast middle to the slow wall and back. That is turbulent flow.

Read the table against that 2 000. The aorta's mean, 1 400, is below it, and only the top of each beat goes over: probes placed just above the aortic valve found the flow highly disturbed at peak ejection in normal people (peak Re 5 700 to 8 900) and truly turbulent in one with a high cardiac output (peak Re 10 000). Behind a stiff or narrowed aortic valve it is turbulent for nearly the whole beat, and that turbulence is the murmur heard through a stethoscope (Stein & Sabbah 1976; Sabbah & Stein 1976). Everywhere downstream the numbers fall far below: tens in the arteries and veins, a few hundred in the venae cavae, a hundredth in the arterioles, a thousandth in the capillaries and venules. Apart from the first few centimetres out of the heart, the circulation is laminar.

That is why Tab 1's plug can exist. Every pipe in Tab 1 was assumed laminar; the word is in §1.2's list of what was assumed. Here is why that was safe. The force balance on a cylinder of fluid (§1.2) assumed that the only thing passing stress between layers is friction between neighbours, and it gave a stress that grows from zero on the axis to its largest at the wall. §1.3's plug lives in that calm centre: wherever the stress falls below the yield stress the blood does not shear and rides along as a solid core, and §1.4's rouleaux stack in the same low-shear core. In turbulent flow the eddies would keep throwing fast fluid into the core and slow fluid out of it; the quiet middle would not exist, and neither would the plug. A turbulent pipe also has a flattened mean profile, but for the opposite reason: mixing evens out the speed, rather than a yield stress holding the core rigid. Blood can form a plug because almost all of it moves in layers.

Three things to carry. The capillary is slow because the tree is wide, not because the blood is thick: continuity alone takes 0.19 m/s to 0.3 mm/s. Below about 2 000 the Reynolds number keeps a flow laminar, and only the aorta's peak crosses it; everywhere else the blood moves in layers, which is what lets §1.3's plug and §1.4's rouleaux exist. And below the arterioles the Reynolds number is so small that every vessel is a Poiseuille pipe, which is what lets this module get away with one law.

2.2 · Where the pressure goes, and how pipes join

The tree is pipes joined to pipes, and Week 6 §2.1 already taught the only two ways to join anything. There they were springs: in series the two share the force and their displacements add; in parallel they share the displacement and their forces add. For pipes the same two rules read

eq. 6.2.1 again — the two joining rules, for pipes $$\text{series: } Q \text{ shared},\ \ \Delta P = \Delta P_1 + \Delta P_2,\ \ R_h = R_1 + R_2;\qquad \text{parallel: } \Delta P \text{ shared},\ \ Q = Q_1 + Q_2,\ \ \frac{1}{R_h} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$$

and the dictionary needs saying out loud, because it is the opposite of the first guess. Pressure feels like the push, so it is tempting to let it play the force. It does not: in series the flow is what both pipes share, and the drops add, so the flow plays the force and the pressure drop plays the displacement. Keep that straight and the whole tree is two rules: the levels are in series with one another (the same 83 mL/s through each, their drops adding up to the whole pressure), and within a level the vessels are in parallel (the same drop across each, their flows adding up to the same 83 mL/s).

Where the pressure goes. A mean of about 95 millimetres of mercury at the aorta; about 35 entering the capillary and 15 leaving it (the textbook pair, reproduced from Landis's 1930 measurements in a human nail fold; a later direct measurement in a warm finger gave 36 at the arterial end and 25 at the venous, the venous value depending on site and temperature); nearly nothing at the heart's door. Of the 94 mmHg spent on the way round, the arterioles spend about 45, almost half, across two millimetres of their length, with the capillaries taking another 20. The arteries, for all their length, take 12; the veins 7. The arterioles spend it because of eq. 7.1.4: their radius is small, the fourth power is merciless, and there are not yet so many of them in parallel that the parallel rule rescues the total. That is the physical reason they are the valve: the level that holds the drop is the level whose radius sets the flow.

Where the volume goes. The second budget on the same tree is the blood itself. The arteries, which hold the pressure, hold only about a tenth of the blood; the capillaries and arterioles together a twentieth; the heart and lungs a seventh. About seventy per cent of the five litres is in the veins, because a wide tube at low pressure is where blood can wait, which is what §1.3 said rouleaux are for. Pinch the arterioles and the capillary pressure falls and the flow with it, Module 8's \(P_c\) and Module 9's lever in one move; fill or empty the veins and the pressure hardly notices, which is why the body uses them as the reservoir.

3.1 · Forty-five per cent cells: more cells, more rubbing

Spin a tube of blood and it separates: a little under half the volume is cells, almost all of them red, and the rest is plasma. That fraction is the haematocrit, \(H\), about 0.45. A red cell is a dimpled disc about 7.8 µm across, 2.6 µm thick at its rim and under a micron at the centre, with a volume of 94 femtolitres and a surface of 135 µm², a third more surface than a sphere of the same volume would have, which is what lets it fold, in Module 8, through capillaries narrower than itself.

Now §1.1's counting picture pays, in two instalments, and the first was worked out by someone who was not thinking about blood at all. In 1905, for his doctoral thesis, Albert Einstein wanted to know how big a sugar molecule is. Sugar thickens water (§1.1's honey is the extreme case), and he reasoned that how much it thickens should say how much of the water's volume the molecules take up. So he pictured each molecule as a tiny rigid ball in a liquid being sheared. The ball cannot be sheared, so the liquid has to bend its flow around it and shear harder beside it, and that extra rubbing is extra friction: the liquid acts more viscous. While the balls are few and far apart, a dilute suspension, each one disturbs the liquid on its own and never meets another, so the extra friction simply counts balls: it is proportional to the share of the volume they take up, whatever their size. His first number in front was 1. Jacques Bancelin, a student of Jean Perrin's, measured suspensions of small particles and found the rise did depend only on the volume they took up, not their size, but was steeper; Einstein asked his collaborator Ludwig Hopf to recheck the calculation, Hopf found a slip in a derivative, and the corrected number, published in 1911, is five halves. With it, sugar's thickening and its diffusion gave Avogadro's number as 6.56 × 10²³ (Straumann 2005).

Blood is the same problem with bigger balls. The plasma is the liquid, the cells are the balls, and the haematocrit is exactly Einstein's share of the volume. A red cell is neither rigid nor a sphere, but while the cells are few each one still disturbs the plasma on its own, so Einstein's law, written in the quantity the bench below plots, the apparent viscosity relative to plasma, is

eq. 7.3.1 — Einstein's dilute law: each cell disturbs the plasma on its own $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = 1 + 2.5\,H$$

where \(H\) is the haematocrit, the fraction of the volume that is cells, and the 1 is plasma on its own. It is a straight line, and blood leaves it almost at once. By 1933 physiologists were measuring how blood's apparent viscosity changes with its fraction of cells, in blood flowing through the isolated hindlimb of a dog (Whittaker & Winton 1933).

How is the friction measured? The way the bench below does it, with a capillary viscometer: hold the pressure, time a fixed volume of the fluid through a glass tube, and compare with plasma's time. At one pressure through one tube, eq. 7.1.3 makes the flow inversely proportional to the viscosity, so the time to fill grows in proportion to it:

eq. 7.1.3 again — at one pressure through one tube: a capillary viscometer $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = \frac{t}{t_0}$$

where \(t_0\) is plasma's time. Plasma fills the bench's millilitre in 9.2 s; blood at a haematocrit of 0.45 takes 29.2 s, 3.2 times as long.

As the crowd thickens the cells begin to meet, and the second instalment arrives. Count the meetings the way §1.1 counted contacts. The chance that one cell has a neighbour within reach grows with the crowding, \(H\); the number of cells doing the meeting also grows with \(H\); so the number of cell-on-cell rubbings in a given volume grows as \(H \times H = H^2\). The first friction grows like a line and the second like a square, and a square overtakes a line: that is the bend. Read it off Pries's compilation of wide-tube measurements, which is what the bench's fills are. At \(H = 0.2\) blood reads 1.7 times plasma where the dilute line says 1.5, an excess of 0.2; at 0.45 it reads 3.2 where the line says 2.1, an excess of 1.1. The crowding went up 2.25-fold and the excess went up 5.4-fold, and \(2.25^2 = 5.1\): the excess is a square, about \(5H^2\) on this compilation from 0.2 to 0.45. Put the two instalments together, the line and the square:

eq. 7.3.2 — the crowd's square: cells alone, plus pairs $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = 1 + 2.5\,H + k\,H^2, \qquad k \approx 5$$

where \(k\) counts how much each pair within reach costs, read off the measured curve. At \(k = 0\) it is eq. 7.3.1 again. Above 0.45 the measured curve grows faster still, 4.9 times plasma by \(H = 0.6\) against the square's 4.3, because in a crowd that dense the cells jam in threes and fours, which a count of pairs misses. Blood sits on the steep part of its own curve.

Why forty-five, then? From the late 1950s physiologists asked how the oxygen blood delivers depends on its haematocrit (Crowell, Ford & Lewis 1959; Crowell & Smith 1967). More cells carry more oxygen, and more cells cost more pressure; the curve says how much more, so put the two on one line. The oxygen a vessel delivers each second goes as the cell fraction times the flow, \(H \times Q\). At a fixed pressure drop, eq. 7.1.3 makes the flow inversely proportional to the viscosity, \(Q \propto 1/\eta_{app}\). So the oxygen delivered for each unit of pressure the heart spends is

eq. 7.3.3 — oxygen delivered per unit of pressure $$\text{oxygen per unit pressure} \;\propto\; \frac{H}{\eta_{app}/\eta_{plasma}}$$

a fraction whose top grows like a line and whose bottom grows like the curve above it. At small \(H\) the top wins, at large \(H\) the bottom wins, and in between there is a best haematocrit. Einstein's line alone never makes one: \(H/(1 + 2.5H)\) only rises, so cells that only disturbed the plasma would always carry more oxygen. The crowd's square does: with \(1 + 2.5H + kH^2\) in the bottom the fraction stops rising where \(kH^2 = 1\), at \(H = 1/\sqrt{k}\) (the 2.5 cancels), 0.45 for \(k \approx 5\). The fills top out a little lower, at 0.40, because above 0.45 they climb faster than a square, and a steeper climb pulls the top down.

Arrhenius's extension. The square was fitted to the fills. There is an older law that needs no fitting. Svante Arrhenius, the Swedish chemist, had been writing the viscosity of a solution as an exponential of its concentration since 1887, and in 1917 he set Einstein's law beside measured suspensions (Arrhenius 1917). Written with Einstein's 2.5, so that it starts on Einstein's line, his exponential is the law optimal-haematocrit theory uses (Stark & Schuster 2012; Schuster & Stark 2014): \(\eta_{app}/\eta_{plasma} = e^{2.5H}\). One way to see why it should be an exponential: Einstein's 2.5 assumes every cell is added to plasma. Add blood's cells a spoonful at a time and only the first spoonful goes into plasma; every later one goes into blood already thickened by the ones before. If each spoonful still raises the viscosity by 2.5 times its share of the volume, but now 2.5 times that share of whatever the viscosity already is, the rises compound, the way interest does, and compounding is an exponential. For small \(H\) it is Einstein's line again; written out further,

$$e^{2.5H} = 1 + 2.5H + 3.1H^2 + 2.6H^3 + \dots$$

it is the line, then a crowd's square with \(k = 2.5^2/2 = 3.1\) that nobody fitted, then the threes and fours the square missed. Against the fills it runs a little low but close: 1.65 at 0.2 where they read 1.7, 3.1 at 0.45 where they read 3.2, within four per cent all the way to 0.45. Only the densest crowd pulls away: 4.5 at 0.6 against 4.9.

Now put it in the bottom of eq. 7.3.3, and read the top of the hill in words first. Add a few more cells, \(dH\). The oxygen each litre carries rises by the fraction \(dH/H\). The viscosity rises by the fraction \(2.5\,dH\), the same at every \(H\), and at a fixed pressure the flow falls by that same fraction. At low haematocrit the first fraction is the bigger, and more cells pay; at high haematocrit the second is, and they cost; the top is where the two are equal, \(1/H = 2.5\). The same thing by the derivative:

$$\frac{d}{dH}\left(H\,e^{-2.5H}\right) = e^{-2.5H}\,(1 - 2.5H) = 0 \quad\text{where}\quad 2.5H = 1$$

That is Arrhenius's extension of Einstein's law, and the best haematocrit it gives:

eq. 7.3.4 — Arrhenius's extension: Einstein's 2.5 compounded, and the top of the hill $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = e^{2.5H} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad H^{*} = \frac{1}{2.5} = 0.40$$

The number Einstein worked out for sugar in 1911 sets the top: one over it, 0.40, with nothing fitted. The fills top out there too (0.395 on this compilation). Arrhenius's curve gets the top right even though it runs a few per cent low, because the top depends only on how fast the viscosity climbs in proportion, and near 0.40 the fills climb about two and a half per cent for each extra hundredth of haematocrit: Einstein's 2.5, compounded. At the top Arrhenius makes blood \(e \approx 2.72\) times as viscous as plasma, where the fills read 2.8, an agreement Schuster and Stark point to (2014). This is the headline of optimal haematocrit theory: the haematocrit that delivers the most oxygen for each unit of pressure is about 40 per cent, close to what people carry (Stark & Schuster 2012; Schuster & Stark 2014).

Two cautions keep it honest. Einstein's 2.5 is for rigid spheres far apart, and red cells are soft discs packed shoulder to shoulder; the theory works, in its authors' words, "despite considerable simplifications such as neglecting the deformation, orientation and aggregation of erythrocytes" (Schuster & Stark 2014). And the top is flat: anything from 0.30 to 0.50 delivers within four per cent of the best, and 0.45, this module's normal blood, within one per cent; across vertebrates the theory predicts haematocrits of 0.3 to 0.5, which is what is observed (Šitina, Stark & Schuster 2024). So the body runs its blood on a broad, flat top at the knee of the curve. It is only the first term of the body's accounting: the heart's work, the vessels' own responses, exercise (which moves the best value up) and the oxygen the plasma carries are further terms, and living vessels read higher than glass (§3.2). But it is the term that fixes the neighbourhood.

In a slow venule, the crowd is also §1.3's crowd: at low shear the core of the tube is where the rouleaux sit, a plug of stacked cells carried along by the sheared blood around it. The fills below are measured, not computed from cells: they are Pries's compilation of tube measurements, and the next section follows the same compilation into narrower tubes.

More cells, more rubbing, and faster than the count. Einstein's dilute law (eq. 7.3.1), worked out for sugar in water, counts cells and grows like \(H\); the crowd counts pairs and grows like \(H^2\), about \(5H^2\) on the measured curve (eq. 7.3.2); so the apparent viscosity climbs gently and then steeply. Oxygen per unit of pressure, the haematocrit over the relative viscosity (eq. 7.3.3), has no top on Einstein's line alone. Compound Einstein's 2.5, as Arrhenius's \(e^{2.5H}\) does (eq. 7.3.4), and the top falls at \(1/2.5 = 0.40\), where the measured fills top out too, on a top so flat that 0.45 delivers within one per cent of it: the body runs its blood at the knee of that curve.

2. Why does the dilute law's friction grow like \(H\) but the crowd's like \(H^2\)?

Answer

The first counts cells: each one disturbs the plasma on its own. The second counts pairs: the number of cells, times the chance that each has a neighbour within reach, and both grow with \(H\). A count of pairs is a square.

3. Einstein's line gives no best haematocrit; Arrhenius's exponential, built on the same 2.5, gives 0.40. What does the exponential have that the line lacks?

Answer

Compounding. Add a few cells, \(dH\): the oxygen each litre carries rises by the fraction \(dH/H\), which shrinks as the blood fills. On Arrhenius's curve the viscosity rises by the fraction \(2.5\,dH\) at every \(H\), so the cost catches the gain where \(1/H = 2.5\). On Einstein's line it rises by \(2.5\,dH/(1 + 2.5H)\), which shrinks too and always stays below \(dH/H\) (multiply both by \(H/dH\): \(2.5H/(1 + 2.5H)\) is less than 1), so the cost never catches the gain and there is no top.

3.2 · The Fåhræus–Lindqvist story: a viscosity that falls with the tube

Told as the mystery it was. In 1931 two Swedes, Robin Fåhræus and Torsten Lindqvist, pushed blood through glass tubes of ever smaller bore and measured the flow each pressure bought. They read the result back through Poiseuille's law, §1.2 run backwards: the viscosity that eq. 7.1.3 would need to explain the flow they saw:

eq. 7.1.3 again — read backwards: the apparent viscosity of whatever is in the tube $$\eta_{app} = \frac{\pi R^4\,\Delta P}{8\,Q\,L}$$

Everything on the right is measured: the bore \(R\) and length \(L\) of the glass, the pressure \(\Delta P\) they set and the flow \(Q\) they collected. The number on the left is a definition, not a discovery: the viscosity a plain fluid would have needed to give that flow. Divided by plasma's, it is the relative viscosity the chart below plots. For water, or plasma, it comes out the same in every tube. For blood it did not.

In tubes wider than about three hundred microns it was the number you have, about three times plasma. Below that it was less, and in the narrowest they drew, forty microns across, it was about six tenths of the value at three hundred. The apparent viscosity of blood fell as the tube narrowed: on the compilation that later filled their curve in, 3.2 times plasma in a wide tube, 3.0 at three hundred microns, 2.4 at a hundred, 1.9 at forty.

How can a fluid care how wide its pipe is? Water does not; its viscosity is the same number in any tube you like. The answer is that blood is not a fluid. The cells drift toward the axis, where the flow is fast, and leave a sleeve of nearly pure plasma against the wall. Look back at §1.1's three frictions. The wall only ever feels plasma, so the cell–wall rubbing all but stops; the cells still rub each other in the core, but the core is lubricated against the wall by a layer that costs almost nothing to shear. The narrower the tube, the bigger a share of its radius that sleeve is, so the cheaper the tube gets. (The same drift explains a second oddity Fåhræus found two years earlier: the cells ride the fast centre, so at any instant a tube holds a smaller fraction of cells than the blood flowing through it; the tube's haematocrit is below the blood's.)

Put a number on the sleeve. The simplest picture with the right shape is two fluids in one pipe (Haynes 1960): a core of radius \(R_c\) that carries the cells, with the crowd's viscosity \(\eta_c\) from §3.1 (3.2 times plasma), inside a sleeve of pure plasma that runs from \(R_c\) out to the wall at \(R\). One pressure pushes both. Run §1.2's derivation twice, once for each layer, and join them where they meet (the Derive block below does it); the tube's apparent viscosity, relative to plasma, comes out as

eq. 7.3.5 — the two-layer tube: a core of cells inside a sleeve of plasma $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = \frac{1}{\,1 - \left(\dfrac{R_c}{R}\right)^{4}\left(1 - \dfrac{\eta_{plasma}}{\eta_c}\right)}$$

Read it at its two ends. In a wide tube the sleeve is a negligible share of the radius, \(R_c/R \to 1\), and the line collapses to \(\eta_c/\eta_{plasma}\): the tube reads the crowd, 3.2. Take the cells away, \(\eta_c \to \eta_{plasma}\), and it reads 1: plasma. In between, the sleeve enters as the fourth power of the core's share, the same fourth power as eq. 7.1.3, and that is why so thin a layer matters so much: a sleeve only a tenth of the radius thick makes the core count for only \(0.9^4 = 0.66\) of the tube, and hands the other third to the plasma. Now ask what sleeve the numbers on the curve need. At 300 µm, a reading of 3.0 needs a sleeve of 1.3 µm; at 40 µm, 1.9 needs 1.7 µm; and a sleeve of a micron and a half, held fixed, reproduces the measured curve within six per cent from 300 µm down to 20 µm. The sleeve is about a fifth of a red cell's width in every tube. It never grew. What grew as the tube shrank was the share of the radius that a fixed micron and a half is: one per cent at 300 µm, fifteen per cent at 20 µm. That is the whole fall, from one layer of plasma thinner than the cell's own rim.

Then the question that hung for decades. Capillaries are narrower than a red cell. If the viscosity keeps falling as the tube narrows, what happens there? The 1931 pipettes were never pulled fine enough to see it. Had they kept going, the curve bottoms out near the cell's own size, about seven microns, where blood reads only one and a quarter times plasma; and then it turns. What happens on the far side of that minimum is §3.4.

One curve, one sleeve. From a millimetre down to seven microns the apparent viscosity falls because the cells leave the wall; a plasma sleeve about a micron and a half thick, entering as the fourth power of the core's share, draws the fall from 300 µm down to 20 µm. Below about 15 µm the tube is too few cells across to have a fluid core, and §3.4 takes over. Living vessels read higher than glass at every size, about four times higher in a 10 µm vessel (Pries 1994), because their walls are lined with a soft layer that bare glass lacks; but the shape of the curve is the same.

The sleeve is also where the T cell ends up. A red cell is a soft disc that folds into the fast core; a T cell is a stiffer, rounder thing of about the same size, and the crowd of red cells squeezes it outward until it is rolling along in the plasma sleeve against the wall, most efficiently at the low shear and middling haematocrit of a venule, and more so when the red cells are aggregating (Goldsmith & Spain 1984; Fedosov & Gompper 2014). That is margination, and it answers the second half of this module's opening question: nothing grabbed the T cell and moved it to the side; the red cells did, by crowding the centre. §3.3's bench draws the T cell where margination leaves it, against the wall; the push that gets it there is not computed here, and its mechanics is a story for another course.

Derive eq. 7.3.5, the two-layer tube: §1.2's pipe run twice, and joined

The picture. A core of radius \(R_c\) with viscosity \(\eta_c\); a sleeve of plasma with viscosity \(\eta_{plasma}\) from \(R_c\) to the wall at \(R\); one pressure drop \(\Delta P\) over a length \(L\). Both layers are treated as plain Newtonian fluids, and the core's viscosity is the crowd's, held at §3.1's wide-tube value.

  1. The force balance does not care what the fluid is. §1.2's cylinder of radius \(r\) is pushed by \(\Delta P\,\pi r^2\) and held by the shear on its side, \(\tau(r)\,2\pi r L\), so \(\tau(r) = \Delta P\,r/2L\) in both layers alike (§1.3 kept the same balance with a different fluid).
  2. The sleeve, from the wall in. No slip at \(R\), plasma's viscosity: eq. 7.1.2 with \(\eta_{plasma}\),
    $$u_s(r) = \frac{\Delta P}{4\eta_{plasma}L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right), \qquad R_c \le r \le R$$
  3. The core, joined at the seam. The core moves with the sleeve at \(r = R_c\) and then obeys its own viscosity inward:
    $$u_c(r) = u_s(R_c) + \frac{\Delta P}{4\eta_c L}\left(R_c^2 - r^2\right), \qquad 0 \le r \le R_c$$
  4. Add up the flow, ring by ring. \(Q = \int_0^R u\,2\pi r\,dr\) over both layers. The sleeve's rings give \((R^2 - R_c^2)^2/2\eta_{plasma}\) and the core's give \((R^2 - R_c^2)R_c^2/\eta_{plasma} + R_c^4/2\eta_c\), each times \(\pi\Delta P/4L\). Collect:
    $$Q = \frac{\pi\,\Delta P}{8L}\left[\frac{R^4 - R_c^4}{\eta_{plasma}} + \frac{R_c^4}{\eta_c}\right]$$
    With \(R_c = R\) this is eq. 7.1.3 for the core alone; with \(R_c = 0\) it is eq. 7.1.3 for plasma.
  5. Read it back through eq. 7.1.3. The apparent viscosity is whatever single \(\eta\) gives this \(Q\): \(\eta_{app} = \pi R^4 \Delta P / 8QL\), so
    $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = \frac{1}{1 - \left(\dfrac{R_c}{R}\right)^4 + \left(\dfrac{R_c}{R}\right)^4 \dfrac{\eta_{plasma}}{\eta_c}} = \frac{1}{1 - \left(\dfrac{R_c}{R}\right)^4\left(1 - \dfrac{\eta_{plasma}}{\eta_c}\right)}$$
The sleeve enters only through \((R_c/R)^4\), the same fourth power that made eq. 7.1.3 so sensitive to the radius. What the picture leaves out, and declares: the core is a little denser than the blood that entered the tube, because the sleeve holds no cells (Fåhræus's other effect), so its viscosity is a little above 3.2 and the true sleeve a little thicker than the one read here; and the cells do not stop dead at a sharp seam. The shape is the point, and the measured curve is the evidence. The harness checks the closed form against a ring-by-ring sum of the two-layer field.

3.3 · What the wall feels

The wall of a vessel is a single layer of endothelial cells, and what they feel of the flow is the shear at the wall. §1.2's profile is a parabola, and the slope of a parabola at its edge is easy: the shear rate at the wall is

eq. 7.3.6 — the wall shear rate $$\dot\gamma_w = \frac{4Q}{\pi R^3} = \frac{4\bar u}{R} = \frac{8\bar u}{D}$$

and the stress the wall feels is eq. 7.1.1 with that rate, \(\tau_w = \eta\,\dot\gamma_w\), where \(\eta\) is the viscosity of whatever touches the wall. Notice that \(\eta\) cancelled out of the rate: for one fluid filling the vessel, the rate is set by the flow and the geometry alone. Run Tab 2's tree through it. The aorta, wide, shears its wall at only about sixty per second on its mean flow, and about three hundred at the systolic peak; the arterioles, narrow and still brisk, at the highest rates in the tree, over five hundred; the venules at about a hundred on the tree's mean speed, and between about 50 and 800 measured in living mesentery. The stress is the rate times a viscosity, and which viscosity is a real question, because blood is not one fluid at its wall (the next paragraph). In the large vessels the arteries read about one to seven pascals and the veins about a tenth to six tenths, and the endothelium reads these numbers: steady laminar shear keeps it quiet and healthy, while the low and reversing shear at bends and branches is where atherosclerosis begins. That is the one line F25's blood lecture gave this fact, and it is all this module needs of it.

Blood is not one fluid at its wall. §3.2 put a sleeve of plasma there, about a micron and a half thick, and let the core slide on it, so the profile is no longer one parabola and eq. 7.3.6's slope no longer holds at the wall. What still holds is §1.3's force balance: a cylinder of fluid filling the vessel is pushed by \(\Delta P\,\pi R^2\) and held by the wall's stress on its side, so \(\tau_w = \Delta P\,R/2L\) (eq. 7.1.6) whatever is inside. And the push the venule needs is eq. 7.1.3's with blood's apparent viscosity in it, \(\Delta P = 8\eta_{app}L\bar u/R^2\). Put the two together:

eq. 7.3.7 — the wall through its sleeve: blood's stress, felt through plasma $$\tau_w = \frac{\Delta P\,R}{2L} = \eta_{app}\,\frac{8\bar u}{D}, \qquad \dot\gamma_{wall} = \frac{\tau_w}{\eta_{plasma}} = \frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}}\,\frac{8\bar u}{D}$$

The wall feels the stress the push sets, charged at blood's apparent viscosity, and it feels it through plasma, so the plasma at the wall is sheared harder than eq. 7.3.6 says by the factor \(\eta_{app}/\eta_{plasma}\) that §3.2's curve gives the vessel: 1.8 in a 30 µm venule. Eq. 7.3.6 stays the number for one fluid, and the rate the flow sets; the apparent-viscosity charge is the one the in-vivo measurements use.

And the core is §1.3's fluid. Blood's crowd has a yield stress, \(\tau_y\), taken here at 0.005 Pa, the upper end of what has been measured at this haematocrit (§1.3). Wherever the stress falls below it, near the axis, the core does not shear: it rides as a plug. The force balance puts the stress at radius \(r\) at \(\tau_w\,r/R\), so the plug's edge is eq. 7.1.6's \(r_p/R = \tau_y/\tau_w\). In the venule by the bite that is 15 µm × 0.005/0.57 = 0.13 µm, under one per cent of the radius: at a millimetre a second the plug is there, but far too small to change the wall. Slow the flow and it grows, because \(\tau_w\) falls with the speed and \(\tau_y\) does not: at 0.02 mm/s the plug is a third of the radius, at 0.01 mm/s three fifths. A flatter profile that carries the same flow has to turn more sharply at the wall, so the wall is sheared harder than \(8\bar u/D\) by more than the sleeve's factor: 2.5 times near stasis, against 1.78 at the venule's own speed. That is the slow end of §1.4's story, where the stacks form and the blood stalls.

The T cell, at the wall. Put the numbers on the venule by the bite: 30 µm across, blood moving at about a millimetre per second. Eq. 7.3.6 gives 267 s⁻¹; plasma at that rate would put 0.32 Pa on the wall, and whole blood as one fluid 0.93 Pa. The sleeve gives the answer between them: blood in a 30 µm tube reads 1.78 times plasma on §3.2's curve, so the wall feels 0.57 Pa, 5.7 dynes per square centimetre, through plasma sheared at about 480 s⁻¹. The T cell is now where §3.2 left it: against the wall of a postcapillary venule, its foot in the plasma sleeve and its centre 3.5 µm out, past the 1.6 µm sleeve, where the flow moves at 0.95 mm/s. Tab 4 estimates that speed from eq. 7.3.6 alone, as the cell's radius times the wall rate, 0.93 mm/s; the two agree to about two per cent, so the simple estimate holds. What that flow does to a cell that is touching the wall is Tab 4's last number and Module 8's first section.

The wall feels the push, through whatever touches it. For one fluid \(\dot\gamma_w = 4\bar u/R\), a few hundred per second in the venule and the highest in the arterioles, and the viscosity cancels out of it. Blood puts plasma at its wall, so the stress is the force balance's \(\Delta P\,R/2L = \eta_{app}\,8\bar u/D\), 0.57 Pa in the venule by the bite, and the plasma there is sheared 1.8 times harder than eq. 7.3.6 says. The core's yield stress makes a plug at the axis that matters only as the flow nearly stops. The force that flow puts on a cell at the wall is what every bond in Module 8 will have to hold against.

3.4 · The inverse Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect: when the tube is smaller than the cell

Pick the curve up where §3.2 put it down, at its minimum. Fåhræus and Lindqvist's finest tube was about forty microns; Pries's compilation, with a capillary viscometer of its own, took glass tubes down to 3.3 µm (Pries 1992). Below about seven microns, the cell's own size, the apparent viscosity stops falling and climbs, steeply: 1.25 times plasma at 7 µm, 1.5 at 5, 2.3 at 4 and 4.1 at 3.3 µm, the narrowest tube measured, toward a wall at about 2.7 µm through which a red cell cannot pass at all (Secomb & Pries 2013). Narrower tube, higher viscosity: the Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect run backwards, which is why it is called the inverse effect.

What changed is the sleeve. A red cell is 7.8 µm across and the tube is now narrower than that, so the cell cannot ride the fast centre at a comfortable distance from the wall. It is pushed into the lumen, folds into the parachute that Module 8 §1.1 draws, and is pressed against the wall all round; the micron and a half of free plasma that lubricated §3.2's tube is squeezed down to a film. And the cell moves as one body. There is no shearing inside it, as there was inside §3.2's core; the whole difference in speed between the cell and the wall is taken up across that film, and eq. 7.1.1 says what that costs: a stress of \(\eta_{plasma}\) times the cell's speed over the film's thickness. Halve the film and you double the toll, at the same speed.

The same line, used twice. No new physics is needed. Over the length of one cell, take §3.2's two-layer line, eq. 7.3.5, and let the core be a cell that cannot shear, \(\eta_c \to \infty\), so that \(\eta_{plasma}/\eta_c \to 0\); the sleeve becomes the film, \(\delta = R - R_c\), and that stretch of tube reads \(1/[1 - (R_c/R)^4]\): plasma's 1 plus a toll that, when the film is thin beside the radius, is about \(R/4\delta\). Between the cells the tube holds plain plasma and reads 1. The cells take up about \(H\) of the length, so the whole tube reads plasma plus \(H\) cells' worth of tolls:

eq. 7.3.8 — single file: plasma, plus a toll for every cell $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} \;\approx\; 1 + H\,\frac{R}{4\,\delta}$$

(the Derive block below keeps the two small factors this drops, and the bench uses the exact form). In §3.2 the fourth power was the sleeve's friend: a sleeve a tenth of the radius thick handed a third of the tube to the plasma. Here it is the cell's enemy: as the film closes, the toll \(R/4\delta\) climbs without limit. Ask what film the measured climb implies, the way §3.2 asked what sleeve the fall implied. At 7 µm, a reading of 1.25 needs a film of about a micron, 0.97 µm: §3.2's sleeve at the same tube (0.93 µm), with nowhere left to go. At 5 µm the film is 0.44 µm; at 4 µm, 0.15 µm; at 3.3 µm, 0.06 µm, sixty nanometres, under a hundredth of the cell's width. The cell has been pressed to the wall until the plasma between them is thinner than the soft lining a living wall carries, and at about 2.7 µm there is no film left and the cell does not go through.

Two more things the compilation saw there, and the first is now arithmetic. In tubes of six microns and less the viscosity rises linearly with haematocrit, while in tubes of nine microns and more it rises faster than linearly (Pries 1992). Eq. 7.3.8 says why: in single file each cell pays its own film's toll and \(H\) counts the cells, so the reading is 1 plus a count of cells, a straight line in \(H\); no cell rubs another, so §3.1's square term is gone. The film the line needs is then the same at every haematocrit, which is what lets the bench read it. And living capillaries read higher than glass, four times higher at 10 µm (Pries 1994): the wall's soft lining takes a share of the film, so the plasma left to shear is thinner still. Module 8 §1.1 opens inside this tube, with the folded cell, the film and the one-cell wall, and asks what crosses that film in the second the cell spends there.

Below the cell's size the sleeve becomes a film, and the fourth power turns against the cell. A cell that cannot shear takes the whole speed difference across a plasma film \(\delta\) thick; each cell's toll goes as \(R/4\delta\) and the tube reads about \(1 + H\,R/4\delta\) (eq. 7.3.8), from a micron of film at 7 µm to sixty nanometres at 3.3 µm, and no passage near 2.7. The picture. Cells in single file, each a plug of radius \(R_c = R - \delta\) that cannot shear, moving at a speed \(U\) with a film of plasma between it and the wall; plain plasma in the gaps between them. One flow \(Q\) passes every cross-section.

  2. Along a gap. Eq. 7.1.3 for plasma: over a gap of length \(g\), \(\Delta P_g = 8\eta_{plasma}Q\,g/\pi R^4\).
  3. Add the drops and read back through eq. 7.1.3. With cells taking up a share \(L_c/L\) of the length,
    $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = 1 + \frac{L_c}{L}\,\frac{R_c^4}{R^4 - R_c^4}$$
  4. How much of the length is cells. The cells carry a share \(H\) of the volume that flows out (Pries's tubes set this, the discharge haematocrit), but they ride faster than the mean, so the tube holds fewer of them at any instant: its own haematocrit is \(H\,(R^2 + R_c^2)/2R^2\), Fåhræus's tube effect of §3.2.   5. Combine.
    $$\frac{\eta_{app}}{\eta_{plasma}} = 1 + H\,\frac{(R_c/R)^2}{2\,[1 - (R_c/R)^2]} \;\approx\; 1 + H\,\frac{R}{4\,\delta}$$
    the second form, eq. 7.3.8, for a film thin beside the radius (the exact toll is \(R/4\delta\) less about 3/8).
The reading is linear in \(H\) by construction: each cell adds its own toll and no cell meets another. 4.1 · The T cell's trip to the bite, on a clock

Everything this module measured is now put on one clock and asked one question at a time, the way the story asks it. How long is one lap? Five litres of blood, pumped at five litres a minute: the whole volume goes round once a minute, so a cell that has just left the heart is back at it, on average, in sixty seconds. It is a wide average: a lap through the coronary bed is seconds, a lap through a resting calf's veins can be minutes, because the veins are where blood waits (§2.2).

How many T cells pass the bite each minute? The venule by the bite carries, by §1.2's worked example, about 0.7 nanolitres a second, 42 nanolitres a minute. A microlitre of blood holds about a thousand T cells (the reference interval runs from about 540 to 1 790), so that venule is passed by about forty T cells a minute. Of those, the ones carrying a receptor for this particular saliva are rare, a small minority at best, which is why the body does not rely on any one pass: the T cell that does carry it will come round again, and again, roughly once a minute, and the venule beside the bite will be one of thousands it brushes past. Module 8's capstone is what happens on the pass that counts.

How fast is it going when it gets there, and what pushes it? By continuity it has slowed from 0.19 m/s in the aorta (0.9 at the top of a beat) to about a millimetre per second in the venule, six hundred times. By §3.2 the red cells have pushed it into the plasma sleeve, against the wall, where §3.3 says the shear rate is 270 per second. A cell in a free stream feels Stokes' drag,

eq. 1.3.3 again — Stokes' drag, at the speed of the fluid beside the wall $$F = 6\pi\eta a v \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad F_{wall} \approx 6\pi\eta\,a\,(a\dot\gamma_w)$$

and a cell sitting against the wall is pushed by the fluid moving past its middle, one radius out from the wall, where the shear flow's speed is the shear rate times the cell's radius. For a 3.5 µm T cell in plasma at 270 per second that speed is 0.93 mm/s and the push is about 74 piconewtons (about three times that if the wall felt whole blood rather than the sleeve). It is an estimate: a cell rubbing a wall is pushed somewhat harder than this, and the course does not correct for it. Keep that number; it is what every bond in Module 8 must hold against.

and at the numbers F25's own homework gave a T cell (a 7 µm cell, \(D = 100\) µm²/s, 0.04 molecules per cubic micron), that floor is 0.13 seconds: the counting alone would take the whole time it has, before the cell has decided anything or done anything about it.
