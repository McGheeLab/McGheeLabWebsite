Coherence, Interference & the Microscope

Optics Foundations · Lesson 5 of 5 — Two ordinary bulbs never make stripes, but two laser beams paint crisp bright-and-dark bands. Those interference patterns encode the finest detail light can carry — and set the hard limit on the smallest thing a microscope can ever resolve. This is where Lessons 1–4 land: how we actually see the very small.

Background: coherent light interferes, ordinary light just adds up

Shine two flashlights on the same wall and you get one brighter patch — never a set of stripes. Yet two beams split from a single laser overlap into a striking pattern of bright and dark bands. The difference is a property called coherence: whether the waves keep a steady, predictable relationship with one another.

Light is a wave (Lesson 1). When two waves cross, their heights add at every point — superposition. Where crest lands on crest, they reinforce into a bigger crest: constructive interference, a bright spot. Where crest lands on trough, they cancel: destructive interference, a dark spot. The deciding factor is the phase difference — how far one wave's crests are shifted relative to the other's.

A laser is coherent: its waves march in lockstep, so the phase difference between two beams stays fixed. The bright and dark spots sit still, and you see a stable interference pattern. Two ordinary bulbs are incoherent: each atom emits its own little wavetrain at a random, ever-shifting phase, billions of times a second. The bright and dark spots still form — but they jump to new positions faster than your eye (or a camera) can catch, so they average out to smooth, uniform brightness. The stripes are washed away.

$$ \text{constructive (bright): } \Delta = m\lambda \qquad \text{destructive (dark): } \Delta = \left(m+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda \qquad m=0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots $$

Here \(\Delta\) is the path difference — the extra distance one wave travels to reach a point compared with the other. A whole number of wavelengths and the two arrive in step (bright); a half-wavelength off and they arrive opposed (dark). Coherence is simply what makes this bookkeeping hold still long enough to see.

Two coherent sources sit 10 µm apart, emitting \(\lambda = 500\ \text{nm}\) light. At a point straight ahead (equidistant from both), the path difference is \(\Delta = 0\) — a whole number of wavelengths (\(m=0\)), so it is bright. Step sideways until one source is exactly \(250\ \text{nm} = \lambda/2\) farther than the other: now \(\Delta = \lambda/2\), the waves cancel, and that spot is dark. Keep stepping and bright and dark alternate — a fixed fringe pattern. Make the sources incoherent and that same geometry produces only a uniform glow: the fringes are still "there" instant by instant, but their positions scramble and blur to nothing.

  • Waves add by superposition: crest+crest = bright, crest+trough = dark.
  • Bright when path difference \(\Delta=m\lambda\); dark when \(\Delta=(m+\tfrac12)\lambda\).
  • Coherent light (a laser) holds a fixed phase relationship → a stable, visible fringe pattern.
  • Incoherent light (bulbs) has random, shifting phase → fringes wash out to uniform brightness.
  1. Start on Laser (coherent). Watch the expanding wavefronts from the two sources overlap into a fixed lattice of bright and dark bands.
  2. Drag Source separation. Closer sources spread the bright bands apart; farther sources pack more, finer bands into the field.
  3. Switch to Lamp (incoherent). The rings still expand, but now the pattern flickers and re-scrambles every frame — averaged over time it is just uniform brightness. Read the fringe visibility stat collapse toward zero.
  4. Flip back and forth. Same geometry, same wavelength — only coherence decides whether you see crisp stripes or a featureless glow.

Background: two slits turn fine structure into a wide pattern

Thomas Young's classic experiment makes interference impossible to miss. Send coherent light through two narrow slits a distance \(d\) apart. Each slit acts as a fresh source of spreading waves, and on a screen a distance \(L\) away they overlap into a row of evenly spaced bright fringes. A bright fringe appears wherever the two paths differ by a whole number of wavelengths.

Working out the geometry for a screen far away (\(L \gg d\)) gives a beautifully simple spacing between neighboring bright fringes:

$$ \Delta y \;=\; \frac{\lambda L}{d} \qquad\text{and along the screen}\qquad I(y) \;=\; I_0\,\cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi\, d\, y}{\lambda L}\right) $$

Read that spacing carefully, because it is the seed of everything in Tab 4. The fringe spacing \(\Delta y\) is inversely proportional to the slit separation \(d\): push the slits closer together and the pattern spreads wider apart. It is also proportional to \(\lambda\): redder (longer-wavelength) light spreads wider than blue. So a piece of fine structure (small \(d\)) throws a coarse, wide interference pattern, while coarse structure (large \(d\)) throws a fine, tightly-packed one. Fine detail and wide diffraction are two sides of one coin — remember that when we ask how small a microscope can see.

Green laser light, \(\lambda = 532\ \text{nm}\), passes through slits \(d = 0.10\ \text{mm}\) apart onto a screen \(L = 2.0\ \text{m}\) away. The fringe spacing is \(\Delta y = \lambda L / d = (532\times10^{-9})(2.0)/(1.0\times10^{-4}) = 1.06\times10^{-2}\ \text{m} \approx 10.6\ \text{mm}\) — a centimetre between bright bands, easily seen across a room. Halve the slit spacing to \(d = 0.05\ \text{mm}\) and the fringes double to \(\approx 21\ \text{mm}\) apart. Swap to a red laser (\(\lambda = 660\ \text{nm}\)) and they widen again, to \(\approx 13.2\ \text{mm}\) at the original spacing.

  • Two coherent slits produce evenly spaced bright fringes: \(\Delta y = \lambda L / d\).
  • Fringe intensity follows \(I(y) = I_0\cos^2(\pi d y / \lambda L)\).
  • Smaller slit spacing \(d\) → wider fringes; longer \(\lambda\) → wider fringes.
  • Fine structure ↔ wide pattern: this inverse link is the origin of the diffraction limit (Tab 4).
  1. Drag Slit separation \(d\). As the slits close up, watch the bright bands on the screen fan apart — the inverse relationship in action.
  2. Drag Wavelength \(\lambda\) from blue to red. The whole pattern stretches wider as the color reddens, and the fringes recolor with it.
  3. Slide Screen distance \(L\) out. Farther screen → proportionally wider spacing (the pattern just fans out over a longer throw).
  4. Confirm the fringe spacing stat always equals \(\lambda L / d\), and check it against the two canvases: the plotted \(\cos^2\) peaks sit exactly \(\Delta y\) apart.

Background: how light actually travels through a microscope

A compound microscope is just a well-organized path for light. In a modern widefield instrument: a lamp illuminates the sample; light scattered or emitted by a point in the sample is collected by the objective lens; the objective (with a tube lens behind it) forms a magnified image at the camera or eyepiece. Trace one point of the sample and every ray it sends into the objective is bent back together to a single point in the image — that is what "in focus" means.

The single most important number for the objective is its numerical aperture, \(NA\). The objective can only gather the cone of light that falls within its rim; \(\theta\) is the half-angle of that cone. With the sample sitting in a medium of refractive index \(n\),

$$ NA \;=\; n\,\sin\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \theta \;=\; \arcsin\!\left(\frac{NA}{n}\right) $$

A wider collection cone (larger \(\theta\), larger \(NA\)) scoops up more of the light — and, crucially, more of the steeply-angled rays that carry the sample's fine detail. In air the index is \(n=1\), which caps \(NA\) below 1 (about 0.95 in practice). To go higher, high-power objectives are designed to look through a drop of immersion oil (\(n\approx1.515\)) between lens and coverslip; that raises the ceiling to \(NA\approx1.4\). Magnification only enlarges the image — it is \(NA\) that decides how much real information the objective captures. In Tab 4 we will see that \(NA\) sets the resolution outright.

A "10×" air objective has \(NA=0.30\). Its collection half-angle is \(\theta = \arcsin(0.30/1) = 17.5^\circ\) — a fairly narrow cone. A "60×" oil objective has \(NA=1.40\) with \(n=1.515\): \(\theta = \arcsin(1.40/1.515) = \arcsin(0.924) = 67.5^\circ\) — a cone nearly two-thirds of the way to grazing, gulping far more light and far more fine detail. Now defocus by moving the sample off the objective's focal plane: the rays from a point no longer reconverge on the image plane. Instead of a sharp point they land as a spread-out blur, and the image goes soft — even though the very same objective is being used.

  • Light path: illumination → sample → objective → tube lens → image (camera/eye).
  • The objective collects a cone of light set by its numerical aperture \(NA=n\sin\theta\).
  • Higher \(NA\) = wider cone = more light and more fine detail captured; oil (\(n\approx1.515\)) beats air (\(n=1\)).
  • In focus: a sample point maps to an image point. Off focus: its rays miss the image plane and blur.
  1. Start on Low-NA (10×). Note the narrow collection cone the objective accepts from the sample point, and the sharp image where the rays reconverge.
  2. Switch to High-NA oil (60×). The cone flares much wider — the objective is now gathering steeply-angled rays it could not reach before. Watch the collection half-angle stat climb toward 68°.
  3. Drag the Sample focus slider off zero (or pick Out of focus). The rays no longer meet at the image plane; they cross before or after it and paint a blur instead of a point.
  4. Nudge Magnification and confirm it changes image size but not the cone — it is \(NA\), not magnification, that governs how much light gets in.

Level up: the diffraction limit — lamp vs laser, widefield vs confocal

Now the payoff. Because of diffraction, a lens can never image a point as a point. Even a perfect objective smears every point of the sample into a small blob of light called the Airy disk — a bright central spot ringed by faint halos. This blob is the microscope's point-spread function (PSF), and it is the fundamental unit of blur that all real images are built from. Its angular radius (first dark ring) for an aperture of diameter \(D\) is \(1.22\,\lambda/D\).

Two nearby points each make their own Airy disk. As you bring them together, the disks overlap and eventually merge into one indistinguishable blob. The classic Rayleigh criterion calls them just resolved when the center of one disk falls on the first dark ring of the other — which leaves a shallow dip (about 26% below the peaks) in the combined brightness. In terms of the objective's \(NA\) this is:

$$ d_{\text{Rayleigh}} \;=\; \frac{0.61\,\lambda}{NA} \qquad\qquad d_{\text{Abbe}} \;=\; \frac{\lambda}{2\,NA} $$

Both say the same thing: resolution improves (\(d\) shrinks) with shorter wavelength and higher \(NA\). The Abbe form is slightly more optimistic than Rayleigh; both are of order \(\lambda/2\) and neither can be beaten by a conventional lens. Tie it back to Tab 2: fine detail is carried by finely-spaced fringes — high spatial frequencies — and those correspond to the most steeply-angled rays. A lens with limited \(NA\) simply cannot collect the steepest rays, so it drops the finest detail. Resolution is a question of which fringe spacings the lens can still gather.

How you illuminate matters too. A widefield microscope floods the sample with incoherent lamp light and images everything at once. A confocal microscope instead scans a focused coherent laser spot across the sample and places a pinhole in front of the detector that blocks light from everywhere except the focal point. Rejecting out-of-focus light both optically sections (you get clean slices through a thick specimen) and sharpens the effective PSF, improving lateral resolution by a factor of about \(1/\sqrt{2}\approx0.72\) in the ideal (vanishingly small pinhole) case. Confocal does not smash the diffraction limit outright — it refines it.

$$ d_{\text{confocal}} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\; d_{\text{Rayleigh}} \;=\; \frac{0.61\,\lambda}{\sqrt{2}\,NA}\;\approx\;\frac{0.43\,\lambda}{NA} $$

Green fluorescence, \(\lambda = 510\ \text{nm}\), through an oil objective with \(NA=1.4\). Widefield Rayleigh limit: \(d = 0.61\lambda/NA = 0.61\times510/1.4 = 311.1/1.4 \approx 222\ \text{nm}\). The Abbe estimate is \(\lambda/(2\,NA) = 510/2.8 \approx 182\ \text{nm}\). Switch to confocal: \(d_{\text{conf}} \approx 222/\sqrt{2} \approx 160\ \text{nm}\). So two labeled structures 180 nm apart are unresolved in a naïve reading of Abbe but sit right at the widefield Rayleigh edge — and become comfortably resolved on the confocal, which also strips away the blur from other focal planes. Drop to blue excitation (\(\lambda = 405\ \text{nm}\)) and Rayleigh falls to \(0.61\times405/1.4 \approx 176\ \text{nm}\): shorter wavelength buys resolution.

  • A point images as an Airy disk (the PSF); its size is set by \(\lambda\) and \(NA\).
  • Two points are just resolved at \(d_{\text{Rayleigh}} = 0.61\lambda/NA\); Abbe gives \(\lambda/(2NA)\).
  • Resolution improves with shorter \(\lambda\) and higher \(NA\) — never beyond ~\(\lambda/2\) for a normal lens.
  • Confocal adds a pinhole: optical sectioning + ~\(\times0.72\) sharper lateral PSF. It refines, not defeats, the limit.
  1. Set Two-point separation large and watch two clean Airy disks with a deep dark valley between them — clearly two objects.
  2. Slide them together. As the separation drops through \(d_{\text{Rayleigh}} = 0.61\lambda/NA\), the dip between the peaks fills in; below it the pair merges into a single blob. Watch the resolved? verdict flip.
  3. Raise NA or lower λ and the Rayleigh limit shrinks — two points that were merged snap back into being resolvable. This is why we reach for high-NA oil and shorter wavelengths.
  4. Switch to Confocal. Each PSF narrows by ~×0.72 and the same two points resolve at a tighter separation. Compare the Rayleigh line-profile dip in widefield vs confocal.
