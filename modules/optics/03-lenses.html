Bending Light: Lenses

Optics Foundations · Lesson 3 of 5 — In Lesson 2 you saw a flat surface tip a ray sideways. Now bow that surface into a curve. Suddenly every ray bends by just the right amount to pile up at a single point — a focus. That trick, repeated and stacked, is every lens, magnifier, camera, and telescope ever built.

Background: bow the surface, and the rays start to gather

Recall the one rule from Lesson 2: when light crosses from air into a denser material like glass, it bends toward the surface normal — the line pointing straight out of the surface at that spot. On a flat pane the normal points the same way everywhere, so a bundle of parallel rays all tip by the same angle and stay parallel. Nothing gathers.

Now curve the surface. On a bulging (convex) surface the normal fans outward: it points slightly up near the top, straight ahead at the middle, slightly down near the bottom. A ray that arrives high up meets a steeply tilted normal and bends hard downward; a ray near the axis meets an almost-flat normal and barely bends. Every ray is turned by just the right amount to head for one shared point on the axis — the focus. The bending at each spot still obeys Snell's law from last lesson:

$$ n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2 $$

Here \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) are measured from the local normal, \(n_1\approx1\) for air, and \(n_2=n\) for the glass. Two knobs decide where the rays meet. Sharpen the curvature (a smaller radius \(R\), a fatter bulge) and the normals fan more steeply, so the focus pulls in close. Raise the index \(n\) and each ray bends harder for the same tilt, which also pulls the focus in. For a single curved surface the focus sits a distance \( f \approx n R/(n-1)\) beyond it — flatten the surface (\(R\to\infty\)) and the focus races off to infinity, which is just the flat-pane "no gathering" case again.

Take a glass surface (\(n=1.5\)) bulged to a radius of curvature \(R = 30\) mm. The focus lands a distance \(f = nR/(n-1) = 1.5\times 30/(1.5-1) = 45/0.5 = 90\) mm past the surface. Now swap in a denser glass, \(n=1.8\): \(f = 1.8\times 30/0.8 = 67.5\) mm — the higher index yanks the focus 22 mm closer, even though the curve never changed.

  • A curved surface gives each ray a differently tilted normal, so Snell bends them by different amounts.
  • A convex surface aims parallel rays at one point: the focus.
  • Smaller radius \(R\) (more curve) → focus closer. Flat surface (\(R\to\infty\)) → no focus.
  • Higher index \(n\) → harder bending → focus closer: \(f \approx nR/(n-1)\).
  1. Start with Curvature nearly flat. The rays barely bend and no focus forms on screen — just like a window pane.
  2. Drag toward a strong curve (small \(R\)). Watch the outer rays bend hard, the inner ones barely, and all of them collapse onto one point. Read the focal distance shrink.
  3. Now hold the curve fixed and raise the index \(n\) from 1.0 to 2.0. The focus pulls in even though the surface never changed — denser glass bends light harder.
  4. Set \(n=1.0\) (glass same as air). Nothing bends at all: with no index jump, Snell does nothing and there is no lens.

Background: two shapes, two opposite jobs

A real lens has two curved surfaces, but for thin lenses we can skip the internal detail and treat the whole thing as one bending plane with a single number: its focal length \(f\). There are two families, set by whether the lens is fatter in the middle or fatter at the edges.

A convex (converging) lens — fat in the middle — does exactly what the curved surface in Tab 1 did: it takes parallel rays and squeezes them down to a real focus a distance \(f\) on the far side. You could hold a screen there and catch a bright dot; the light genuinely piles up in space. A concave (diverging) lens — thin in the middle, thick at the rim — does the opposite: it spreads parallel rays apart. They never cross. But if you trace the spreading rays backward (the dashed lines), they all seem to fan out from a single point on the incoming side. That is a virtual focus: no light is actually there, yet your eye, following the rays back, swears the light came from that spot.

$$ f > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{converging (real focus at } +f) \qquad f < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{diverging (virtual focus at } f<0) $$

The sign of \(f\) is the type. A short focal length (small \(|f|\)) means a strong lens that bends light sharply; a long focal length means a weak, gentle lens. We will make this quantitative in Tab 4, but the picture is the whole point here: converging = pull together to a real point; diverging = push apart from a virtual one.

A converging lens with \(f = +100\) mm sends parallel sunlight to a real focus 100 mm behind it — hot enough to scorch paper (this is the "magnifying-glass burns" trick). Flip to a diverging lens of \(f = -100\) mm: the same sunlight spreads out as if streaming from a virtual point 100 mm in front of the lens. You could never burn paper with it — there is no real hot spot to find.

  • Thin lens ≈ one bending plane described by a single focal length \(f\).
  • Convex / converging (\(f>0\)): parallel rays meet at a real focus you can project on a screen.
  • Concave / diverging (\(f<0\)): parallel rays spread; back-projected dashes meet at a virtual focus.
  • Smaller \(|f|\) = stronger lens (bends more); the sign of \(f\) sets the type.
  1. Click Converging. Drag Focal length and watch the parallel rays cross the axis exactly at \(x=f\). Shrink \(f\) and the crossing marches in toward the lens.
  2. Click Diverging (\(f\) goes negative). The rays now spread apart and never cross. Follow the dashed back-projections to the virtual focus on the left at \(x=f\).
  3. Change the number of incoming rays with Ray fan. Notice every ray, high or low, is aimed at the same focal point — that is what "focus" means.
  4. Push \(f\) toward zero from either side: the lens gets very strong and the focus crowds right up against it.

Background: an object, three rays, and where the picture lands

A focus is what a converging lens does to parallel rays. But most things you look at are not infinitely far away — they sit some finite object distance \(d_o\) in front of the lens. Now the lens does something richer: it builds an image. To find where, you do not need any equation yet — you just trace three special rays leaving the tip of the object, because any two of them locate the image where they cross:

$$ \text{(1) parallel in} \to \text{through far focus} \qquad \text{(2) through center} \to \text{straight} \qquad \text{(3) through near focus} \to \text{parallel out} $$

Ray (1) comes in parallel to the axis and, like Tab 1, leaves through the focal point on the far side. Ray (2) passes straight through the lens's center undeviated. Ray (3) goes through the near focus and emerges parallel. Wherever these cross is the tip of the image. Read off two things: the image distance \(d_i\) (how far behind the lens the picture forms) and the magnification \(M\) (how tall, and which way up). Geometry gives the same answers as the thin-lens relation you will meet in Tab 4:

$$ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i}, \qquad d_i = \frac{1}{\,1/f - 1/d_o\,}, \qquad M = -\frac{d_i}{d_o} $$

Everything hinges on where \(d_o\) sits relative to \(f\). If the object is farther than \(f\), the rays cross on the far side: a real, inverted image (\(d_i>0\), \(M<0\)) — this is how a camera and your eye work. Put the object at exactly \(2f\) and the image lands at \(2f\) too, the same size but flipped (\(M=-1\)). Slide the object inside \(f\) and the rays exit still diverging — they never cross in front. Trace them back (dashes) and they meet behind the object: a virtual, upright, magnified image (\(d_i<0\), \(M>1\)). That is precisely a magnifying glass.

Lens with \(f = 50\) mm, object at \(d_o = 150\) mm (i.e. \(3f\)). Then \(d_i = 1/(1/50 - 1/150) = 1/(0.0200 - 0.00667) = 1/0.01333 = 75\) mm — a real image 75 mm behind the lens. Magnification \(M = -d_i/d_o = -75/150 = -0.5\): half-size and inverted. Now move the object inside the focus, \(d_o = 30\) mm: \(d_i = 1/(0.0200 - 0.0333) = 1/(-0.01333) = -75\) mm. The negative sign means a virtual image 75 mm in front, and \(M = -(-75)/30 = +2.5\) — upright and 2.5× bigger. Same lens, opposite behavior, decided entirely by \(d_o\) vs \(f\).

  • Trace any two of the three principal rays; their crossing is the image tip.
  • \(1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i\), and \(M = -d_i/d_o\) (negative \(M\) = inverted).
  • Object beyond \(f\): real, inverted image (camera). At \(2f\): same size, flipped.
  • Object inside \(f\): virtual, upright, magnified image (the magnifier).
  1. Start with a far object. Watch the three rays cross on the right and drop a small, upside-down real image — the camera regime. Read \(d_i\) and \(M\) (negative).
  2. Slide the object distance in to \(2f\). The image swings out to \(2f\) and \(M\) reaches exactly \(-1\): same size, inverted (the "1:1" copy setting).
  3. Keep sliding in past the focus (\(d_odiverging; the dashed back-traces meet behind the object. The stat flips to virtual, upright and \(M>1\) — you have built a magnifying glass.
  4. Watch the moment you cross \(d_o=f\): the image shoots off to infinity, then reappears on the other side as virtual. That boundary is the whole story of this tab.

Level up: the equations, the lensmaker, and stacking lenses

Time for the real machinery. Everything you traced by eye is captured by two relations. For a single thin lens, the object and image distances and the magnification obey

$$ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i}, \qquad M = -\frac{d_i}{d_o}. $$

And where does \(f\) itself come from? From the glass and its two surface curvatures (\(R_1\) front, \(R_2\) back), through the lensmaker's equation:

$$ \frac{1}{f} = (n-1)\!\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right). $$

Now stack two thin lenses of focal lengths \(f_1\) and \(f_2\), separated by a distance \(d\). The pair behaves like one lens whose combined power depends on the spacing:

$$ \frac{1}{f_{\text{sys}}} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} - \frac{d}{f_1 f_2}. $$

That last \(-d/(f_1 f_2)\) term is where all the design freedom lives. Sitting the lenses close (\(d\to 0\)) just adds their powers — a stronger single lens. Sliding them apart moves the system focal plane: send parallel light in and the final focus (the back focal distance behind lens 2) shifts as you change \(d\). Push \(d\) all the way to the special value \(d = f_1 + f_2\) and \(1/f_{\text{sys}}\) drops to zero — the system has infinite focal length. Parallel light in comes out parallel again: an afocal telescope. It no longer forms a focus at all; instead it magnifies angles, making distant things look bigger:

$$ \text{Telescope at } d=f_1+f_2:\qquad M_\theta = -\frac{f_1}{f_2}\;=\;-\frac{f_{\text{obj}}}{f_{\text{eye}}}. $$

Between the extremes you get every useful gadget: two positive lenses spaced for a real relayed image (a relay), a strong-plus-weak pair as a beam expander, or a converging + diverging pair as a compact Galilean scope. One rail, three sliders — the whole zoo.

Take an objective \(f_1 = 100\) mm and an eyepiece \(f_2 = 50\) mm. Set the spacing to the sum, \(d = f_1 + f_2 = 150\) mm. Then \(1/f_{\text{sys}} = 1/100 + 1/50 - 150/(100\times 50) = 0.0100 + 0.0200 - 0.0300 = 0\): the system is afocal — a Keplerian telescope with angular magnification \(M_\theta = f_1/f_2 = 100/50 = 2\times\). Now nudge the spacing to \(d = 120\) mm (short of afocal): \(1/f_{\text{sys}} = 0.0300 - 120/5000 = 0.0300 - 0.0240 = 0.0060\), so \(f_{\text{sys}} = 167\) mm — the system now does refocus parallel light, and the focal plane has moved to a finite spot behind the eyepiece. Sliding \(d\) is how zoom and focus work.

  • Thin lens: \(1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i\), \(M=-d_i/d_o\). Lensmaker: \(1/f=(n-1)(1/R_1-1/R_2)\).
  • Two lenses: \(1/f_{\text{sys}} = 1/f_1 + 1/f_2 - d/(f_1 f_2)\); the spacing \(d\) tunes the system.
  • Changing \(d\) shifts the back focal distance — that is focusing and beam expanding.
  • At \(d=f_1+f_2\) the system is afocal (a telescope): no focus, angular mag \(-f_1/f_2\).
  1. Set \(f_1=100\), \(f_2=50\). Slide the separation \(d\) and watch \(f_{\text{sys}}\) and the back focal distance in the stats change — the final focus slides along the rail.
  2. Drive \(d\) to \(150\) mm (\(=f_1+f_2\)). \(1/f_{\text{sys}}\) hits zero, the exit rays go parallel, and the verdict flips to telescope with angular mag \(2\times\).
  3. Try a strong-then-weak pair with a small gap: the exit beam is wider than it went in — a beam expander. Then make lens 2 diverging (Galilean) and compare.
  4. Confirm the challenge below: for \(f=50\), \(d_o=75\), predict \(d_i\) before you read it, then check against \(1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i\).
