Scattering, Index Matching & Metamaterials

Optics Foundations · Lesson 4 of 5 — Milk, fog, and skin all look cloudy for the same reason: every time light crosses a boundary where the material's density changes, it gets nudged off course. Match that mismatch away and things turn clear; engineer the mismatch on purpose and you can bend light with a flat surface.

Background: light gets nudged at every index mismatch

Light travels in a straight line — until it hits a boundary where the material suddenly gets denser or thinner. We measure that "optical density" with the refractive index \(n\) (water is \(n=1.33\), glass \(n\approx1.5\), air \(n=1.0\)). Whenever light crosses a spot where \(n\) jumps, part of it gets deflected off to the side. We call that deflection scattering.

One tiny particle — a single fat droplet in milk, one water droplet in fog, one cell in tissue — barely bends the beam. But a real cloudy substance is packed with millions of them. Each crossing kicks the light in a slightly different random direction, and after enough kicks the beam is completely scrambled. That is why you cannot see through milk or fog: the light still gets through, but it has been randomized into a useless haze. The photons that make it straight through without being scattered are called ballistic photons, and their fraction shrinks the way any survivor-count shrinks — exponentially. This is the Beer–Lambert law:

$$ T \;=\; e^{-\mu_s L}, \qquad \ell \;=\; \frac{1}{\mu_s} $$

Here \(T\) is the fraction of light that survives straight through, \(L\) is how far it travels, and \(\mu_s\) (the scattering coefficient, units 1/length) counts how strongly the medium scatters. Its inverse \(\ell = 1/\mu_s\) is the mean free path — the average distance a photon flies before its next collision. Two things crank \(\mu_s\) up: more scatterers per volume (density), and a bigger index mismatch \(\Delta n\) at each one. In fact the strength of each bounce grows with \(\Delta n^2\), so \(\mu_s \propto \text{density}\times\Delta n^{2}\). Squeeze the particles together or make them optically very different from their surroundings, and the medium turns opaque.

Suppose a foggy sample has \(\mu_s = 2\ \text{mm}^{-1}\). The mean free path is \(\ell = 1/\mu_s = 0.5\ \text{mm}\) — light averages just half a millimetre between bounces. Send a beam \(L = 2\ \text{mm}\) deep and the ballistic survivors are \(T = e^{-\mu_s L} = e^{-(2)(2)} = e^{-4} \approx 0.018\) — under 2% get through un-scattered. Double the particle density and \(\mu_s\) doubles to \(4\ \text{mm}^{-1}\); now \(T=e^{-8}\approx0.0003\), essentially opaque. The exponent is what kills you.

  • Scattering happens at every refractive-index mismatch \(\Delta n\); one particle is weak, millions randomize the beam into haze.
  • Ballistic (un-scattered) transmission follows Beer–Lambert: \(T=e^{-\mu_s L}\).
  • Mean free path \(\ell = 1/\mu_s\) is the average flight between collisions.
  • \(\mu_s\) rises with scatterer density and with \(\Delta n^{2}\) — pack them tight or make them optically very different and it goes opaque.
  1. Drag Scatterer density from few to many. Watch the photon paths pick up more and more random kinks, and the ballistic-transmission stat plunge.
  2. Now drag the Index mismatch \(\Delta n\) up from 0. At \(\Delta n=0\) the light sails straight through even a crowded medium — no mismatch, no scattering. Small \(\Delta n\) changes have an outsized effect because \(\mu_s\propto\Delta n^{2}\).
  3. Read the mean free path \(\ell\). When \(\ell\) drops below the sample width, most photons bounce at least once and the transmitted image on the right blurs away.
  4. Click Clear water, Foggy, and Milk and watch the verdict march from "see straight through" to "opaque."

Background: erase the mismatch and the cloudiness vanishes

Here is the beautiful consequence of "scattering lives at the index mismatch." The strength of each scattering event scales with the square of the difference in refractive index between a particle and the fluid around it:

$$ \text{scattering strength} \;\propto\; \big(n_{\text{particle}} - n_{\text{medium}}\big)^{2} \;=\; \Delta n^{2} $$

Read that carefully: if you can make \(n_{\text{medium}}\) equal \(n_{\text{particle}}\), then \(\Delta n = 0\), the scattering strength goes to zero, and the object becomes transparent even though nothing about the object itself changed. The light no longer "sees" any boundaries to bounce off of, so it travels straight and you can suddenly image deep inside. This trick is called optical clearing. You soak a sample — a chunk of tissue, a pile of glass beads — in a fluid whose index is tuned to match the sample's own material (\(n_{\text{obj}}\approx1.45\) for many biological tissues). Watch clear glass beads disappear when you drop them into oil of the same index; the beads are still there, but with \(\Delta n=0\) they scatter no light.

This is exactly how modern tissue-clearing methods (CLARITY, Scale, and others) let microscopes see through a whole mouse brain: they replace the watery, mismatched interior with an index-matched medium so the tissue turns glass-clear and light penetrates millimetres instead of micrometres. Over-shoot the match — make the medium more dense than the object — and \(\Delta n\) grows again (now with the opposite sign), so the haze comes back.

A tissue sample has \(n_{\text{obj}}=1.45\). In plain water (\(n_{\text{med}}=1.33\)) the mismatch is \(\Delta n = 0.12\), so scattering strength \(\propto (0.12)^2 = 0.0144\) — cloudy. Swap in a clearing agent at \(n_{\text{med}}=1.45\): now \(\Delta n = 0\) and scattering \(\propto 0 \) — transparent. Land just short at \(n_{\text{med}}=1.44\) and \(\Delta n = 0.01\), scattering \(\propto (0.01)^2 = 0.0001\) — that is 144× weaker than water, because the strength went with \(\Delta n^{2}\), not \(\Delta n\). Getting close pays off enormously.

  • Scattering strength \(\propto (n_{\text{obj}}-n_{\text{med}})^{2}\), so it vanishes at a perfect index match.
  • Optical clearing soaks a sample in an index-matched fluid to make it transparent without altering the object itself.
  • Because the law is quadratic, halving \(\Delta n\) cuts scattering to a quarter — getting close matters a lot.
  • Over-matching (medium denser than the object) re-introduces a mismatch and the haze returns.
  1. Start in Water: the medium index sits well below the object, the surrounding fluid is milky, and the internal structure is hidden.
  2. Drag Medium index \(n_{\text{med}}\) up toward the object's \(n_{\text{obj}}\). Watch the haze thin and the hidden internal pattern sharpen as \(\Delta n\to0\).
  3. Hit the exact match (\(\Delta n=0\)): the sample turns clear and the imaging-depth stat shoots up. Read "relative scattering" — it reads 0 at the match.
  4. Keep dragging past the match (Over-matched). \(\Delta n\) grows negative, scattering returns, and the sample clouds over again. Matching is a sweet spot, not "more is better."

Background: real samples are stacks, and blur piles up layer by layer

Nothing real is one uniform block. Skin, for instance, is a sandwich: the dry outer stratum corneum, then the living epidermis, then the dermis packed with collagen, then fat below. Each of those layers has a slightly different refractive index, so every interface between them is a little index mismatch — another chance to scatter. And critically, the blur does not reset at each layer; it accumulates. A point of light that is a touch fuzzy after layer 1 gets fuzzier still crossing layer 2, and so on.

We track this with the total optical thickness, the sum of \(\mu_s L\) over the stack. Each identical layer adds the same amount, so \(N\) layers give a total \(N\,\mu_s L\), and the ballistic transmission is \(T = e^{-N\mu_s L}\) — the exponent grows with depth. The width of the blurred spot (the point-spread) widens with every layer too, roughly as \(\sqrt{N}\) because the random sideways kicks add up like a random walk:

$$ \tau_{\text{total}} = N\,\mu_s L, \qquad T = e^{-N\mu_s L}, \qquad w_{\text{blur}} \;\propto\; \sqrt{N} $$

The practical upshot: you can resolve two nearby dots only while the blur \(w_{\text{blur}}\) stays smaller than the gap between them. Through one thin surface layer, two points look like two points. Add layer after layer and their fuzzy halos swell until they merge into one smudge — the depth at which you can no longer tell them apart. This accumulation is why every deep-tissue imaging method fights so hard against scattering, and why clearing the sample (Tab 2) is such a big deal: drop each layer's \(\Delta n\) and the whole stack's blur collapses at once.

Take a layer with \(\mu_s L = 0.5\) of optical thickness. One layer transmits \(T=e^{-0.5}\approx0.61\) ballistically and blurs a point only a little. Stack \(N=6\) such layers (like skin): total \(\tau = 6\times0.5 = 3\), so \(T=e^{-3}\approx0.05\) — only 5% of light travels straight — and the blur width has grown by \(\sqrt{6}\approx2.4\times\). Two dots that were clearly separate at the surface now overlap. Go to \(N=12\) and \(T=e^{-6}\approx0.0025\): the image is essentially a uniform glow.

  • Real samples are stacks of layers; every interface adds another index mismatch and more scattering.
  • Optical thickness adds up: \(\tau_{\text{total}} = N\,\mu_s L\), and \(T=e^{-N\mu_s L}\) falls fast with depth.
  • Blur width grows about like \(\sqrt{N}\) — a random walk of sideways kicks.
  • You can resolve two points only while the blur stays smaller than their spacing; past that depth they merge.
  1. Set Number of layers to 1. The two target dots on the right are sharp and clearly separate — one thin surface layer barely blurs them.
  2. Drag the layer count up. Watch the point-spread halos on the dots swell and the ballistic transmission stat fall as \(e^{-N\mu_s L}\).
  3. Find the depth where the two halos merge into one blob: the "resolve two points?" stat flips to No. That is the deepest you could image here.
  4. Now raise Per-layer mismatch \(\Delta n\). Higher mismatch means each layer blurs more, so you lose the two dots after fewer layers. Try Deep tissue.

Level up: Rayleigh scattering, and bending light with a flat surface

We now sharpen "scattering" into real physics — and then flip it: instead of fighting random scattering, we engineer it to steer light on purpose.

(A) Rayleigh scattering — why the sky is blue. When the scatterers are much smaller than the wavelength (\(d\ll\lambda\): air molecules, tiny density fluctuations), the scattered intensity depends ferociously on wavelength. Lord Rayleigh showed the scattering cross-section goes as

$$ \sigma \;\propto\; \frac{(n-1)^2\,d^{6}}{\lambda^{4}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad I_{\text{scatter}} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{\lambda^{4}} $$

That \(1/\lambda^{4}\) is dramatic. Short (blue) wavelengths scatter far more than long (red) ones, so sunlight's blue is flung all across the sky and reaches your eye from every direction — a blue dome. At sunset the light skims a long path through the atmosphere; the blue is scattered away before it reaches you, leaving the reds and oranges to come straight through. Same law, both effects.

(B) Metasurfaces — bending light with a flat surface. A normal lens is curved so that glass makes light in the middle lag behind light at the edges, reshaping the wavefront. A metasurface does the same job with a flat array of engineered sub-wavelength pillars (nano-antennas), each imprinting a chosen phase shift \(\varphi(x)\) on the light passing through it. Arrange a phase gradient \(d\varphi/dx\) across the surface and the beam bends, obeying the generalized Snell's law:

$$ n_2\sin\theta_t - n_1\sin\theta_i \;=\; \frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi}\,\frac{d\varphi}{dx} $$

For light hitting a flat metasurface head-on in air (\(\theta_i=0,\ n_1=n_2=1\)) this simplifies to \(\sin\theta_t = \dfrac{\lambda_0}{2\pi}\dfrac{d\varphi}{dx}\): the steering angle is set entirely by how fast the phase changes across the surface. Make the phase profile increase toward the edges in just the right curved shape, \(\varphi(r) = -\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\big(\sqrt{r^2+f^2}-f\big)\), and the flat surface focuses parallel rays to a point a distance \(f\) away — a flat metalens that replaces a bulky curved one.

Blue sky. Compare blue (450 nm) to red (700 nm). Rayleigh scattering \(\propto 1/\lambda^4\), so the ratio is \(\left(\dfrac{\lambda_{\text{red}}}{\lambda_{\text{blue}}}\right)^4 = \left(\dfrac{700}{450}\right)^4 = (1.556)^4 \approx 5.85\). Blue light scatters almost 6× more than red — that surplus of scattered blue, arriving from all over the sky, is the color you see overhead.

Beam steering. Want to bend a \(\lambda_0=500\ \text{nm}\) beam to \(\theta_t=30^\circ\) at normal incidence? You need \(\dfrac{d\varphi}{dx}=\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\sin\theta_t = \dfrac{2\pi}{500\,\text{nm}}(0.5) = \dfrac{\pi}{500}\ \text{rad/nm}\) — a full \(2\pi\) of phase every 1000 nm across the surface. That is why the pillars must be sub-wavelength: you have to fit a smooth phase ramp into a space smaller than \(\lambda\).

  • Rayleigh scattering (\(d\ll\lambda\)) goes as \(1/\lambda^{4}\); blue scatters ~5.85× more than red → blue sky, red sunset.
  • A metasurface imprints a designed phase \(\varphi(x)\); a phase gradient steers a beam via the generalized Snell's law.
  • Normal incidence in air: \(\sin\theta_t = (\lambda_0/2\pi)\,d\varphi/dx\) — clamp the argument to \([-1,1]\) (beyond that the beam can't be steered).
  • A radial phase \(\varphi(r)=-\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\sqrt{r^2+f^2}-f)\) makes a flat surface focus like a lens — no curvature needed.
  1. In the Rayleigh plot, drag Wavelength across the visible. Watch the \(1/\lambda^4\) curve rocket up toward blue. Read the blue/red ratio stat — it sits near 5.85.
  2. Switch the metasurface to Steer mode and drag Phase gradient \(d\varphi/dx\). The outgoing beam swings to the angle \(\theta_t\) from the generalized Snell's law; push it hard enough and the argument hits 1 (\(\theta_t=90^\circ\)) and can go no further.
  3. Switch to Focus mode: the flat surface's radial phase profile bends parallel rays to a focal point. Compare the flat metalens to the bulky curved lens doing the same job.
  4. Try the presets: Blue sky, Steer 30°, and Metalens focus.
