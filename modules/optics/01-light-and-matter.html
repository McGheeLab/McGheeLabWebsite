Light & Matter — Why Things Absorb Color

Optics Foundations · Lesson 1 of 5 — A red apple is not "made of red." It is made of atoms that swallowed every color except red and spat that one back at your eye. To see why, we have to catch light in the act of hitting an atom.

Background: light is a travelling electromagnetic wave

Light is not a tiny bullet and it is not a glow that just "is there." It is a ripple in the electric and magnetic fields — an electromagnetic (EM) wave. Picture a cork bobbing on a pond: the water heaves up and down while the ripple races across the surface. In light, what heaves up and down is the electric field \(E\); riding along at a right angle to it is the magnetic field \(B\); and the ripple races forward at the speed of light \(c = 3.0\times10^8\ \text{m/s}\).

Two numbers describe any such wave. The wavelength \(\lambda\) is the distance from one crest to the next. The frequency \(f\) is how many crests pass a fixed point each second. Because every crest travels at \(c\), they are locked together — squeeze the crests closer (small \(\lambda\)) and more of them fly past each second (big \(f\)):

$$ c \;=\; \lambda\, f \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f \;=\; \frac{c}{\lambda} $$

Here is the part that matters for the rest of this lesson: the wavelength of light is its color. Long waves (~700 nm) look red; short waves (~450 nm) look blue-violet. A nanometre (nm) is a billionth of a metre, so visible light — roughly 380 to 750 nm — has crests only a few hundred atoms apart. Wavelengths just longer than red we call infrared (you feel it as warmth); just shorter than violet is ultraviolet (it sunburns you). Same wave, different crest spacing.

Green light has \(\lambda = 550\ \text{nm} = 550\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}\). Its frequency is \(f = c/\lambda = (3.0\times10^{8})/(550\times10^{-9}) = 5.5\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\) — 550 trillion crests every second. That is why we never "see" the flicker: the wave oscillates hundreds of trillions of times faster than your eye could ever follow.

  • Light is an EM wave: an electric field and a magnetic field oscillating at right angles, racing forward at \(c\).
  • Wavelength \(\lambda\) = crest-to-crest distance; frequency \(f\) = crests per second; they obey \(c=\lambda f\).
  • Color is wavelength: ~450 nm blue, ~550 nm green, ~700 nm red.
  • Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → (as Tab 2 shows) more energy per photon.
  1. Drag Wavelength from 380 to 750 nm. Watch the crests spread apart and the color swatch march from violet through green to red.
  2. Read the frequency stat as you go — shorter waves have higher frequency. Confirm \(c=\lambda f\) stays fixed (their product is always \(3\times10^8\)).
  3. Push to the far ends. Below ~400 nm the swatch goes dark (that light is turning into UV, past what your eye detects); above ~700 nm it fades toward IR.

Background: an atom is a fussy customer

Now aim that wave at a single atom. An atom's electrons cannot sit at just any energy — they are only allowed to occupy specific energy levels, like rungs on a ladder with no floor in between. To jump an electron from a low rung to a higher one, you must deliver exactly the energy of the gap between those rungs. Too little and nothing happens; too much and it still doesn't fit the rung. It has to match.

Light delivers its energy in indivisible packets called photons. The energy of one photon is set entirely by its wavelength (equivalently, its frequency):

$$ E_{\text{photon}} \;=\; h f \;=\; \frac{hc}{\lambda} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad E(\text{eV}) \;\approx\; \frac{1240}{\lambda\,(\text{nm})} $$

Here \(h = 6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}\) is Planck's constant, and we measure the energy in electron-volts (eV), the natural currency for atoms. The handy shortcut \(E \approx 1240/\lambda\) lets you convert any color to an energy in your head: a 620 nm orange photon carries \(1240/620 = 2.0\ \text{eV}\). So absorption is a matchmaking problem. A photon is absorbed only when its energy \(hc/\lambda\) equals one of the atom's energy-level gaps — a resonance. Miss the gap and the photon sails straight through as if the atom were not there. This one rule is why materials are picky about which colors they swallow.

Suppose an atom has a gap of \(E_{\text{gap}} = 2.25\ \text{eV}\). Which photon does it absorb? Solve \(1240/\lambda = 2.25\) → \(\lambda = 1240/2.25 \approx 551\ \text{nm}\) — a green photon fits perfectly and is swallowed. A 700 nm red photon carries only \(1240/700 = 1.77\ \text{eV}\) — too little, it passes. A 430 nm violet photon carries \(2.88\ \text{eV}\) — too much for this gap, so it also passes (unless the atom has a second, wider gap that happens to match).

  • Electrons live on fixed energy rungs; a jump needs an exact energy match.
  • Light comes in photons of energy \(E=hc/\lambda\); use \(E(\text{eV})\approx 1240/\lambda(\text{nm})\).
  • A photon is absorbed only at resonance: \(E_{\text{photon}} = E_{\text{gap}}\). Otherwise it passes through.
  • "Enough energy" is not the rule — it must match a gap, not merely exceed it.
  1. Set the atom's Energy gap and then sweep the Photon wavelength. Watch the photon fly in: at most wavelengths it passes straight through the atom.
  2. Hunt for the resonance — the wavelength where the incoming photon is absorbed and the electron leaps to the upper rung. Read "Photon energy" and "Gap" as they lock together.
  3. Click Match the gap: it snaps the photon to \(\lambda = 1240/E_{\text{gap}}\). The verdict turns green — perfect resonance.
  4. Now nudge the wavelength ±20 nm off the match. The photon passes again. Absorption is a sharp, all-or-nothing match, not "close enough."

Background: one atom absorbs a line; a solid absorbs a band

A lone atom absorbs razor-thin slices of color — a couple of exact wavelengths and nothing else. That is why a thin gas glows in sharp lines (think of a neon sign). But a solid is a crowd: trillions of atoms packed into a lattice, each one close enough to feel its neighbors' electrons. When you bring identical energy rungs close together, they cannot all stay at the same energy — they nudge apart into a dense stack of slightly-different rungs. A single sharp line broadens into a band of absorbed wavelengths.

So a real material does not absorb one thin color; it absorbs a whole range. And here is the payoff that this entire lesson has been driving toward: the color you see is whatever is left over — the wavelengths the material did not absorb, reflected back to your eye. Absorb the greens and you reflect reds and blues, which your brain reads as magenta. Absorb the blues and you reflect the warm colors, and the object looks yellow-orange.

Chlorophyll in a leaf absorbs strongly in two bands — deep blue (~430 nm) and red (~660 nm) — to power photosynthesis. It leaves the middle of the spectrum, around 530–560 nm, largely un-absorbed. That surviving green light is reflected to your eye, and that is the only reason leaves look green. The leaf is not green; it is "everything-but-green-absorbed."

  • Packing atoms into a solid splits each sharp energy level into a dense band.
  • So solids absorb a range of wavelengths, not just a few lines.
  • The color you see = the light that was NOT absorbed, reflected back.
  • More atoms / stronger coupling → wider absorbed band → deeper, more saturated reflected color.
  1. Start with Number of atoms at 1: the absorption is a single sharp line. Drag it up toward a full crystal and watch the line fatten into a band.
  2. Slide Absorbed color across the spectrum. The reflected-color swatch always shows the complement of what you removed.
  3. Set the absorbed band to green (~540 nm). The object looks magenta — reds and blues survive. Now set it to blue (~460 nm); the object looks yellow.
  4. Widen the band (more atoms). The reflected color gets darker and more saturated because more of the incoming white light is being swallowed.

Level up: the band gap sets the color of solids

Time for the real machinery. In a crystalline solid the broadened levels merge into two great bands: a filled valence band (where the bonding electrons live) and an empty conduction band above it. Between them lies a forbidden zone with no allowed rungs — the band gap \(E_g\). A photon can be absorbed only if it can kick a valence electron all the way across the gap into the conduction band, which requires

$$ E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \;\ge\; E_g \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \lambda \;\le\; \lambda_c = \frac{hc}{E_g} \approx \frac{1240}{E_g\,(\text{eV})}\ \text{nm} $$

Everything shorter than the cutoff wavelength \(\lambda_c\) (bluer, more energetic) is absorbed; everything longer (redder, weaker) sails through. This single number, \(E_g\), sorts the material world into three families. Insulators have a huge gap (\(E_g \gtrsim 3.1\ \text{eV}\), \(\lambda_c \lesssim 400\ \text{nm}\)): no visible photon can cross, so all visible light passes — they are transparent (diamond, glass, pure salt). Semiconductors have a gap somewhere in the visible or just below, so they absorb part of the spectrum and transmit the rest — that partial bite is exactly why they are colored. Metals have no gap at all: a sea of free electrons absorbs essentially every visible wavelength and promptly re-radiates it, which we perceive as a bright mirror-like reflection (that is a different mechanism from the gap-crossing here — free electrons, not band-to-band jumps).

Three solids, one formula. Silicon, \(E_g = 1.1\ \text{eV}\): \(\lambda_c = 1240/1.1 \approx 1127\ \text{nm}\), out in the infrared — so silicon absorbs the entire visible range and looks dark gray. Cadmium sulfide, \(E_g = 2.42\ \text{eV}\): \(\lambda_c = 1240/2.42 \approx 512\ \text{nm}\) — it absorbs violet, blue and green (shorter than 512 nm) and transmits/reflects yellow, orange and red, giving CdS its famous cadmium-yellow pigment. Diamond, \(E_g = 5.5\ \text{eV}\): \(\lambda_c = 1240/5.5 \approx 225\ \text{nm}\), deep in the UV — no visible photon can cross, so flawless diamond is water-clear.

  • A solid's valence and conduction bands are separated by the band gap \(E_g\).
  • Absorption edge: photons with \(\lambda \le \lambda_c = 1240/E_g\) (nm, eV) are absorbed; longer ones transmit.
  • Big gap → transparent insulator; mid gap → colored semiconductor; no gap → reflective metal.
  • Tuning \(E_g\) slides the absorption edge across the spectrum and changes the material's color.
  1. Sweep Band gap \(E_g\) from 0.5 to 6 eV. Watch the cutoff \(\lambda_c\) and the transmitted-color swatch slide across the spectrum.
  2. Find where \(\lambda_c\) leaves the visible on the long-wavelength side (\(E_g \lesssim 1.6\ \text{eV}\)): the whole visible spectrum is absorbed and the solid looks dark.
  3. Push \(E_g\) above ~3.1 eV: \(\lambda_c\) drops below 400 nm, no visible photon is absorbed, and the material turns transparent — an insulator.
