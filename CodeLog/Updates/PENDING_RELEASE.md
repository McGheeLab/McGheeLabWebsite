# Pending Release — change ledger

> **What this is:** every small change gets ONE entry here (in addition to
> the normal CHANGELOG entry). When there are **5 or more entries**, or
> when Alex says **"push"**, the batch ships as one release:
>
> ```
> python3 scripts/release_push.py          # add --force under 5 entries
> ```
>
> That one command does the whole push from these entries: writes the Ourlab
> mobile changelog entry, bumps the TestFlight build number, bundles the web
> tree into the app, uploads the build, prints the ready-to-paste
> announcement, and moves these entries to **Shipped** below. Two things stay
> manual: the Cyberduck FTP, and pressing 📣 Send on
> `rm/pages/whats-new.html`.
>
> **Why the app is part of it:** Ourlab mobile bundles the `rm/` tree at
> build time, so an FTP-only push updates browser users and silently leaves
> phone users on old code. Every batch ships both halves, and the
> announcement names the build that carries them.
>
> **Entry format:** one line a lab member (not a developer) would
> understand — what they can now do, or what stopped being broken.
> Technical detail belongs in CHANGELOG, not here.

- **The "box inside the box" section now models OUR box, and the answer changed.** Alex pointed out two things I had wrong: our aluminium boxes already have **0.2 µm filters in the lid**, and we don't leave them sitting in the incubator — the box is a **carrier**. Plate goes in it, box comes out, spray the outside, open the lid **in the hood**, do the media change, spray, back in. So the sim now runs that exact cycle, with the same plate carried bare on a rack drawn alongside for comparison. **The box works, and the number is about five degrees.** A hood is not a warm still room — it is 0.32 m/s of air at 22 °C, which roughly doubles what a plate loses, so a bare plate finishes a ten-minute media change at **26 °C**. In the box it finishes at **30 °C**, and if you are quick about it, **34 °C**. Take twenty-five minutes and the aluminium has cooled to about what the plate would have reached anyway — you get the cost without the benefit. **And there is a cost, which is the part nobody would guess.** The box comes back in cold, and the incubator is at 93% humidity with a dew point of 35.7 °C. Water vapour gets through those filters in about four minutes; two and a half kilos of aluminium takes twenty minutes to warm up. **The humidity gets there first**, so for the next hour the inside of your box is wetter than its own surfaces are warm and it **rains on your plate** — about half a gram of water on the lid, which then drips into wells it did not come from. The filters turn out not to be a trade between keeping things out and letting gas in (a 0.2 µm membrane does both, which is why they are there) — they are a trade between **how fast the CO₂ comes back** and **how much water lands on your plate first**. One filter: dry, but the CO₂ takes over an hour. Eight: CO₂ back in nine minutes and a gram of water. The thing that fixes both at once is **being quick with the lid off**, because it is the same degrees Celsius doing both jobs. Two practical rules came out of it: **spray the sides of the box, not the lid vents** — a wet 0.2 µm filter passes no gas at all, and you have just converted your box into a sealed one where the CO₂ never comes back — and **the lid only ever comes off inside the hood**, because that is the entire containment argument.

- **Five ways out of the box's warm-culture-vs-wet-plate trade, all in the sim.** The trade only exists because the box gets cold, so everything is an attack on that. **A warming plate in the hood is the cleanest fix for the condensation, and it has a size:** the box loses about **45 W** sitting in the downflow, so 45 W underneath it is break-even and takes the water from 0.54 g to 0.13 g. **A double-walled box (sealed air gap) is the best fix for the culture** — 34 °C instead of 30 at the end of a media change — but only halves the water, because insulation also slows the re-warming. Together they give 34.8 °C and essentially no water. **Don't go plastic:** it gives up 1.6 °C of the only thing the box is for and removes barely a fifth of the water. **Don't bother anodising:** the radiation that helps it re-warm helps it cool just as much. And a warning the sim will now give you: **a warming plate can be too big.** A jacketed box loses only 13 W, so at 45 or 60 W the *metal* runs to 45–50 °C while the culture still reads 36 — the plate's open top is holding the culture down while the box climbs, and then you shut the culture inside it. Match the plate to what the box actually loses. **The free lever is still the biggest one:** with the lid off, your plate's own exposed top loses about fifteen times more heat than the box can feed back into it, so most of the warmth the box buys is bought while the lid is *shut*. How long the lid is off, and whether the plate's own lid is on between steps, beat any hardware you can buy.

- **"Open the incubator door slowly" turns out to be wrong, and the sim now shows why.** Alex spotted it by watching the animation: the door is exchanging air **the whole time it is opening**, not just once it is open. A 0.9 m door opened at a careful 5 cm/s takes eighteen seconds to travel, and that is worth **eleven and a half seconds of standing there with it wide open** — before you have reached in for anything. So there are two penalties pulling opposite ways: move fast and you push a slug of room air in like a piston; move slow and the box is exchanging the whole time the door is on its way. Put them together and there is a **best speed**, not a limit — and with a normal 8-second reach-in, **creeping at 5 cm/s replaces 62% of the chamber with room air, which is worse than flinging it (51%)**. The bottom of the curve is at about **30 cm/s — roughly three seconds for the door** — and that is not a coincidence: it is exactly the speed the air itself moves, the same Π = 1 the lesson has been using since the cabinet. So the instruction is no longer "gently". It is **match the door to the air, then get it shut** — quick applies to the reach-in, not to the handle. Which also explains something the lesson used to treat as impatience: people who fling doors have correctly noticed that slow is not free, they just had no way to know what the right speed was.

- **The box section now models the design we actually settled on — a drawer that lives in the incubator — and the honest answer is not the one it was bought for.** The plate slides in and out, and anything that needs doing to it happens on a 37 °C warm plate in the hood, so the box never leaves the incubator and never gets cold. The sim now runs the whole chain and, crucially, **draws the same plate with no box alongside as a control** — and the box removes about **5% of the CO₂ dip and about 28% of the temperature dip**. The reason it is so little is worth knowing: **your medium already did it.** 5 mL in a T-25 is 2 mm deep, and it takes CO₂ **18 minutes** to cross that; a 200 µL well is **three hours**. A door opening is over in thirty seconds. The culture was already behind a filter thirty times slower than the disturbance before the box existed. Two more things fell out. **A filter removes ripple, not offset** — turn the openings up to twelve an hour and the chamber never recovers, the culture sits at a permanent depression, and the box takes essentially none of it away; that one is fixed by fewer trips, not a better box. And **the better a box damps, the more it matters when you open it** — the most tightly sealed version holds bad air for twenty-three minutes, so opening the drawer while the incubator door is still open leaves the cells *worse off than having no box at all*. So: **keep the drawer, for the two things it is genuinely excellent at.** It is a **lid** — the contamination that reaches your cultures on this incubator is the part that got in past the HEPA, on the door and off the shelves, and a closed drawer takes that to zero for the plate inside. And it is a **drawer** — you pull your own work out instead of reaching over everybody else's, which is the only thing in the whole lesson that takes a risk to zero rather than making it smaller. The damping is a small bonus on top of a good decision.

- **New section: what the incubator actually costs to run, and what a shelf full of metal does to it.** Alex asked the question nobody had costed — **how much CO₂ comes out of the cylinder every time the door opens** — and it turns out to be the sharpest number in the whole lesson. **About 2.6 litres per opening.** Ten openings a day is 26 L/day and roughly 6,400 L a year, which is a 9 kg cylinder every **six months** — go and look at the one in the corner. And the sensitivity is savage: **four openings a day with a two-second dwell makes that same cylinder last fifty-two months; forty openings with the door held open makes it last three weeks.** A 75× range, and the only thing that changed is how people use the door. It is the same instruction the lesson already gave you for the culture's sake — it just now has a price tag. Two more things came out of it. **Filling the shelves with sealed drawers makes the chamber's CO₂ dips *worse*, not better** — the drawers take up free air, and the same doorway turns over a smaller volume faster, so it falls further on every opening. Only the cultures behind a filter are shielded from that; anything on an open shelf feels it. But **the metal is a twentyfold win on temperature for the entire box**: bare shelves are 0.8 kJ/K and swing 1.3 °C every time the door opens; six aluminium drawers are 16 kJ/K and swing 0.06. And the metal does not know whose culture it is protecting — **a shelf full of drawers steadies the flasks of the person who never made one.** Which gives an awkward but real conclusion: a half-converted incubator is the worst of both, deeper gas dips for everybody and protection only for the people who built a drawer.
