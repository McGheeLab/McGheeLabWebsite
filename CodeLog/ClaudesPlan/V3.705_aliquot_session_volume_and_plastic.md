## The call

Alex, on §3.2 ("Both clocks say more aliquots. So find the bottom."):

> this is missing two big pieces of information
> 1) How much do you actually use i.e. dont split into so many aliquots that
>    you need three tubes for one media change
> 2) what is the cost of using extra tubes

Both are right, and both are invisible in eq. 4.4.1 for the same structural
reason: **that equation is priced entirely in reagent.** Every term is a
fraction of the stock. It therefore cannot see anything denominated in another
currency — your session, your plastic, or your fortnight.

## What was wrong, not just missing

§3.2's own verdict said, of a 500 mL bottle: *"the film term is negligible
against 500 mL, so split it aggressively and stop thinking about it."* With
tubes priced in, 120 aliquots of a $70 bottle is **$42 of plastic against $70
of medium** — 60% — to buy a contamination saving of well under a dollar. The
section was not merely incomplete; its closing advice was wrong for exactly the
class of reagent it was talking about.

## Approach: two new sections, not two more sliders

One new idea per lesson-block is the house contract, and these are two
genuinely different ideas, so §3.2 keeps its model and its widget untouched
(only its over-claiming takeaway, handoff and one verdict string change) and
tab 3 gains **§3.3** and **§3.4**.

### §3.3 — "The tube has to fit the job"  (widget `fit`, eq. 4.4.2)

Introduces `u`, the volume of one session, and the ceiling that follows:

    m = ⌈u/a⌉ tubes per session,    η = u/(m·a)

`η` is the fraction of what you took out that reaches a cell. Plotting it
against `a/u` gives a **comb** that touches 100% only at `a = u, u/2, u/3, …`,
and the teeth are violently asymmetric:

- **just under a session** (a = 0.95u) forces a second tube you barely touch —
  η collapses to **53%**;
- **just over** (a = 1.1u) costs **9%**.

So the rule the section earns is *round up, never down*, which is the opposite
of most people's instinct. Alex's "three tubes for one media change" is the
m ≥ 3 verdict branch, and it says explicitly that eq. 4.4.2 only charges the
volume — the bench also charges 3× the film, 3× the plastic and three chances
to slip inside one sitting.

The checkbox **"strict single use: bin what is left"** is the honest other half.
Unticking it sends η to 100% *on paper* by moving the cost into three terms that
are not on the chart: a second entry (eq. 4.1.1), another session of decay
(eq. 4.3.2), and a freeze–thaw cycle. The only free η = 100% is a tooth.

Two canvases: a picture of one session (the m tubes, the used part in blue, the
leftover in red or amber depending on where it goes) above the comb chart.

### §3.4 — "The tubes cost money, and so does the experiment"  (widget `bill`, eq. 4.4.3–4.4.4)

Puts everything in dollars. Two new terms:

**The plastic.** A tube ladder that is deliberately **not monotonic** — a 2 mL
cryovial ($0.45) costs more than a 15 mL conical ($0.35), because you pay for a
freezer-rated cap, not for polypropylene. Splitting finer can buy more
*expensive* plastic as well as more of it. Condensed into one dimensionless
number, **eq. 4.4.3**:

    ρ = c_tube / (a · P/V)

Medium at the sensible split: ρ = 0.25. Antibody at 20 µL: ρ = 0.013. Two
regimes, twenty-fold apart, and ρ tells you in one number which one you are in.

**C_event — what a contamination costs *you*.** Eq. 4.1.2 priced the blast
radius in millilitres because that is what the bottle holds. The actual bill is
the cells, the plates, the fortnight and the stripped incubator. This is the
slider that changes the answer more than the other three together, and it is
the only one not on a catalogue page.

**The payoff, and the reason this does not undermine the lesson:** at
C_event = $2,000 the money answer for 500 mL of medium is **50 × 10 mL — one
tube, one media change**, which is exactly where §3.2 landed. The money did not
change the answer, it changed the *reason*, and it showed that the
cheap-looking answer (ten 50 mL conicals, $9) is only cheap if a contamination
is free. Drop C_event to zero **and** untick single use and the floor falls out
to k = 10; put either back and it snaps to 50.

**The unifying sentence the tab now earns:** *film limits small precious stocks,
plastic limits big cheap ones, and your session volume limits both.*

## Key decisions

- **N stops being a free parameter.** In `USTOCK`, N = V/u: the number of
  entries over the stock's life *is* the number of sessions. §3.2 keeps its own
  asserted N so its curve is unchanged.
- **Strict single use pins the optimum to a tooth automatically.** Because the
  waste term is in the dollar model, §3.4's argmin lands on a §3.3-legal split
  without any "now round to the nearest sensible number" hand-waving. Verified
  for all four scenarios.
- **`tubesPer` guards the ceiling with 1e-9.** `1/0.2` is `5.000000000000001`,
  and a bare `Math.ceil` calls an exactly-fitting aliquot a six-tube job. Same
  class of bug as the V3.690 `1000^(-1/3)` finding.
- **Prices are sliders and are labelled order-of-magnitude.** Per AUTHORING §6,
  no invented precise figures: the aside says what moves and what does not, and
  the only number nobody can look up — what a fortnight of your work is worth —
  is named as a deliberately conservative stand-in.

## Two defects the harness found in my own narration

1. **§3.4's Try-This step 1 was wrong.** "Drag the contamination cost to zero
   and watch the answer collapse" — it does not, with strict single use ticked.
   The waste term simply takes over from the risk term and the answer stays at
   k = 50. The collapse needs *both* switches, which is a better lesson than the
   one I wrote, and the step now says so (plus a new step 2 that puts strict
   single use back and asks which term caught you).
2. **The "too few tubes" verdict named the wrong mechanism.** It always blamed
   the entry count, but under strict single use every tube is entered exactly
   once. With plastic set free it printed "0% of every tube goes in the sink".
   Three mechanisms can put you left of the bottom — waste, entries, decay — and
   the verdict now picks whichever is actually costing you.
