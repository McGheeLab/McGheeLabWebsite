## Goal

Teach the lab how to **tell a story** in scientific contexts where the
medium is a poster or a presentation, not a manuscript. The central
pedagogical concept: **complexity and scope are inversely correlated
with audience attention** — as you funnel narrower and deeper, you lose
audience; pulling back broad recovers them. Good scientific talks
oscillate between broad and narrow in deliberate cycles. Bad ones
ramp monotonically into depth and lose half the room.

This module is the **spoken/visual counterpart to V3.163 paper writing**
and slots alongside V3.162 PhD triangle in the Foundations category.

## Format

Lecture-style HTML (same pattern as V3.162 + V3.163): single-scroll
page with a sticky TOC, sequential `.training-section` blocks, plus
three meaningful interactive widgets:

1. **The complexity/attention arc** — the centerpiece visualization.
   Seven sliders at evenly-spaced beats along a talk's timeline set
   complexity at each beat. Canvas draws the complexity curve plus a
   derived audience-attention curve via a simple low-pass filter
   (attention decays when complexity is high, recovers when complexity
   drops). A red "lost the audience" band highlights where attention
   falls below threshold. Preset buttons load canonical shapes: bad
   monotonic ramp / bad too-shallow / good oscillating / marathon /
   lightning-talk.
2. **Protagonist picker** — six cards (cells / patient / lab / data /
   instrument / antagonist). Clicking one shows how the same science
   story shifts depending on whose perspective you frame it from.
3. **Capstone storyboard builder** — form with talk format + protagonist
   + opening-hook tactic + classic-arc shape + 3 narrow→broad beats
   + closing message. Renders a printable storyboard sheet a student
   can pin next to their slide-prep workstation.

## Sections (target ~10 with the arc as section 2)

1. Why storytelling — science is communication; the audience attention curve as the core idea.
2. The complexity/attention arc — the interactive centerpiece. Defines "danger zone."
3. The opening hook — the 60 seconds that decide whether the audience invests.
4. The funnel into complexity — how to bring people deeper without losing them.
5. Pulling back broad — the recovery move; harder to do well than the funnel.
6. Choosing your protagonist — interactive picker.
7. Classic story arcs — hero's journey, mystery, tragedy + redemption, discovery, quest.
8. Other hooks beyond the opening — surprise, humor, stakes, beauty, controversy, the unsolved.
9. Format adaptation — elevator pitch, conference talk, seminar, poster.
10. Capstone — storyboard builder for the student's own next talk.

## Audience-attention model

The model is deliberately simple — pedagogical clarity over physical
fidelity. At each beat `t`:

```
attention(t) = attention(t-1)
             + recovery * (1 - attention(t-1))      // exponential recovery toward 1
             - drain * complexity(t)                // drain proportional to current depth
```

with `recovery = 0.15` and `drain = 0.22`, clipped to [0, 1]. Initial
`attention = 1.0`. The red zone is `attention < 0.3`. A monotonic ramp
in complexity will collapse attention into the red zone permanently —
that's the visual lesson.
