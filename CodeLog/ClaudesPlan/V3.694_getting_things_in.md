### §4.1 — Why 70%, and why the wait is the whole thing

The mechanism first, because it is the part that makes the number make sense:
ethanol kills by denaturing proteins and disrupting membranes, and **both need
water**. Two consequences, and the second is the one people have not heard:

1. Protein denaturation *requires* water. Absolute alcohol does not have any.
2. Absolute alcohol dehydrates the outer layer so fast that surface proteins
   coagulate into a **fixed shell** the alcohol then cannot get through — which
   is fixing a specimen, a thing we do on purpose with alcohol.

Two requirements pulling opposite ways is a product of two powers, and a product
of two powers peaks at `C = p/(p+q)`. Putting the peak at 0.70 with `p = 3` fixes
`q = 9/7`:

```
eq. 1.4.2   1/D(C)  ∝  (C/0.70)^3 · ((1-C)/0.30)^(9/7)
```

with a floor of 4% so absolute ethanol is 25× slower rather than inert. The
shape is what matters, not the 70: a broad plateau from ~55% to ~85%, a soft
decline below, a collapse toward 100%.

Then the part that actually decides the outcome:

```
eq. 1.4.1   log10(N0/N) = t_wet / D(C)
```

Time is on top, and it is **wet** time — so the contact you get is
`min(how long you waited, how long it stayed wet)`, which is two separate ways to
lose. Alcohol-rich films dry sooner, so 100% loses on both terms at once.

`D70 = 2.5 s` on steel. The scenario chips carry the argument:

| chip | contact | log off | still alive |
|---|---|---|---|
| spray and wipe — the usual | 5 s | 2.0 | **1,000** |
| spray, then actually wait | 18 s (dried first) | 7.2 | **< 1** |
| 100%, surely stronger | 13 s, D = 62.5 s | 0.2 | **61,015** |
| a light mist | 4 s | 1.6 | 2,512 |

One honest wrinkle, named on the chart and in the prose: past about a 20 s wait
the *achieved* reduction peaks slightly below 70%, because a dilute film stays
wet longer. A green marker shows where the **rate** peaks, which is what 70% is
chosen for.

Asides that keep it from being over-claimed: ethanol is **not sporicidal** at any
concentration; it is inactivated by soil, so clean-then-disinfect. Plus
flammability and no-open-flame-in-a-cabinet.

### The packaging rule was wrong, and Alex caught it

> "Are we sure, I think many sterilogical pipette wrappers are meant to be opened
> in the hood. these things are built for the BSC"

He is right. The first draft of loading rule 5 read *"unwrap outside — porous
packaging cannot be surface-disinfected"*, which is wrong for the single most
common consumable in the room and, worse, **contradicted Lesson 3 §3.3**, where
the peel technique is already taught happening *in* the cabinet: peel from the
plunger end, seat the controller with the pipette still mostly in its wrapper,
slide the wrapper off, wrapper straight to the discard.

The error was conflating two things that both get called packaging:

- A **paper-backed peel pouch is a sterile barrier**, and its paper is porous
  *on purpose* — porous is how the sterilant got in when it was made. You do not
  disinfect it; spraying one can wick liquid through and defeat it. You **peel**
  it, and you peel it at the cabinet, so what comes out is presented into
  filtered air. Opening it at the bench exposes the pipette to room air on the
  way in, which is the thing you were trying to avoid.
- **Corrugate and cardboard** are shipping, not a barrier. Whatever is inside has
  its own packaging. Break the box down outside and bring in the inner packs.

Rule 5 is now *"Bring the barrier in; leave the shipping out"*, and a new insight
box states the principle the two cases share: **the question is never whether a
thing is wrapped — it is where the sterile barrier gets broken and what is
exposed at the moment it does.** Same rule, opposite answers, and the deciding
fact is what is underneath the layer you are about to open. The wrapper still
never *stays*: a wrapper on the work surface is clutter with a wake (§3.2), and a
wrapper on the intake grille is §3.1's absorbent pad under a different name.

### §4.2 — Bringing it in without bringing the room in

The §2.1 section again, with a bottle carried through the opening and set down.
**Nothing new is introduced**: the decontamination is §4.1's model at 70%, the
curtain is §3.3's Π at one crossing instead of twelve, the placement rules are
§3.1's grilles and §3.2's work-zone limit. The right-hand panel is a four-step
checklist, each step labelled with the section that measured it.

Survivors ride visibly on the bottle; a fast carry drags a visible amber slug
through the opening; a dashed ghost marks where it is going to land so the
placement slider is legible even mid-flight.

The placement is judged **only once the bottle has been placed** — Alex:
*"the red outline should not become red until after it was positioned
incorrectly."* He is right, and the reason is worth writing down: marking it red
in transit makes the red mean *this slider is wrong*, which is a criticism of a
setting. Marking it red on arrival makes it mean *you just put it there*, which
is a criticism of an action, and that is the only one a student can learn from.
`flagged = placed && badPlace`, verified by tracing the animation phase against
the flag: `-` on every frame from ph 0.04 to 1.00, `F` from 1.05.

The prose closes with the **loading routine in the order it happens** — eight
rules, from "run it until the airflow indicator goes green" (the manual's own
criterion) to "let it settle before you open the first vessel" — with the point
that the order is what stops you undoing your own work.
