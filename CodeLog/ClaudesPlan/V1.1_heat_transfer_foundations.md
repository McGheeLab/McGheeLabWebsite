# V1.1 — Heat Transfer Foundations (6-lesson interactive course)

## Goal

A six-lesson interactive course under `modules/heat_transfer/`, following the
`modules/AUTHORING.md` spec and matching `modules/solid_mechanics/01-atoms-as-springs.html`
exactly in structure, CSS, and JS helper library. Audience arc per AUTHORING.md §1:
open high-school, escalate one idea per tab, final tab of every lesson is
sophomore-undergraduate level with the real governing equation and a worked example.

## Approach

Six self-contained HTML files, no build step, MathJax CDN only. Each lesson has
4 tabs, every tab has a live simulation. House CSS + shared JS helper library are
copied **verbatim** from the solid-mechanics reference. New this course: an
animation pattern (the reference course has none) for tabs where motion IS the
concept (jiggling atoms, diffusing energy, moving air, photons).

### Animation pattern (new, house-standard for this course)

```js
let animId = null, animT = 0;
const ANIMATED = { tabkey: true, ... };          // tabs that animate
function tick(){ animT += 1/60; refreshActiveTab(); animId = requestAnimationFrame(tick); }
function startAnim(){ if (!animId && ANIMATED[activeTab]) animId = requestAnimationFrame(tick); }
function stopAnim(){ if (animId){ cancelAnimationFrame(animId); animId = null; } }
```

- Tab click handler calls `stopAnim()` before switching, `startAnim()` after.
- Boot calls `startAnim()` after the first draw.
- `document.visibilitychange` → stop/start (don't burn CPU in background tabs).
- Atom jitter uses `mulberry32`-seeded phases (stable across redraws), motion
  driven by `animT` (deterministic, no `Math.random()` per frame).
- Stateful sims (energy-diffusion lattice) keep a state object; any slider or
  scenario change re-initializes it.

## Lesson specs

### Lesson 1 — `01-heat-is-jiggling-atoms.html` — "Heat Is Jiggling Atoms"

Story: heat is not a substance; it is the random vibration of atoms. Energy
passes neighbor-to-neighbor and **takes time** to spread. Different atoms/bonds
hold energy tightly or hand it off easily. Conduction/convection/radiation are
three ways the jiggle gets around.

- **Tab 1 `jiggle` — "Temperature is jiggle"** (ANIMATED)
  2D lattice (~12×6 atoms) vibrating around lattice sites; amplitude ∝ √T.
  Sliders: temperature (0–1000 K), bond stiffness (0.5–2×, stiffer = smaller
  amplitude & faster wiggle: A ∝ √(T/k), ω ∝ √k). Canvas 2: plot of vibration
  amplitude vs T (√ curve) with live dot. Scenarios: Near absolute zero (10 K),
  Room temp (300 K), Red hot (900 K). Verdict narrates regime (frozen stillness /
  everyday jiggle / near melting). Equation box: qualitative T ↔ average kinetic
  energy of vibration. Worked example: amplitude estimate x ≈ √(3k_BT/k_bond):
  k_bond = 20 N/m, T = 300 K → x = √(3·1.38e−23·300/20) ≈ 2.5e−11 m ≈ 0.025 nm,
  ~10% of an atomic spacing (ties back to Solid Mechanics Lesson 1).

- **Tab 2 `spread` — "Passing the jiggle takes time"** (ANIMATED, stateful)
  1D bar of ~40 atom columns; left end held hot. Per-frame diffusion step
  e[i] += D·(mean(neighbors) − e[i]); atoms colored blue→red by local energy,
  jitter amplitude follows local energy. Sliders: handoff speed D (how easily an
  atom gives energy to its neighbor — this IS conductivity at atom scale),
  hot-end temperature. Canvas 2: temperature profile along the bar, redrawn live
  (a diffusing front). Scenarios: Copper-like (D high), Wood-like (D low),
  Restart pulse. Key teaching: some atoms/bonds hand energy over easily (good
  conductors), others hold onto it (insulators); the front creeps — heat
  transport is a random walk, not a wave. Stats: time elapsed, position where
  bar reaches half the hot-end energy.

- **Tab 3 `threeways` — "Three ways the jiggle travels"** (ANIMATED)
  One canvas, three horizontal lanes, same hot wall on the left, cold wall right:
  (a) CONDUCTION: fixed lattice passing jiggle neighbor-to-neighbor (colors creep);
  (b) CONVECTION: free particles picking up jiggle at the hot wall and physically
  carrying it across (drift + return loop);
  (c) RADIATION: wavy photon arrows crossing instantly, warming the far wall even
  with nothing in between.
  Slider: hot-wall temperature. Scenario buttons: "Solid between" (only lane a
  works), "Air between" (lanes a weak + b + c), "Vacuum between" (only c works —
  the thermos-bottle argument). Verdict explains which mechanisms survive and why.
  Canvas 2 optional: relative energy delivered per lane (bar chart via Chart).

- **Tab 4 `levelup` — "Level up: equipartition & the diffusion clock"**
  Real machinery: average thermal energy per atom in a solid ≈ 3k_BT
  (equipartition → Dulong–Petit foreshadow); thermal diffusivity α = k/(ρc);
  diffusion time τ ≈ L²/α — WHY heat is slow at human scales despite atomic
  handoffs being ~picoseconds (random walk: distance ∝ √t).
  Sim: log-log plot of τ = L²/α vs L for a chosen material; sliders: length
  scale L (1 mm–1 m, log), material scenario setting α (copper 1.16e−4,
  glass ~3.4e−7, wood ~1.9e−7, air ~2.2e−5 m²/s). Stats: τ in sensible units.
  Worked example: 1 cm slab — copper τ = (0.01)²/1.16e−4 ≈ 0.9 s; wood
  (0.01)²/1.9e−7 ≈ 530 s ≈ 9 min. Same geometry, ~600× slower.

Sources: Kittel *Intro to Solid State Physics* 8e (lattice vibrations, equipartition);
Incropera/DeWitt/Bergman/Lavine *Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer* 7e (α values, mechanisms);
Halliday/Resnick/Walker *Fundamentals of Physics* 10e (kinetic theory);
Çengel & Ghajar *Heat and Mass Transfer* 5e.

### Lesson 2 — `02-specific-heat.html` — "Specific Heat — Holding the Jiggle"

- **Tab 1 `hold` — "Some materials hold more jiggle"** (ANIMATED)
  Two blocks side by side (choose pair by scenario: water vs copper; water vs
  sand; copper vs aluminum) receiving the SAME power from identical burners;
  thermometer bars rise at different rates (dT/dt = P/(mc)). Slider: burner
  power, elapsed-time scrubber or free-run animation. The intuition: c is how
  much energy one kilogram soaks up per degree — water is a heat sponge.

- **Tab 2 `qmcdt` — Q = mcΔT**
  Equation box Q = mcΔT, every symbol defined. Sliders: m (0.1–5 kg),
  c (100–4200 J/kg·K via material scenarios + free slider), ΔT (1–80 K).
  Canvas 1: block scaled to m with thermometer; Canvas 2: Q vs ΔT lines for
  3 reference materials + live dot. Worked example: pasta water — 1 kg water
  20→100 °C: Q = 1·4186·80 ≈ 335 kJ; at 2 kW that's ≈167 s ≈ 2.8 min.

- **Tab 3 `rate` — "Heating takes time: power is the slope"**
  T(t) = T₀ + P·t/(mc). Sliders: P, m, material c. Canvas: T vs t line, live
  dot; second canvas: bar showing time-to-target. Scenarios: kettle (2 kW, 1 kg
  water), pot of soup, copper pan. Verdict flags "same P, bigger mc → shallower
  slope". Foreshadow: real objects also LOSE heat (Lesson 5).

- **Tab 4 `levelup` — "Level up: calorimetry & Dulong–Petit"**
  Mixing problem: m₁c₁(T_f−T₁) = −m₂c₂(T_f−T₂) →
  T_f = (m₁c₁T₁ + m₂c₂T₂)/(m₁c₁ + m₂c₂). Sim: hot block dropped into water;
  sliders m₁, T₁, material; m₂, T₂; plot both temperatures converging to T_f
  vs time (exponential approach, time constant arbitrary); stats T_f.
  Worked example: 100 g copper at 100 °C into 200 g water at 20 °C →
  T_f = (0.1·385·100 + 0.2·4186·20)/(0.1·385 + 0.2·4186) ≈ 23.5 °C — the water
  barely notices. Then the atomic punchline: per MOLE, most solids store the
  same: C_molar ≈ 3R ≈ 24.9 J/mol·K (Dulong–Petit; each atom stores ≈3k_BT).
  Copper check: 24.9/0.0635 kg/mol ≈ 392 vs measured 385 J/kg·K.

Sources: Çengel & Ghajar 5e; Halliday/Resnick/Walker 10e (calorimetry);
Kittel 8e (Dulong–Petit); CRC Handbook (c values).

### Lesson 3 — `03-crossing-boundaries.html` — "Crossing the Boundary"

Story: put a wall between hot and cold; how fast does heat cross? Steady-state
conduction through a slab: material (k), thickness (L), area (A).

- **Tab 1 `slab` — "A wall between hot and cold"** (can be static)
  Slab with color gradient; sliders T_hot, T_cold; canvas 2: linear steady-state
  temperature profile. Concept: in steady state the profile is a straight ramp;
  heat flows downhill in temperature at a steady rate.

- **Tab 2 `material` — "Material matters: conductivity k"**
  Same slab; log slider k (0.02–400 W/m·K) + material scenarios (copper 401,
  glass 1.0, wood 0.15, still air 0.026). q = kAΔT/L live. Canvas 2: q vs k
  (log-x). Atomic tie-back to Lesson 1: k IS the handoff speed D at human scale.

- **Tab 3 `geometry` — "Thickness and area"**
  Sliders L (2 mm–30 cm), A (0.1–4 m²). Canvas: slab redrawn to scale; canvas 2:
  q vs L hyperbola with live dot. Scenarios: window pane (thin, big A), house
  wall (thick), pot bottom (thin, small). Halve the thickness → double the flow.

- **Tab 4 `levelup` — "Level up: Fourier's law & thermal resistance"**
  q″ = −k dT/dx; resistance form R = L/(kA), q = ΔT/R — Ohm's-law analogy table
  (ΔT↔V, q↔I, R↔R). Sim: slab + resistor-symbol circuit view; sliders k, L, A,
  ΔT; stats R and q. Worked example (honest!): single-pane window, k = 1.0,
  A = 1 m², L = 4 mm, ΔT = 20 K → bare-glass q = 5000 W?! Resolve the paradox:
  the glass itself is only R = 0.004 m²K/W; thin still-air films on each surface
  add ≈0.17 m²K/W, so real q ≈ 20/0.174 ≈ 115 W. The AIR, not the glass, is the
  insulation — perfect setup for Lessons 4–5.

Sources: Incropera 7e (Fourier's law, R-networks, film coefficients);
Çengel & Ghajar 5e (window example, k table); CRC Handbook (k values).

### Lesson 4 — `04-layers-air-gaps.html` — "Layers, Air Gaps & Insulation"

- **Tab 1 `series` — "Boundaries inside boundaries"**
  Two-layer wall; sliders k₁, L₁, k₂, L₂ (+ material scenarios brick+foam,
  glass+glass, copper+steel). Canvas: composite wall with the temperature
  STAIRCASE profile — each layer's ΔT share ∝ its resistance; the worst layer
  dominates. Stats: interface temperature, q.

- **Tab 2 `air` — "Air: terrible conductor, great insulator"** (ANIMATED)
  k_air = 0.026 W/m·K — 15× better than wood, 15,000× better than copper. But
  ONLY if it stands still: in a wide gap, warm air rises → convection loop
  ferries heat across. Sim: wall with air gap; slider gap width (2–50 mm);
  animated circulation loop appears and effective conductance rises once the gap
  exceeds ~15 mm (qualitative Rayleigh threshold). Scenarios: thin trapped gap /
  wide cavity / gap filled with foam. Verdict: best real gap ≈ 10–20 mm.

- **Tab 3 `voids` — "Millions of tiny boundaries: foams and fluff"**
  Porous insulation = solid skeleton + trapped air pockets, each pocket too
  small for convection, each solid-air interface another obstacle. Sim: 2D
  cross-section, slider porosity (0–95%) and pore size; effective k from a
  simple series/parallel blend between k_solid and k_air (state the model
  honestly as a bound/estimate). Scenarios: fiberglass batt, EPS foam, aerogel.
  Why a down jacket, double-glazing, and aerogel are all the same trick.

- **Tab 4 `levelup` — "Level up: resistance networks & R-value"**
  R_tot = Σ Lᵢ/(kᵢA) (+ film resistances); U = 1/R_tot·A concept; note US
  R-value units (ft²·°F·hr/BTU, R-13 ≈ RSI 2.3). Sim: build-a-wall — 3 layer
  slots, each with material scenario + thickness slider; stats per-layer R,
  R_tot, q, interface temps; canvas: wall + staircase + resistor chain. Worked
  example: single pane (from L3, q ≈ 115 W) vs double pane: 2×4 mm glass
  (0.008) + 12 mm still air (0.012/0.026 ≈ 0.46) + films 0.17 → R ≈ 0.64 m²K/W,
  q ≈ 31 W — a 4× improvement from a sliver of trapped air.

Sources: Incropera 7e (composite walls, contact resistance, enclosure
convection); Çengel & Ghajar 5e (R-value, U-factor, window tables);
ASHRAE Handbook—Fundamentals (air-gap and film resistances).

### Lesson 5 — `05-fins-and-airflow.html` — "Fins & Flowing Air"

- **Tab 1 `film` — "Air steals heat from surfaces"** (ANIMATED)
  Hot plate; air particles wander in, pick up jiggle, wander off. The thin
  slow-moving air layer hugging the surface (boundary layer) is the bottleneck.
  Newton's law of cooling q = hA(T_s − T_∞), h = how good the air is at hauling
  heat away. Sliders: T_s, h (2–100). Canvas 2: q vs (T_s−T_∞) line, slope = hA.

- **Tab 2 `blow` — "Blow on it: forced convection"** (ANIMATED)
  Slider wind speed v (0–15 m/s); h from McAdams' flat-surface wind correlation
  h ≈ 5.7 + 3.8v W/m²K (state its empirical origin); animated particles speed
  up, boundary layer thins. Canvas 2: h vs v + cooling curve T(t) of a small
  hot object: exponential with τ = mc/(hA) — blowing on soup halves the wait.
  Scenarios: still room / breeze / leaf blower.

- **Tab 3 `fins` — "More edge: why heatsinks are spiky"**
  q = hA_total(T_s−T_∞): can't raise h? Raise A. Sliders: fin count N (1–30),
  fin length (5–60 mm); canvas draws the heatsink cross-section; canvas 2:
  q vs N showing near-linear growth then a gentle crowding penalty (fins too
  close choke the airflow — apply a spacing-based derating and SAY it's
  qualitative). Scenarios: bare plate / CPU cooler / crammed fins.

- **Tab 4 `levelup` — "Level up: fin efficiency & Biot number"**
  A fin isn't uniformly hot — the tip runs cooler. Fin parameter m = √(hP/(kA_c)),
  efficiency η = tanh(mL)/(mL); temperature profile θ/θ_b = cosh(m(L−x))/cosh(mL).
  Biot number Bi = hL_c/k (when is a body "all one temperature"?). Sim: sliders
  h, k (aluminum/copper/steel scenarios), L, thickness t; canvas 1: fin colored
  by local T with profile plot; stats m, mL, η, q_fin vs ideal. Worked example:
  aluminum fin k = 205, t = 2 mm, L = 30 mm, h = 25 (per metre width:
  P ≈ 2, A_c = 0.002) → m = √(25·2/(205·0.002)) ≈ 11.0 m⁻¹, mL = 0.33,
  η = tanh(0.33)/0.33 ≈ 0.97 — a short fat aluminum fin is nearly perfect;
  try steel (k = 45): m ≈ 23.6, mL = 0.71, η ≈ 0.86 and falling.

Sources: Incropera 7e (fins ch. 3, convection ch. 6–7, Biot ch. 5);
Çengel & Ghajar 5e; W.H. McAdams *Heat Transmission* 3e (1954) (wind h correlation).

### Lesson 6 — `06-radiation-and-color.html` — "Radiation & Color"

- **Tab 1 `photons` — "Light is energy delivery"** (ANIMATED)
  Photon arrows stream onto a surface; fraction α absorbed (atoms jiggle harder,
  surface warms), fraction 1−α reflected. Slider: absorptivity α (0.05–0.98),
  light intensity. Atomic lens: an absorbed photon's energy becomes vibration —
  radiation is jiggle that traveled as a wave and became jiggle again.

- **Tab 2 `color` — "Why the black car is an oven"** (ANIMATED or live-solve)
  Equilibrium: absorbed αG balances convective + radiative losses:
  αG = h(T−T∞) + εσ(T⁴−T∞⁴), solved iteratively (bisection each redraw).
  Sliders: sun intensity G (200–1100 W/m²), wind h (5–25). Scenarios set (α, ε):
  black paint (0.95, 0.90), white paint (0.25, 0.90), bare aluminum (0.15, 0.05).
  Canvas: three panels with live equilibrium temps; canvas 2: energy balance
  bars. Worked example: G = 1000, h = 10, T∞ = 30 °C: black ≈ 84 °C,
  white ≈ 46 °C — and note white paint still EMITS well in IR (α_solar ≠ ε_IR —
  the selective-surface idea, formalized in Tab 4).

- **Tab 3 `glow` — "Everything glows"** 
  Every object emits; hotter → far more, and bluer. Planck spectrum vs T
  (300–6000 K slider, log-y), visible band shaded; Wien λ_peak = 2898 μm·K/T.
  Scenarios: you (310 K → 9.3 μm, deep IR), toaster wire (1100 K, dull red),
  sun (5778 K, peak 0.50 μm, mid-visible). Stats: λ_peak, total emission ∝T⁴
  teaser. Why thermal cameras see in the dark.

- **Tab 4 `levelup` — "Level up: Stefan–Boltzmann & Kirchhoff"**
  q = εσA(T⁴ − T_surr⁴), σ = 5.670×10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴; Kirchhoff's law α_λ = ε_λ
  (good absorbers are good emitters, wavelength by wavelength — resolves the
  white-paint puzzle from Tab 2); linearized radiation coefficient
  h_r = 4εσT̄³ to compare with convection. Sim: sliders ε, T_s, T_surr; canvas:
  q_rad vs T (the T⁴ sweep) with q_conv line for comparison; stats h_r.
  Worked examples: (1) person in a room: ε ≈ 0.97, A ≈ 1.8 m², skin 306 K,
  walls 293 K → q ≈ 0.97·5.67e−8·1.8·(306⁴−293⁴) ≈ 140 W — you radiate like a
  bright lightbulb; (2) Earth's equilibrium: T = [S(1−a)/4σ]^{1/4} =
  [1361·0.7/(4·5.67e−8)]^{1/4} ≈ 255 K.

Sources: Incropera 7e (radiation ch. 12); Çengel & Ghajar 5e; Kittel 8e or
Halliday/Resnick/Walker 10e (Planck, Wien, Stefan–Boltzmann); NASA Earth fact
sheet / IPCC AR6 for S = 1361 W/m², albedo 0.30.

## Key decisions

- **Copy, don't reinvent**: CSS + helper library verbatim from
  `solid_mechanics/01-atoms-as-springs.html`; same ID conventions
  (`s-/v-/st-/vd-/cv-`), same FMT/SCEN/DRAW wiring.
- **Animation is opt-in per tab** via the ANIMATED map + start/stopAnim —
  motion only where motion is the concept (AUTHORING.md §4).
- **Synthetic closed-form models** everywhere; qualitative penalties (fin
  crowding, air-gap convection onset) are labeled as qualitative in the prose.
- **All worked-example numbers verified by hand** in this plan.
- **Cross-lesson continuity**: L1 jiggle → L2 stores jiggle → L3 jiggle crosses
  a wall → L4 many walls → L5 walls with moving air → L6 jiggle without a
  medium. Footer teasers chain the lessons; L1 ties back to Solid Mechanics L1.
