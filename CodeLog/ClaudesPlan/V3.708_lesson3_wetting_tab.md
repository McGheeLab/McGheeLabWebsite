### 2.1 New §2.1 — "Why liquid sticks to plastic at all" (widget `wet`)

The fundamental the rest of the tab rests on. A drop on a solid either spreads or beads,
and which one is a three-way balance of surface energies at the contact line:

- **eq. 3.4.1** Young — `γ_sv = γ_sl + γ_lv cos θ`
- **eq. 3.4.2** Young–Dupré — `W_adh = γ_lv(1 + cos θ)`, the energy per unit area it
  costs to pull the liquid off. This is the number that decides every later question:
  which surface wins a drop, how much stays on a tip, why a wall can take it away.

`θ` is computed from the solid's surface energy by Good–Girifalco,
`cos θ = 2Φ√(γ_s/γ_l) − 1`, with `Φ(γ_s) = 0.66 + 0.006(γ_s − 18)` — an **empirical fit
to tabulated contact angles**, stated as such in the prose, not a first-principles
constant. It reproduces the real numbers across the whole range that matters:

| surface | γ_s (mN/m) | θ model | θ published |
|---|---|---|---|
| PTFE | 18 | 110° | 108–112° |
| polypropylene (a tip) | 30 | 93° | 95–102° |
| polystyrene, untreated | 34 | 88° | 87–91° |
| TC-treated PS (a flask) | 57 | 54° | 55–60° |
| lab-clean glass | 70 | 25° | 25–40° |

**The lab-specific payoff:** serum proteins adsorb onto polypropylene and raise its
effective surface energy from ~30 to ~45 mN/m, taking θ from 93° to ~72°. A tip that has
held serum-containing medium is a *different surface* from a fresh one — which is why
every wetting error in this lesson is worse for medium than for PBS.

### 2.3 New §2.3 — "Going in: how deep, and at what angle" (widget `depth`)

The same eq. 3.2.1/3.2.2, now applied to the **outside** of the tip. A tip is a cone, so
the wetted outer area grows **faster than linearly** with immersion depth:

`A(s) = π(d₀s + ks²)` for `d₀` = 0.8 mm and taper `k` = 0.075 mm radius per mm.

| immersion | wetted area | film | carried out |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 mm | 5.97 mm² | 4.6 µm | 0.027 µL |
| 15 mm | 90.7 mm² | 9.3 µm | 0.84 µL |

Thirty-one times, for burying the tip instead of dipping it — and 0.84 µL on a 100 µL
delivery is the 0.9 µL the budget's depth term has been asserting all along.

**Angle is handled here, honestly, and the honest answer is a surprise.** The obvious
mechanism — tilt changes the vertical height of the liquid column, so the hydrostatic
head on the cushion changes, so eq. 3.1.2's *pressure* half moves the volume — computes
to `ΔV = 0.03 µL` at 30°, about **twenty times too small** to be the ~0.5–0.7% the
manufacturers measure. So the section computes it, finds it wanting, and identifies the
real mechanism: a tilted tip is immersed along a longer path, `s = h/cos φ`, and the
wetted area grows superlinearly in `s`. **"Hold it vertical" is not a pressure rule, it
is an immersion-depth rule in disguise.**

### 2.4 New §2.4 — "Coming out: the drop that stays" (widget `touch`)

- **eq. 3.4.3** Tate — `V_drop = 2πaγ/ρg` — promoted from the inline mention in §1.2,
  since a whole section now rests on it.

The residual after a dispense is modelled as a cap pinned at the orifice rim **plus** the
static meniscus creeping up the outer wall, `h_men = ℓ_c√(2(1 − sin θ))`:

| tip surface | θ | bead | creep | total residual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fresh polypropylene | 93° | 0.145 µL | 0.014 µL | **0.16 µL** |
| PP after serum | 72° | 0.086 µL | 0.484 µL | **0.57 µL** |
| a wetting surface | 30° | 0.028 µL | 4.94 µL | **4.97 µL** |

The bead *shrinks* as the surface gets more wettable and the creep *explodes*, so the
total goes the way intuition says even though its larger half goes the other way. That
crossover is the section's real content.

**Why touching off works** is then eq. 3.4.2 doing arithmetic: a TC-treated flask wall
is `W = 115 mJ/m²` against polypropylene's `68`, and dragging along the wall multiplies
the contact area as well. Transfer fraction `η = 1 − exp(−(W_wall/W_tip)(drag/2 mm))`.

### 2.5 §2.5 — reverse pipetting (was §2.3), gains the last missing fundamental

**"Wait one second" gets its equation.** The tip plus cushion is an RC circuit: Poiseuille
resistance through the bore against the compressibility of the trapped air.

- **eq. 3.2.3** — `τ_flow = R·C = 8μL_bore·V_cushion/(π r⁴ P₀)`

τ = 4 ms for PBS in a 200 µL tip, but **0.34 s for Matrigel** — three time constants is a
second, which is exactly where the rule comes from and why it is about viscous liquids
rather than about all pipetting.
